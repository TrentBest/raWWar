> Air formation geometry, flight coordination, mission packages, and safe simulation.

An air formation manual from early aviation to modern and speculative aerospace operations, emphasizing three-dimensional geometry, energy, weather, and human factors.

This raWWar-owned LOD 2 manual is for historical study, roleplaying, and simulation design. It distinguishes evidence from interpretation and fictional adaptation. It is not a real-world operational training manual. Shared physical behavior belongs to Physics; embodied movement belongs to Gestures; this book defines warfare-domain meaning, context, and evaluation.

## 1. Scope

Air formations coordinate aircraft or aerial systems through three-dimensional space. Unlike a ground unit, an aircraft continuously manages energy, lift, speed, altitude, attitude, fuel, weather, and separation. Formation is a high-workload cooperative activity, not merely a set of dots on a map.

## 2. Formation dimensions

Represent relative bearing, range, altitude, vertical separation, closure rate, speed, heading, bank, reference aircraft, and role. A 2D map is insufficient for safe understanding. Every displayed position needs a reference frame, timestamp, uncertainty, and scale.

## 3. Historical development

Study early visual formations, bomber streams, fighter escort, patrol patterns, carrier aviation, helicopter formations, air mobility packages, and contemporary distributed operations in their own contexts. Aircraft performance, radio, radar, navigation, weapons, airspace rules, and mission changed what was practical.

## 4. Roles and mission packages

Formation elements may provide command, escort, sensing, transport, refueling, rescue, electronic support, or other mission functions. Role assignment is not proof of capability: availability depends on aircraft condition, crew qualification, payload, weather, fuel, and communications.

## 5. Three-dimensional separation

Spacing must consider closure, wake turbulence, maneuver, visibility, altitude, and emergency escape options. Training representations should communicate envelopes and uncertainty rather than imply a single exact line is safe for every aircraft. Avoid translating real flight procedures into casual game instructions.

## 6. Weather and environment

Cloud, icing, turbulence, wind shear, precipitation, visibility, temperature, density altitude, terrain, and daylight affect performance and formation integrity. Weather is spatially and temporally variable; the formation may encounter different conditions across its footprint.

## 7. Human factors

Workload, fatigue, motion sickness, sensory conflict, stress, spatial disorientation, language, and cockpit design affect performance. A formation leader cannot assume every participant sees the same picture or hears the same transmission.

## 8. Command and communications

Radio calls, data links, visual signals, pre-briefed plans, and air traffic coordination have different reliability and authority. Track who issued, received, acknowledged, and executed an instruction. Latency or lost link should trigger safe, predeclared behavior.

## 9. Training and ghost flight

Ghost aircraft should show target station, orientation, altitude band, speed trend, and transition path. A player may practice formation joining, station maintenance, departure, rejoin, and orderly break in a controlled fictional environment. Gestures can guide hand placement and cockpit interactions; haptics should be sparse, interpretable, and subordinate to visual and audio warnings.

## 10. Evaluation

Score separation envelope, station stability, closure management, heading/altitude agreement, communication discipline, workload management, fuel awareness, and safe response to disruption. Report each component separately and show the time series so learners can understand drift and correction.

## 11. Safety and accessibility

Never present the game as flight training or a substitute for certified instruction. Provide seated and non-motion alternatives, adjustable cue intensity, remappable controls, visual equivalents for haptics, and clear sensor-confidence states. A failed tracker must not be scored as a deliberate maneuver.

## 12. Uncrewed and autonomous formations

Uncrewed aircraft introduce questions of authority, identification, collision avoidance, delegated action, communications loss, and accountability. Distinguish autonomous navigation from autonomous use of force. Human authorization, abort paths, and audit trails remain explicit design concerns.

## 13. Aerospace beyond the atmosphere

Spacecraft formations require orbital mechanics, relative motion, propellant, thermal limits, radiation, communication delay, debris, and collision avoidance. They are not aircraft formations with a different backdrop. Distinguish atmospheric flight, suborbital trajectories, orbital rendezvous, and deep-space coordination.

## 14. Simulation contract

Each participant needs a vehicle model, performance envelope, role, crew/automation authority, sensor state, communications state, and assigned reference. Formation definitions specify relative geometry, allowed transitions, tolerance bands, and emergency dissolution behavior. Physics owns flight dynamics; raWWar owns doctrine and mission context.

## 15. Scenarios

Include a historical visual-formation drill, a weather-degraded transport formation, a rescue package with a damaged aircraft, a mixed human/autonomous patrol, and a speculative orbital inspection group. Make the evidence label and safety boundary visible.

## Cross-domain ownership and implementation boundary

These books are descriptive domain knowledge. They do not introduce executable APIs, duplicate a physics engine, or claim that a HUD, haptic rig, or simulation feature already exists. A host may opt into SURPS composition; without it, the raWWar Experience remains valid. Any game-specific doctrine, scoring rubric, or fictional equipment must be versioned and identified as such.
