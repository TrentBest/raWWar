# Planetary Assault and Expeditionary Doctrine

![SURPS Rulebook LOD 2](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/TrentBest/TheSingularityWorkshop.SingularityUniversalRoleplayingSystem/development/docs/assets/rulebook-lod-2.svg)

> A planet is not a beachhead. It is a world of environments, infrastructure, inhabitants, institutions, and interdependent systems.

This LOD 2 sourcebook develops planetary assault and expeditionary warfare as a distinct science-fiction domain. It studies the transition from interplanetary or orbital presence to atmospheric and surface operations, force organization, landing and reception systems, foothold governance, sustainment, civilian protection, and escalation. It is a conceptual doctrine and simulation framework, not a real-world attack plan, landing procedure, targeting guide, or weapons manual.

## 1. Scope and terminology

Planetary assault describes a coercive or military attempt to establish presence or control on a planetary body. Planetary expeditionary operations are broader and may include defense, relief, evacuation, inspection, peacekeeping, scientific support, treaty enforcement, or reconstruction. The terms must not be treated as synonyms.

An expeditionary force can arrive with permission and still face severe operational constraints. An assault can achieve a landing while failing politically, logistically, legally, or humanely. Define the mission's actual political objective before describing its military organization.

## 2. Evidence labels and science-fiction discipline

Use four explicit labels:
- **Established:** supported by evidence, known physical principles, or documented current capability.
- **Extrapolated:** a plausible extension of existing science or engineering, with uncertain implementation.
- **Speculative:** dependent on unresolved science, engineering, economics, or political assumptions.
- **Fictional:** an invented setting rule.

Current orbital access and robotic planetary exploration are established; routine large-scale military landing on another inhabited planet is not. Faster-than-light travel, gravity control, force fields, and effortless atmospheric entry must be marked fictional unless a setting explicitly defines them as technology.

## 3. A planet is a complex environment

A planetary body may include oceans, continents, mountains, deserts, ice, atmosphere, weather, tectonics, biospheres, cities, industrial regions, habitats, orbital infrastructure, and communities with distinct political systems. A landing zone is not an empty tile. A planetary campaign interacts with a living world.

Represent environmental conditions by location and time. Physics owns gravity, atmosphere, fluids, heat, radiation, terrain, and material behavior. raWWar owns force organization, mission context, political relationships, doctrine concepts, and simulation requirements.

## 4. The orbital-to-surface transition

Planetary operations cross several different domains: interplanetary transit, orbital insertion or arrival, orbital support, atmospheric interface where applicable, surface access, and onward movement. Each transition has its own vehicles, people, authorities, constraints, and failure modes.

Do not collapse these into one generic landing animation. Preserve mission identity and custody across transport changes. Distinguish a vehicle's arrival from the arrival of its passengers, cargo, command authority, and support services.

## 5. Orbital presence and access

An orbital presence may include crewed vehicles, uncrewed systems, stations, habitats, relays, depots, scientific platforms, and local infrastructure. These assets do not automatically share one command or legal status. Their trajectories are time-dependent, and access depends on orbital state, available energy, communications, and support.

[Formations VI](formations-vi-orbital-and-space-forces.md) owns orbital formation organization. [Orbital Warfare and Space Infrastructure](orbital-warfare-and-space-infrastructure.md) owns the wider infrastructure and governance context. This volume considers how those systems constrain an expeditionary mission.

## 6. Planetary arrival systems

Arrival systems may be fictionalized as reusable transports, expendable landers, cargo systems, surface elevators, tether networks, atmospheric craft, or locally operated transfer infrastructure. Their capabilities must be tied to explicit technology assumptions, not borrowed from naval or aircraft terminology without qualification.

Record capacity, reliability, turnaround, environmental limits, maintenance, crew exposure, and dependence on local facilities. Do not provide real-world entry trajectories, landing profiles, targeting parameters, or operational procedures.

## 7. Force composition and mission packages

An expeditionary organization may include command, security, engineering, medical, scientific, communications, logistics, environmental assessment, legal liaison, diplomatic staff, and civilian support. A combat-oriented force is only one possible composition.

The force should be built around the actual mission and its support obligations. A small relief mission may require more medical, engineering, water, and public-health capacity than armed personnel. A scientific expedition may require contamination control and local consent more than coercive authority.

## 8. The landing is not the objective

A successful physical arrival does not establish durable control, legitimacy, safety, or mission success. The receiving environment may lack compatible infrastructure, may reject the expedition, or may be unable to support the arriving population. An apparently secure foothold can remain dependent on distant transport and fragile orbital services.

Define separate success criteria for arrival, safe reception, sustained service, lawful authority, civilian protection, and political settlement. A game should allow the mission to fail or change after landing.

## 9. Establishing a foothold and reception system

A foothold is a network of access, shelter, medical care, communications, utilities, transport, storage, governance, and relationships with local institutions. It is not merely a perimeter or a captured location.

The first durable capabilities may be water, pressure-safe shelter, sanitation, power, repair, medical triage, translation, and reliable communication. Their priorities depend on the environment and population. Engineering work must include inspection, ownership, maintenance, and handover.

## 10. Planetary logistics and industrial dependence

A planet can be physically rich but operationally inaccessible. Resources may require survey, extraction rights, processing, energy, skilled labor, spare parts, and environmental safeguards. Imported equipment may be impossible to repair locally. The expedition's endurance depends on the full supply chain and its weakest interfaces.

The [Art of Resupply](the-art-of-resupply.md), [Interstellar Logistics and Strategy](interstellar-logistics-and-strategy.md), and [Formations IX](formations-ix-engineering-and-construction-forces.md) provide adjacent disciplines. Keep imported supplies, locally available resources, usable production, and sustainable capacity distinct.

## 11. Atmosphere, gravity, weather, and biology

A world may have high or low gravity, dense or thin atmosphere, toxic chemistry, corrosive dust, extreme temperatures, severe weather, unfamiliar pathogens, or no breathable atmosphere. Native life, if present, may be vulnerable to terrestrial organisms and vice versa.

Do not assume Earth-normal physiology or environmental compatibility. Track uncertainty and quarantine requirements as setting rules. Alien biology is not a cosmetic skin applied to a human health system; it may require different models and ethical safeguards.

## 12. Civilian populations and political authority

An inhabited world is not a single actor. It may contain states, cities, indigenous communities, autonomous regions, corporations, religious or cultural institutions, displaced populations, stateless people, and local governments. The recognized authority may not be the only legitimate or representative institution.

Never infer consent or hostility from species, appearance, language, or residence. Model individual agency, institutional legitimacy, internal disagreement, legal protections, and the difference between a government and its population.

## 13. Planetary defense and reciprocal vulnerability

Defenders may include planetary governments, local authorities, orbital services, civil defense, emergency networks, and noncombatant institutions. Their priorities may be protecting people and infrastructure rather than defeating an invading force. Attackers and defenders both depend on fragile life-support, energy, communication, and transport systems.

This book does not provide targeting, strike sequencing, weapons employment, or methods for defeating planetary defenses. It treats defensive capability as an institutional and infrastructure system and emphasizes civilian survival, escalation control, and negotiated outcomes.

## 14. Command, law, and rules of authority

A planetary mission may involve expeditionary command, vessel masters, host governments, local authorities, treaty bodies, civilian agencies, scientific institutions, and autonomous systems. Arrival does not confer sovereignty. Emergency access does not automatically authorize permanent occupation or resource extraction.

Represent the source and limits of authority, duration, oversight, appeal, accountability, and termination conditions. Separate mission command, technical control, civil jurisdiction, and legal responsibility.

## 15. Autonomous systems and delegated agency

Robotic scouts, cargo systems, habitat maintenance, medical support, and decision aids may be essential when communications are delayed or environments are hazardous. Their authority must be explicit and bounded. A system should expose uncertainty, provenance, sensor health, and the limits of its delegation.

Autonomous decisions affecting civilians, life support, access, or irreversible environmental change require accountable human and institutional governance. See [Autonomous Systems and Machine Agency](autonomous-systems-and-machine-agency.md).

## 16. Escalation, restraint, and nonviolent alternatives

Planetary operations create extreme escalation risks because infrastructure damage can threaten entire populations. A conflict over a landing site, port, relay, or resource may become a crisis for unrelated communities. Military advantage cannot be assessed independently of ecological and civilian consequences.

Scenarios should include diplomacy, inspection, arbitration, evacuation, shared administration, humanitarian access, demilitarized arrangements, and withdrawal. War is not the inevitable outcome of contact or disagreement.

## 17. Campaign phases as analytical categories

A campaign can be studied through political authorization, transit, arrival, reception, establishment of services, interaction with local institutions, sustained presence, transition, and withdrawal. These are analytical categories, not a prescriptive operational sequence. A mission may skip, repeat, or reverse phases.

For each phase, record the responsible organization, objective, legal authority, dependencies, affected population, uncertainty, and conditions for stopping or changing course.

## 18. Failure modes and consequences

Failure can occur through transport loss, environmental incompatibility, communications interruption, medical overload, resource shortfall, local rejection, illegitimate authority, contamination, infrastructure damage, coalition fracture, or strategic misjudgment. These failures interact: a logistics interruption may become a public-health crisis, while a legitimacy crisis may close access to repair facilities.

Model cascading consequences without making them deterministic. Preserve opportunities for rescue, repair, mediation, evacuation, and de-escalation.

## 19. Tabletop and simulation framework

A planetary expeditionary scenario should define:
- planetary body, environment, and evidence status;
- inhabitants, institutions, and contested authority;
- mission purpose and legal basis;
- participating organizations and command boundaries;
- transport and arrival technology assumptions;
- orbital and surface infrastructure dependencies;
- environmental, medical, and biological constraints;
- logistics, repair, and local production;
- civilian protection and humanitarian obligations;
- autonomous system delegation;
- success, failure, withdrawal, and negotiated settlement conditions.

Use persistent identities for people, vessels, organizations, facilities, and communities. A landing event should not silently create ownership or political control.

## 20. Fictional case design

A useful fictional case might involve a coalition expedition arriving at a world whose orbital relay is operated by one authority, surface ports by several city leagues, and water infrastructure by a local commons. The expedition's charter permits emergency relief but not occupation or extraction. A storm damages a transfer facility, while local groups disagree over who may authorize repairs.

The central challenge is coordination, consent, service continuity, and the temptation to expand emergency authority—not a prescribed sequence of attacks. Multiple endings should include shared administration, local-led relief, evacuation, treaty revision, or mission termination.

## 21. Sources and research agenda

For established context, use planetary science, astrodynamics, atmospheric entry research, spaceflight history, disaster response, expeditionary logistics, international law, humanitarian practice, and comparative colonial history. For speculative claims, state the engineering and political assumptions explicitly. Fictional case studies should identify their invented premises.

Do not cite science fiction as evidence of physical feasibility. It can provide design inspiration, not proof.

## 22. Ownership and boundaries

raWWar owns planetary expeditionary doctrine as warfare-domain knowledge, organizational context, historical comparison, scenario framing, and simulation requirements. Physics owns physical laws and environment. SURPS owns shared composition conventions and remains opt-in. Orbital, logistics, engineering, medicine, and autonomous-system providers retain their authoritative domains.

## Editorial rule

A planet is not an objective marker. It is a world. Any doctrine that cannot account for the people, institutions, infrastructure, and ecology already there is an incomplete doctrine.
