> From temporary field camps to fortified bases, legionary camps, and persistent installations.

A comparative manual for military camps and bases: layout, terrain, security, services, command, civilian interaction, endurance, and the logistics that keep a position alive.

This raWWar-owned LOD 2 manual is for historical study, roleplaying, and simulation design. It distinguishes evidence from interpretation and fictional adaptation. It is not a real-world operational training manual. Shared physical behavior belongs to Physics; embodied movement belongs to Gestures; this book defines warfare-domain meaning, context, and evaluation.

## 1. What is a base?

A base is an organized place that supports people, equipment, command, repair, storage, movement, or operations over time. An encampment may be temporary and mobile; a fort, depot, airfield, naval station, forward operating site, and permanent garrison have different purposes and lifecycles. Not every base is a fortress, and not every camp is a miniature city.

## 2. Design begins with purpose

Define mission, expected population, duration, mobility, threat environment, climate, host community, legal authority, access, and resupply assumptions before drawing a perimeter. A layout is a set of tradeoffs among protection, movement, sanitation, visibility, repair, morale, and environmental impact.

## 3. Comparative historical forms

Compare temporary campaigning camps, Roman marching and permanent camps, medieval field encampments, fortified caravanserais and depots, early-modern siege camps, naval anchorages, expeditionary bases, airbases, and modern logistics hubs. Roman camps had recognizable planning traditions, but practices varied by period, terrain, threat, and operational context.

## 4. Small mobile encampments

A small company or raiding party needs sleeping/rest areas, command and communications, equipment accountability, cooking and water, sanitation, medical care, animal or vehicle handling, watch and access control, waste management, and a route to depart. Compactness improves mobility but increases congestion, noise, and vulnerability to a single disruption.

## 5. Fictional and literary examples

Use fantasy literature as a design prompt, not historical evidence. The Aiel in Robert Jordan's Wheel of Time and Mat Cauthon's Band of the Red Hand can inspire contrasting camp identities, but raWWar should create original layouts and avoid copying protected prose or distinctive visual expression. The question is what the camp's organization says about command, mobility, trust, discipline, and supply.

## 6. Larger field camps

A larger force needs differentiated zones for subordinate units, command, stores, maintenance, medical care, transport, animals, workshops, water, sanitation, waste, and visitors. Routes should prevent routine traffic from blocking emergency movement. The camp must remain legible to new arrivals and resilient when a zone becomes unavailable.

## 7. Roman camp as a case study

Explore the Roman castrum as an evolving military planning tradition: gates, streets, headquarters, standards, barracks, stores, workshops, defenses, and relationship to roads and campaign geography. Do not turn a familiar schematic into a universal template for every legion, every era, or every terrain. Distinguish temporary marching camps from permanent forts and legionary bases.

## 8. Terrain, water, and ecology

Site selection considers slope, drainage, flood, soil, water quality, fire, wind, heat, cold, disease ecology, local livelihoods, and access. A camp can damage the watershed or displace residents even when its perimeter is secure. Environmental consequences persist after departure.

## 9. Defense and protection

Perimeter, observation, access control, lighting, barriers, shelters, dispersal, warning, and emergency routes should be designed around a stated threat model. This book describes design principles at a game and historical level; it does not provide a modern real-world base hardening blueprint. Security must coexist with medical access, civilian protection, and lawful conduct.

## 10. Internal life and governance

A base is a community of workers, soldiers, contractors, dependents, visitors, prisoners, and sometimes displaced civilians. Responsibilities for water, food, sanitation, medical services, discipline, complaints, safeguarding, and local agreements should be explicit. Do not erase labor or treat civilian presence as scenery.

## 11. Resilience and continuity

Plan for power loss, water interruption, fire, severe weather, epidemic, damaged communications, supply delay, overcrowding, and evacuation. Redundancy, repair capacity, distributed stores, trained personnel, and clear authority matter as much as walls.

## 12. Layout as simulation data

Represent zones, access edges, capacity, service dependencies, hazard exposure, occupancy, ownership, and movement flows. A map icon does not equal a functioning service. A clinic without staff, power, consumables, or access is not operational medical capacity.

## 13. Scenario and scoring design

Scenarios include a one-night mobile camp, a column establishing a temporary halt, a Roman-style marching camp reconstruction, a long-term frontier fort, an airbase under severe weather, and a humanitarian staging base. Score mission support, safety, sanitation, access, continuity, local impact, and time-to-establish—not just perimeter strength.

## 14. Sources and adaptation

Historical reconstructions should cite archaeological plans, excavation reports, contemporary descriptions, inscriptions, logistics records, and scholarly disagreement. Literary examples are labeled as fiction. Any game-specific invented camp is labeled as original adaptation.

## Cross-domain ownership and implementation boundary

These books are descriptive domain knowledge. They do not introduce executable APIs, duplicate a physics engine, or claim that a HUD, haptic rig, or simulation feature already exists. A host may opt into SURPS composition; without it, the raWWar Experience remains valid. Any game-specific doctrine, scoring rubric, or fictional equipment must be versioned and identified as such.
