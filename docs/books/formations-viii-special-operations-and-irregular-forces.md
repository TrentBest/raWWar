# Formations VIII: Special Operations and Irregular Forces

**Category:** Domain · Formations and irregular organizations  
**LOD:** 2  
**Status:** Living sourcebook · version 0.1.0  
**Scope:** Specialized regular forces, irregular armed organizations, resistance, insurgency, partisan formations, militias, auxiliaries, clandestine political networks, governance, accountability, and transition.

## 1. Purpose and scope

This volume studies organizations whose structure, legal status, relationship to a state, or mode of political participation differs from a conventional standing formation. It is not a manual for conducting raids, clandestine activity, sabotage, evasion, targeting, or violence. Its purpose is historical understanding, organizational design, civilian-centered scenario construction, and transparent simulation.

The title joins two subjects that must not be collapsed. **Special operations** describes a category of military specialization that can exist inside a regular state force. **Irregular** describes an organizational or political relationship to conventional state military structures, formal recognition, or established methods of warfare. A highly specialized unit may be entirely regular; an irregular movement may be locally organized without possessing specialist military capability.

## 2. A vocabulary that resists false equivalence

Terms are historically contingent and often politically contested. Use them as claims to investigate, not neutral labels.

- **Special operations force (SOF):** a state-authorized military organization selected, trained, equipped, or tasked for missions requiring specialized capabilities. Definitions and legal authorities vary by state and era.
- **Specialized regular unit:** a formally constituted unit with a distinct role, qualification system, or institutional culture. Not every elite or specialist unit is a special operations force.
- **Irregular armed organization:** a broad analytical term for an armed group that does not fit the conventional organization of a state's regular armed forces. It says nothing by itself about legitimacy or conduct.
- **Resistance movement:** organized opposition to an authority, occupation, coercive system, or external domination. Resistance can be political, cultural, civil, humanitarian, or armed.
- **Insurgency:** a sustained political and organizational challenge to an authority's control or legitimacy. It is not simply a synonym for guerrilla fighting.
- **Partisan organization:** a historically variable term often used for irregular forces associated with resistance or a political movement, especially in occupied or contested territory.
- **Militia:** a locally or politically organized armed body; its legal status, command, membership, and relationship to public authority vary widely.
- **Auxiliary or proxy force:** an organization supported, sponsored, or employed by another actor while retaining some degree of separate identity or agency. Support does not prove total control.
- **Paramilitary organization:** an armed or security organization structured along military lines but not necessarily part of the regular armed forces. The term may describe lawful institutions, political organizations, or abusive coercive bodies; context is essential.
- **Clandestine political network:** a non-public organization or relationship system. It may be unarmed and should not be automatically classified as a military formation.
- **Criminal armed group:** an organization whose activity includes organized predation or illicit coercion. Criminality, political claims, and local protection can coexist and must be examined separately.

## 3. Four independent dimensions

Do not place every organization on a single regular-to-irregular ladder. Record at least four independent dimensions:

1. **Institutional position:** state military, police or gendarmerie, locally authorized body, non-state organization, or uncertain/contested status.
2. **Political relationship:** loyal, oppositional, separatist, revolutionary, communal, commercial, externally sponsored, or mixed.
3. **Organizational form:** permanent unit, temporary coalition, cell-based network, territorial organization, community defense body, or fluid association.
4. **Conduct and accountability:** command responsibility, discipline, treatment of civilians and detainees, resource practices, internal review, and external oversight.

A group's label does not settle its legitimacy. A state may use the word “terrorist” or “bandit” to delegitimize opponents; a movement may call itself “resistance” while committing abuses. Record the speaker, context, evidence, and affected communities.

## 4. Specialization within regular forces

Specialized units emerge from institutional requirements, geography, technology, political decisions, and accumulated experience. Their identity may rest on selection, qualification, trust, specialist equipment, language or cultural knowledge, technical expertise, or a distinctive command relationship.

Selection is not proof of moral superiority, invulnerability, or universal competence. Qualification is role-specific and decays without practice, support, and reassessment. A small specialist organization remains dependent on wider institutions for personnel administration, intelligence governance, legal review, medical care, transport, maintenance, communications, and replacement capacity.

For historical and fictional comparison, distinguish a unit's public mythology from its documented mandate, actual record, and the conditions under which it operated.

## 5. Organizational anatomy

Describe organizations through observable structures rather than romantic archetypes:

- membership and eligibility;
- leadership selection and succession;
- authority and delegation;
- internal discipline and dispute resolution;
- political or community relationships;
- resource acquisition and distribution;
- medical, welfare, and family support;
- records, communication, and institutional memory;
- recruitment, retention, departure, and reintegration;
- oversight, complaint, and accountability channels.

A network may have no single commander yet still contain influential brokers, resource gatekeepers, or local authorities. A formal hierarchy may exist on paper while practical authority is fragmented. The model should preserve both declared structure and observed relationships.

## 6. Historical comparison without a universal template

Irregular and specialized formations appear in many different settings: resistance to imperial rule, occupation, civil war, frontier conflict, anti-colonial movements, revolutions, state-building, local defense, and organized crime. Similar names do not guarantee similar institutions.

Compare cases through their own political and material context: who claimed authority, what legal order applied, what communities experienced, what sources survive, and how the organization changed over time. Avoid projecting modern special-operations categories onto ancient or medieval forces unless the comparison is explicitly analytical rather than a claim of institutional continuity.

## 7. Resistance under occupation and coercive rule

Resistance is not one organization or one method. It may include non-cooperation, mutual aid, preservation of language and institutions, documentation, strikes, escape assistance, political organizing, public advocacy, and armed opposition. Different participants may disagree about acceptable conduct and desired outcomes.

The civilian population is not a resource pool owned by a movement. People may support, oppose, fear, avoid, negotiate with, or simply endure competing authorities. Track coercion, consent, survival needs, displacement, reprisals, and the risks borne by people who have little control over armed actors.

## 8. Insurgency as a political and social process

An insurgency is not reducible to a fighting force. It may attempt to build institutions, claim representation, administer territory, collect resources, deliver services, or negotiate recognition. It may also fragment, prey upon civilians, or lose its constituency.

Analyze the relationship between stated aims and actual conduct. Distinguish political participation from coerced compliance, public support from silence under threat, and territorial presence from legitimate governance. Do not assume an insurgency inevitably grows, wins, or becomes a state.

## 9. Partisans, militias, and local defense

Local armed organizations vary in origin and accountability. Some are formally authorized and integrated into public institutions; others are self-organized, factional, externally sponsored, or contested. Their relationship with the surrounding population may change rapidly as leadership, threat, resources, and political conditions shift.

A local-defense label does not guarantee defensive conduct. Record who authorized the organization, whose safety it claims to protect, how membership is controlled, how disputes are handled, what oversight exists, and what happens when an emergency ends.

## 10. Auxiliaries, proxies, and external sponsorship

External support can include funding, equipment, training, political recognition, logistics, sanctuary, or diplomatic backing. The sponsor may seek influence without exercising complete command. The supported organization may bargain, resist, reinterpret, or redirect the relationship.

A simulation should represent degrees of dependence and agency rather than a binary “puppet” flag. Track conditions attached to support, competing patrons, local legitimacy, accountability gaps, and the consequences when sponsorship is withdrawn.

## 11. Clandestine networks are not automatically military formations

Underground political parties, labor networks, religious communities, couriers, publishers, aid organizations, family ties, and mutual-aid systems may operate under repression without being combat organizations. Their participants may reject violence or have no knowledge of armed activity.

Do not automatically merge a civil network into an armed group's command tree. Preserve consent, compartmentalized knowledge, independent goals, and the danger of collective punishment. A scenario can center on protecting civic institutions, preserving evidence, negotiating safe access, or restoring public life without turning every participant into a combatant.

## 12. Civilians, law, and protection

Legal classification depends on facts and applicable law, not on a game's faction name. International humanitarian law distinguishes categories of persons and regulates conduct in armed conflict; domestic law and human-rights obligations may also apply. This sourcebook is not legal advice and does not replace jurisdiction-specific expert review.

For historical study and fictional design, ask who is protected, who claims authority, how detention and treatment are reviewed, how missing persons are recorded, how property and livelihoods are affected, and what remedies exist. Never use civilian identity as a proxy for allegiance.

## 13. Fragmentation, factionalism, and internal conflict

Organizations split over ideology, leadership, geography, resources, external patrons, generational change, or disputed conduct. Splinter groups may claim continuity while rejecting the parent organization's authority. A central committee may not control local members; local autonomy may be real or merely rhetorical.

Represent factional relationships as dated claims with evidence and uncertainty. Avoid treating a movement as a single actor across decades. Track defections, mergers, ceasefires, leadership disputes, local agreements, and changing constituencies without assuming that every split leads to violence.

## 14. Demobilization, disarmament, and reintegration

The end of armed activity is an institutional transition, not a button that deletes personnel. Possible pathways include disarmament, demobilization, integration into public institutions, civilian employment, local political participation, prosecution, amnesty under lawful conditions, exile, continued non-state organization, or renewed conflict.

Outcomes depend on security guarantees, credible institutions, livelihoods, recognition, trauma care, family reunification, land and property disputes, education, and public trust. Avoid treating former members as interchangeable or permanently dangerous. Preserve individual agency and the rights of victims and affected communities.

## 15. Command, identity, and personnel state

For simulation, keep person, role, organization, affiliation, and current authority as separate records. Membership can change without erasing personal identity. A person may have a historical affiliation, a current legal status, a temporary assignment, and a contested public label simultaneously.

Useful state dimensions include:
- identity and provenance;
- current affiliation and effective dates;
- qualification and recency;
- declared versus observed authority;
- willingness and consent where relevant;
- welfare, health, and family impact;
- legal status and review provenance;
- uncertainty and source confidence.

Do not infer allegiance from ethnicity, language, religion, residence, family relationship, or appearance.

## 16. Readiness and capability without operational tradecraft

Readiness is contextual and multidimensional. A unit's existence does not prove availability, competence, cohesion, lawful authorization, or fitness for a particular task. Model staffing, qualification currency, leadership continuity, equipment condition, communications reliability, medical support, institutional trust, and legal authority as distinct dimensions.

This book deliberately excludes actionable methods for clandestine operations, raids, sabotage, evasion, targeting, weapons employment, or defeating security systems. Scenarios should examine organizational consequences, historical evidence, civilian protection, negotiation, oversight, and recovery rather than train players in real-world violence.

## 17. Ghost organization and transparent evaluation

A training or simulation presentation may show an organization as a ghost structure: role slots, reporting relationships, qualification states, support dependencies, and missing institutional links. The visualization must distinguish confirmed records from inferred or fictional placeholders.

A transparent rubric can assess:
- clarity of mandate and authority;
- role coverage and succession resilience;
- lawful oversight and complaint access;
- civilian protection and community trust;
- medical and welfare support;
- resource sustainability;
- information provenance;
- transition and demobilization planning.

Do not score success by casualties, intimidation, coercive recruitment, or control of civilians. Make uncertainty visible and permit nonviolent outcomes.

## 18. Failure, abuse, and institutional safeguards

Failure modes include leader capture, factional fragmentation, resource predation, coercive recruitment, impunity, false attribution, sponsor withdrawal, command ambiguity, intelligence misuse, collective punishment, and the conversion of emergency authority into permanent rule.

Safeguards include traceable authorization, independent review, documented orders, complaint channels, protection for dissent and whistleblowing, transparent resource accounting, civilian representation, and credible investigation of abuses. Safeguards can fail; record whether they exist on paper, function in practice, and are trusted by affected people.

## 19. Scenario frames

- **The contested mandate:** a specialist unit receives conflicting public descriptions of its authority; players reconstruct the legal and institutional record.
- **The village between authorities:** residents negotiate services and protection while refusing to be treated as a faction's property.
- **The divided movement:** a ceasefire proposal exposes disagreements among local organizations, political representatives, and external sponsors.
- **The missing roster:** families seek reliable information about dispersed members while institutions dispute responsibility for records.
- **After the emergency:** a local defense organization must decide its future under civilian oversight, with several lawful transition paths.
- **The archive of labels:** historians compare official records, testimony, propaganda, and later memory to understand how one organization was described by competing actors.

Each scenario should provide meaningful roles for civilians, mediators, archivists, local officials, medical workers, former members, and observers—not only armed leaders.

## 20. Evidence and editorial method

Label material as:
- **Documented:** supported by identifiable primary evidence or well-established records.
- **Interpretation:** an argued reading of incomplete or contested evidence.
- **Contested:** credible sources disagree or the record is politically shaped.
- **Folklore and memory:** stories that reveal cultural memory but are not direct proof of events.
- **Fictional:** invented setting material.
- **Speculative:** a plausible proposal whose evidence or implementation remains uncertain.

Identify the source's author, audience, date, institutional position, and likely incentives. Test official reports against independent records and affected communities' testimony where available. Do not treat absence from an archive as proof that an event or person did not exist.

## 21. Simulation contract and ownership boundaries

raWWar owns the warfare-domain vocabulary, historical context, organizational distinctions, scenario framing, and domain-specific evaluation. Physics owns physical laws and simulation. Gestures owns reusable embodied movement and interaction. Data and identity providers own their authoritative records. The host owns execution and persistence. SURPS composition is opt-in; this sourcebook does not create a hidden runtime dependency.

A reusable organization descriptor should identify:
- organization identity and version;
- scope and historical/fictional status;
- institutional position and political relationship as separate fields;
- membership and authority model;
- effective dates and provenance;
- support dependencies;
- legal and accountability references;
- uncertainty and evidence labels;
- links to related organizations and scenarios.

Descriptors should not encode stereotypes or infer personal allegiance from protected or incidental characteristics.

## Editorial rule

Never equate specialization with virtue, irregularity with illegitimacy, a movement with its entire population, or a political label with a verified legal status. Keep institutional form, political purpose, conduct, evidence, and civilian experience distinct.
