# Orbital Warfare and Space Infrastructure

**Category:** Speculative and future warfare · **LOD:** 2 · **Version:** 0.1.0  
**Scope:** Conflict, security, resilience, and governance involving orbital systems and space infrastructure. This is a systems-level framework, not a prediction of inevitable war in space.

## Start with the infrastructure

Modern societies depend on space-based services for communications, navigation, timing, weather observation, remote sensing, scientific research, and coordination. Future settlements may add habitats, depots, transport nodes, power systems, manufacturing, and rescue services. These assets have owners, operators, users, dependencies, maintenance cycles, and legal status. A useful space-conflict study begins with those relationships rather than assuming every satellite is a weapon.

Space infrastructure is often dual-use. The same observation system can support disaster response and military planning; the same communications network can serve civilians and armed forces. This creates difficult questions of distinction, proportionality, escalation, and resilience.

## Physical environment

Orbital motion is governed by gravity and velocity. Objects in orbit do not behave like ships that can stop, turn, or hold position without cost. Changes in orbit require energy and time; relative motion, orbital inclination, altitude, transfer windows, and debris environments shape access. Space systems also face radiation, thermal cycling, micrometeoroids, limited repair opportunities, and communications constraints.

A scenario should identify which physical assumptions it uses. Do not invent arbitrary “space lanes” unless the setting defines why they exist. If fictional propulsion or artificial gravity changes these constraints, document the rules and consequences.

## Infrastructure dependencies

A system map may include launch facilities, tracking networks, ground stations, communications relays, navigation and timing services, satellites, servicing vehicles, fuel depots, traffic coordination, data centers, and human operators. Future settings may add lunar or asteroid facilities and orbital habitats. Failures can cascade: loss of timing affects coordination; debris can threaten unrelated operators; interrupted communications can undermine rescue and emergency services.

Resilience includes redundancy, repairability, graceful degradation, transparent coordination, and the ability to operate safely when information is incomplete. It is not merely hardening every asset.

## Conflict and coercion

Potential conflict patterns include interference with services, coercive inspection, blockade-like restrictions, contested access, cyber and information operations, sabotage, attacks on ground infrastructure, threats to commercial operators, and disputes over rescue or salvage. These are analytical categories, not recommendations. The book should focus on governance, consequences, prevention, and scenario design rather than procedural instructions for damaging real systems.

Escalation may be driven by ambiguous attribution, compressed decision time, dual-use ambiguity, alliance commitments, commercial incentives, or fear that a temporary loss of access will become permanent. Communication channels, verification, incident reporting, and agreed rules can reduce miscalculation.

## Law and responsibility

Outer space is subject to international legal frameworks, including the Outer Space Treaty and other relevant agreements. The legal status of a specific activity depends on the facts and applicable law; do not assume that “space is ungoverned.” Distinguish state responsibility, private operator conduct, registration, jurisdiction, liability, peaceful use, and the law applicable during armed conflict. This book is educational and not legal advice.

Civilian services and essential infrastructure require particular care. A disabled navigation or communications service can affect aircraft, ships, emergency response, financial systems, and disaster relief far beyond the immediate theater.

## Tabletop and simulation framework

Represent orbital assets as a dependency network with attributes such as orbit, service, operator, users, redundancy, maintenance state, resilience, legal status, and uncertainty. Campaign-level variables may include access, availability, trust, attribution confidence, debris risk, repair capacity, and escalation. Keep tactical detail abstract unless a fictional setting has a well-defined technology model.

Scenario seeds: a satellite failure during a ceasefire; a rescue craft denied access to a disputed station; a private operator pressured by rival governments; a debris event with uncertain origin; a lunar settlement seeking independent communications; or a verification mission whose data is challenged by all parties.

## Research direction

Ground the real-physics layer in orbital mechanics, space systems engineering, space traffic management, space policy, resilience engineering, and international law. Clearly label all extrapolation. Never present a speculative scenario as a forecast or current operational capability.
