# Military Resources and Sustainment

> The Singularity Workshop · raWWar Domain Sourcebook · LOD 2 · Version 0.1.0

A lifecycle study of the resources that make organized forces possible: food, water, energy, materials, labor, finance, transport, maintenance, recovery, and the human and political consequences of scarcity.

## Purpose and use

This volume is a reusable historical, institutional, and simulation-design reference. It distinguishes documented history, current evidence, extrapolation, speculation, and fictional setting rules. It complements the broader [Warfare and Organized Force](warfare-and-organized-force.md) foundation and the [sourcebook catalogue](index.md).

## 1. Scope and central question

A military force is constrained by what it can obtain, move, preserve, transform, allocate, and replace. This book treats resources as a living system rather than a list of inventory categories. It follows resources from source to user and back into repair, reuse, waste, or the environment.

The central question is not simply “how much does the force have?” It is “what can the force reliably make available, to whom, where, when, in what condition, and at what cost?” A warehouse full of food does not feed a unit if the food is inaccessible, spoiled, culturally unsuitable, unsafe, or cannot be distributed. A fuel reserve is not useful to a platform that cannot accept it. A trained workforce is not interchangeable with a pile of components.

The volume is historical, institutional, and simulation-design material. It does not provide real-world operational targeting, attack planning, or instructions for seizing or sabotaging supply systems.

## 2. Resource categories

Track at least: food and feed; potable and process water; energy carriers and generation; clothing and protective equipment; shelter and utilities; construction materials; metals and engineered materials; tools and replacement parts; medical supplies; communications and information services; transport capacity; skilled and unskilled labor; finance and credit; time; land and access rights; and ecological services.

Resources may be consumable, durable, reusable, repairable, recyclable, renewable, replenishable, or effectively non-renewable on the relevant timescale. The classification depends on the campaign horizon. A forest may regenerate over decades but cannot replenish a fleet's timber requirement this month. A machine tool may last years but become useless without calibration, power, operators, or spares.

Record quality and compatibility, not just quantity. Spoiled grain, contaminated water, incompatible fuel, untrained labor, corroded parts, and obsolete ammunition are not equivalent to ready resources.

## 3. Food: the first logistics problem

Foraging is among the oldest ways armed groups have obtained food, but it is not a magic source. It depends on season, ecology, population density, local knowledge, preservation, transport, and the presence of edible resources. Foraging can supplement a force; it rarely guarantees stable large-scale supply. It can also damage the same landscape and communities on which future survival depends.

Agricultural states and settled military institutions developed different relationships with food: taxation in kind, granaries, market purchase, contracted supply, state estates, transport animals, ration issues, and provisioning fleets. Each system distributes risk differently. Stored grain can buffer seasonal shortage but is vulnerable to spoilage, pests, fire, mismanagement, and political seizure.

Food is not a single commodity. Nutrition, water content, preparation fuel, dietary practice, food safety, storage life, and distribution labor all matter. Soldiers, sailors, animals, prisoners, patients, and civilian dependents may have different requirements. Model kitchens, milling, baking, slaughter, preservation, sanitation, and waste as work systems.

## 4. From foraging to requisition, tribute, and plunder

Historical forces have obtained provisions through foraging, purchase, taxation, tribute, requisition, contracts, state provisioning, gifts, allied contributions, captured stores, and plunder. These categories are not synonyms. They differ in authority, consent, legal status, accounting, predictability, and effects on civilians.

Requisition may be formally authorized but still coercive. Tribute may be embedded in a political relationship. Purchase can enrich local markets or inflate prices beyond civilian reach. Plunder may provide immediate goods while destroying trust, production, and future access. A force that consumes the seed grain, breeding stock, tools, or winter stores of a district may win a short-term supply advantage while creating famine, displacement, resistance, and a long-term security crisis.

Do not model pillage as a free resource bonus. Track who owned the goods, who loses them, whether the goods can be used, how they are transported, the political consequences, and the degradation of future supply. Distinguish documented practice from later propaganda and romanticized accounts.

## 5. Water, sanitation, and disease

Water has multiple uses: drinking, cooking, cleaning, medical care, animal care, cooling, industrial processes, and fire protection. A source that is adequate for one use may be unsuitable for another. Access, treatment, storage, distribution, contamination, and waste removal are linked.

Sanitation is a resource system, not an optional amenity. Waste management, drainage, latrines, washing, food handling, pest control, and disease surveillance affect readiness and civilian health. A force can possess abundant calories and still become incapacitated by unsafe water or infectious disease.

In simulations, represent source capacity, treatment throughput, storage, distribution reach, quality confidence, and failure recovery. Avoid reducing disease to a random morale penalty; it has causes, time courses, unequal exposure, and institutional consequences.

## 6. Energy and fuel

Energy systems vary by era and setting: human and animal labor, biomass, charcoal, coal, petroleum, gas, batteries, electrical grids, nuclear systems, solar generation, and speculative fusion or other technologies. Each has distinct extraction, conversion, storage, transport, safety, and waste requirements.

Fuel availability is not the same as usable energy. Platforms require compatible fuels, trained operators, maintenance, functioning distribution, and sometimes specialized infrastructure. Electrical energy may be abundant at a station but unavailable to a vehicle in the field. Batteries impose mass, charging, degradation, thermal, and recycling burdens.

For advanced settings, record power generation, storage, conversion efficiency, peak demand, thermal rejection, reserve margin, and dependency on external infrastructure. Do not let a generic “energy” resource erase incompatible carriers or conversion limits.

## 7. Clothing, shelter, and personal equipment

Clothing and protective equipment are consumable or durable systems with fit, climate, repair, replacement, hygiene, and manufacturing requirements. Footwear, weather protection, bedding, armor, respirators, pressure garments, and exoskeleton interfaces have different lifecycles. Fit and condition influence injury, fatigue, mobility, and dignity.

Shelter ranges from temporary cover and tents to barracks, ships, hardened facilities, habitats, and planetary settlements. It requires land or volume, materials, utilities, maintenance, waste systems, and protection from environmental hazards. A deployed force may need to build, rent, share, or negotiate access to facilities; each choice affects local communities and legal authority.

## 8. Ammunition, medical stores, and specialist consumables

Ammunition and medical supplies are mission-specific consumables, but their treatment should remain distinct. They require controlled manufacture, quality assurance, storage, transport, inventory discipline, trained use, disposal, and legal oversight. Medical stores also depend on diagnosis, skill, sterile conditions, patient volume, and clinical governance.

This book does not provide weapons employment or clinical procedures. For medical organization and casualty movement, see [Medicine and Healing](medicine-and-healing.md) and [Battlefield Medicine and the Casualty Chain](battlefield-medicine-and-the-casualty-chain.md). For transport and distribution architecture, see [The Art of Resupply](the-art-of-resupply.md).

## 9. Labor, skill, and time

A resource system consumes human attention and time. Farmers, miners, cooks, mechanics, engineers, medics, drivers, sailors, administrators, interpreters, cleaners, and repair crews are not interchangeable labor tokens. Skill takes time to acquire and can be lost through casualties, displacement, exhaustion, coercion, or institutional collapse.

Track staffing, qualification, workload, rest, turnover, safety, and succession. A facility with machines but no skilled maintainers is not fully operational. A unit may be technically equipped but unable to use its equipment because its specialists are absent. Forced labor and conscription may increase nominal capacity while degrading quality, legitimacy, safety, and long-term productivity.

## 10. Finance, markets, and political authority

Military resources are paid for through taxation, borrowing, public credit, private investment, requisition, tribute, allied contributions, commercial contracts, captured wealth, and other arrangements. These mechanisms create political constituencies and obligations. A force's budget is not a neutral pool: it reflects who can tax, who can lend, who is paid, whose property is taken, and who bears the cost.

Markets may continue to function during war, adapt around scarcity, or be distorted by price controls, blockade, insecurity, currency failure, and state intervention. Distinguish the existence of goods from purchasing power and physical access. A wealthy headquarters cannot buy supplies that cannot reach the theater.

## 11. Storage, preservation, and inventory truth

Stores degrade. Food spoils, medicines expire, batteries age, metals corrode, lubricants change, seals fail, and records become inaccurate. Inventory should carry condition, provenance, date, storage history, compatibility, ownership, and confidence.

Separate physical stock, reserved stock, allocated stock, in-transit stock, serviceable stock, and stock actually delivered to the end user. Do not let a requisition or dispatch order count as consumption satisfied. Record shrinkage and loss without automatically assuming theft; damage, measurement error, spoilage, and administrative delay also occur.

## 12. Transport and distribution

A resource must cross a network of routes, ports, depots, vehicles, crews, handling facilities, permissions, and schedules. Transport capacity is constrained by more than vehicle count: loading equipment, packaging, route conditions, weather, traffic, maintenance, labor, and receiving capacity all matter.

The final handoff is often the hardest. A shipment can reach a theater but remain unavailable because no receiving team, storage, distribution plan, compatible connector, or authorized custodian exists. See [The Art of Resupply](the-art-of-resupply.md), [Formations IX: Engineering and Construction Forces](formations-ix-engineering-and-construction-forces.md), and [Formations X: Armadas and Flotillas](formations-x-armadas-and-flotillas.md).

## 13. Repair, salvage, recycling, and circular supply

The resource lifecycle does not end at consumption or damage. Repair, refurbishment, cannibalization, salvage, recycling, remanufacture, and safe disposal can restore capacity or recover valuable material. These processes require diagnosis, skilled labor, tools, energy, feedstock separation, quality control, and a market or user for recovered output.

Recycling is not lossless or free. Mixed materials, contamination, coatings, composites, energy costs, transport, and degraded quality can make recovery difficult. Record yield and quality separately. Salvage rights and ownership can be contested, especially after battle, disaster, or occupation.

## 14. Scarcity, prioritization, and allocation

Scarcity is relational: demand exceeds supply at a particular place, time, quality, or price. A civilization may have abundant material in aggregate while a remote garrison faces acute shortage. Allocation decisions reveal institutional priorities and create winners, losers, and political consequences.

Model priority rules explicitly: medical urgency, civilian survival, infrastructure continuity, mission needs, contractual commitments, political influence, or equal shares. No rule is neutral in every circumstance. Record who decides, what information they possess, what appeals exist, and who bears the external cost.

## 15. Forage, requisition, and civilian survival in campaign history

Historical case studies should compare the supply practices of different periods without assuming a single linear progression from foraging to modern logistics. Industrial armies still depend on local food, water, labor, roads, ports, and markets. Modern forces may rely on global contracts while retaining local dependencies.

For each case, ask how the force fed people and animals; who produced the food; how it was acquired and transported; what was stored; what happened when routes failed; and how civilians adapted. Distinguish official doctrine from actual practice and retrospective legend.

## 16. Resources in space and across planets

A space force requires life support, water, oxygen or other atmosphere, food, radiation protection, thermal control, propellant or reaction mass, power, spares, medical capability, and pressure-vessel maintenance. Recycling can reduce resupply but cannot be assumed perfect. Every closed-loop system has leakage, contamination, maintenance, and replacement requirements.

A planetary garrison depends on local environment and infrastructure. A resource that is abundant on one world may be toxic, inaccessible, chemically bound, or uneconomic on another. Extraction also competes with settlement, ecological protection, local sovereignty, and the needs of civilian populations. Link to [Orbital Warfare and Space Infrastructure](orbital-warfare-and-space-infrastructure.md) and [Interstellar Logistics and Strategy](interstellar-logistics-and-strategy.md).

## 17. Resource state for simulation

A reusable resource record should include identity and category; quantity and unit; quality and condition; location and custody; owner and legal status; acquisition method; production or expiry date; storage conditions; compatibility; transport state; reserved and available amounts; demand rate; replenishment lead time; loss and waste; provenance confidence; and environmental or civilian externalities.

A force readiness summary should never collapse all resources into one percentage. Food endurance, water quality, energy reserve, maintenance backlog, medical capacity, replacement pipeline, labor availability, and political authorization are separate dimensions. A force can be well armed and unable to move; well supplied and medically overwhelmed; or materially capable but politically unable to act.

## 18. Playable scenarios and evaluation

Scenario frames include a winter garrison with abundant grain but inadequate fuel; a force choosing between purchase and coercive requisition; a fleet whose water-production plant fails; an army whose animal feed competes with civilian food; a repair depot with parts but no qualified technicians; and a remote settlement deciding whether to sell strategic materials to rival powers.

Evaluate endurance, truthful inventory, distribution equity, civilian harm, environmental effects, labor welfare, repair capacity, institutional legitimacy, and adaptation. A resource crisis should create meaningful choices rather than merely subtracting hit points.

## 19. Sources and provider boundaries

Research should use quartermaster records, tax and estate documents, ship manifests, ration scales, market prices, archaeology, agricultural history, environmental reconstruction, labor records, industrial statistics, and civilian testimony. Military memoirs and official returns are useful but rarely complete.

## Editorial and simulation principles

- Preserve provenance, uncertainty, ownership, authority, and the difference between nominal capacity and usable capacity.
- Treat civilian life, labor, environment, and political legitimacy as part of the system rather than externalities that can be ignored.
- Separate physical state from legal status, commercial ownership, organizational command, and permission to act.
- Use Established, Extrapolated, Speculative, and Fictional labels whenever future technology is discussed.
- SURPS participation is opt-in. raWWar owns warfare-domain meaning and simulation requirements; authoritative physical and industrial models remain with their respective providers.
