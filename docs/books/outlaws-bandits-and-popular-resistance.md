# Outlaws, Bandits, and Popular Resistance

**Category:** Irregular warfare and contested authority  
**LOD:** 2  
**Status:** Living sourcebook · version 0.1.0  
**Scope:** Outlawry, brigandage, banditry, social resistance, rebellion, insurgency, and their folklore.

## Why this belongs in raWWar

Not all organized force wears a uniform or claims a state. Armed groups outside formal institutions can rob, tax, protect, intimidate, resist, govern, or do several of these at once. Their relationship with surrounding communities is rarely simple. Some are celebrated as defenders; others are feared predators; many are both to different people at different times.

The Robin Hood tradition is an especially useful doorway into this subject, but it must not be mistaken for a transparent historical biography. The legendary outlaw is a layered cultural figure whose stories have been retold and transformed. Historical outlaws, popular rebels, forest communities, and political resistance movements deserve their own evidence-based treatment.

## Essential distinctions

- **Outlawry:** a legal or social status of exclusion from ordinary protection; its meaning varies by jurisdiction and period.
- **Banditry and brigandage:** predatory taking by armed groups, often along roads, borders, rural districts, or weakly governed regions.
- **Social banditry:** a scholarly lens for cases where communities interpret a bandit as resisting an oppressive order. It is not a label to apply automatically.
- **Popular resistance:** collective action against a policy, authority, occupation, extraction regime, or threat to local life; it may be armed or unarmed.
- **Rebellion:** organized opposition to an established authority, with aims ranging from policy change to regime replacement or independence.
- **Insurgency:** sustained organized challenge to an authority's control or legitimacy, often involving political mobilization and irregular force.
- **Self-defense and local protection:** community efforts to protect people and resources; these can become coercive or predatory and require scrutiny.

These categories can overlap but are not synonyms. A group may describe itself as resistance while its actions harm the population it claims to defend. A government may call opponents bandits to deny political standing. The book should record competing descriptions and assess evidence.

## Conditions and relationships

Outlaw groups emerge in varied contexts: weak or fragmented authority, contested frontiers, war disruption, land enclosure, taxation, debt, famine, forced displacement, corrupt local administration, and networks of patronage. None of these conditions mechanically produces banditry or rebellion. People make choices within constraints, and local histories differ.

The relationship between an armed group and nearby communities is central. Consider:
- who provides food, shelter, information, recruits, or silence;
- whether support is voluntary, negotiated, coerced, or extracted;
- who benefits from captured wealth;
- how disputes are resolved;
- whether the group protects or exploits vulnerable people;
- what happens when the group becomes a governing authority.

A group's legitimacy is not established by its rhetoric or by a romantic ballad. It must be studied through conduct, constituency, alternatives, and the voices of those affected.

## The Robin Hood problem

Robin Hood is best approached through layers: medieval literary traditions; later ballads and theatrical adaptations; political uses of the outlaw image; modern popular culture; and historical individuals sometimes proposed as inspirations. The existence of historical outlaws does not prove the legendary narrative happened. Nor should one modern version be treated as the definitive character.

For original settings, a Robin Hood-like figure can be designed as a local protector, a dispossessed noble, a rebel organizer, a smuggler, a predatory gang leader, or a symbol claimed by several factions. Let the evidence in the fictional world determine which interpretation is persuasive.

## Research method

Use court rolls, legal statutes, petitions, tax and land records, local chronicles, oral traditions, ballads, and material evidence with care. Legal records show what authorities prosecuted and how they categorized people; they do not provide a neutral census of all violence. Folklore reveals social hopes, fears, and political memory, not necessarily factual events.

Avoid treating “the people” as one voice. Villagers, landlords, laborers, merchants, clergy, travelers, women, children, and local officials may experience the same group differently.

## Tabletop and simulation use

Strong scenarios place players inside conflicting obligations rather than a simple good-versus-evil contest: a village asked to shelter fugitives; a road network threatened by predation; an unpopular tax collector; a rebel group that needs food; a local leader negotiating amnesty; or a legendary outlaw whose deeds are being appropriated by rival movements.

Track legitimacy, fear, material support, coercion, information, faction cohesion, civilian safety, and political outcomes. Do not reward violence as the only meaningful form of agency. Negotiation, exposure, sanctuary, reform, escape, and community organization can all change the situation.

## Editorial rule

Never romanticize predation, flatten a community into a faction, or treat a state's label as the final truth. Distinguish documented history, scholarly interpretation, folklore, and original fiction at every stage.
