## Scope, ownership, and evidence labels

This is a raWWar-owned LOD 2 domain manual. It treats medicine as a human, institutional, scientific, ethical, logistical, and narrative domain—not merely a list of healing items or a way to restore hit points. Its scope runs from ancient healing traditions through modern trauma systems and into carefully labeled future possibilities. It provides a shared foundation for a planned series of focused histories, battlefield medicine volumes, fantasy healing design, and future medical systems.

This book is educational and game-design material, not clinical training, a treatment protocol, or a substitute for emergency services. Real medical decisions and procedures require qualified professionals, appropriate equipment, local protocols, and current clinical guidance. Operational descriptions are intentionally kept at a conceptual level where procedural detail could cause harm.

Use four labels for claims about technology and the future:
- **Established:** supported by current evidence, accepted clinical practice, or deployed systems. Historical claims still require source criticism.
- **Extrapolated:** a plausible extension of established science or engineering, with explicit uncertainty.
- **Speculative:** depends on unresolved science, engineering, economics, regulation, or social acceptance.
- **Fictional:** a rule invented for a particular setting or game.

## 1. What medicine is

Medicine is the organized attempt to understand, prevent, recognize, relieve, and treat injury and illness. Healing is broader: it includes biological recovery, psychological recovery, social support, spiritual meaning, rehabilitation, adaptation, and the restoration of a person's place in a community. These concepts overlap, but they are not interchangeable. A wound may close while pain, disability, grief, or social exclusion remain.

A useful model separates several interacting domains:

1. **Prevention:** reducing exposure to disease and injury through sanitation, vaccination, protective equipment, training, environmental design, and public policy.
2. **Recognition:** noticing signs, gathering a history, examining a patient, measuring physiology, and distinguishing observation from inference.
3. **Stabilization:** protecting immediate life functions and preventing a worsening condition while definitive care is arranged.
4. **Definitive treatment:** addressing the underlying injury or disease with appropriate medical, surgical, pharmacological, or supportive care.
5. **Recovery and rehabilitation:** restoring function where possible, adapting to lasting changes, managing pain, and supporting mental health.
6. **Palliation and end-of-life care:** relieving suffering and respecting the patient when cure is not possible or is not the chosen goal.
7. **Public and community health:** protecting populations through surveillance, clean water, food safety, infection control, education, and equitable access.

The medic is not simply the person with the largest healing statistic. A medic recognizes uncertainty, communicates clearly, allocates scarce resources fairly, protects patient dignity, and knows when a situation exceeds their training or equipment.

## 2. The long history of healing

There is no single straight line from superstition to modern science. Medical traditions developed in many places, sometimes independently, sometimes through exchange, and often with mixtures of observation, inherited theory, spiritual practice, practical craft, and political authority. A modern historian should avoid judging every earlier practitioner as either foolish or secretly modern. People worked with the knowledge, institutions, materials, and explanations available to them.

### Prehistoric and early community care

Human care predates written medical texts. Evidence can include healed fractures, dental wear, skeletal disease, trepanation, burial practices, and archaeological traces of plant use. Such evidence can show survival after injury or sustained care, but it rarely reveals exactly who treated whom or what they believed. Do not infer a complete medical system from one healed skeleton.

Care was embedded in families and communities. Birth, injury, infection, disability, aging, and death were social experiences as well as bodily events. Knowledge could be held by caregivers, midwives, healers, bone-setters, herbal specialists, and religious authorities without fitting modern professional categories.

### Ancient urban civilizations

Written medical traditions appear in multiple regions, including Mesopotamia, Egypt, South Asia, China, and the Mediterranean. Their surviving records vary greatly in purpose and reliability: recipes, case descriptions, legal texts, ritual instructions, anatomical observations, and later compilations are not equivalent kinds of evidence.

Ancient medicine combined practical observation with cosmology, religion, humoral or other explanatory theories, and the limits of available instruments. Surgery, wound care, dentistry, obstetrics, pharmacology, and public sanitation developed unevenly. A game can represent different schools and practitioners without making any civilization a monolithic medical culture.

### Classical and late antique traditions

Greek and Roman medical writing shaped later Mediterranean and European traditions, but neither society had one uniform medicine. Hippocratic texts are a collection, not the record of one unchanging authorial voice. Galenic medicine became influential through later transmission and adaptation. Roman military and civic systems supported varied forms of care, but claims about standardized hospitals or universal soldier treatment need careful qualification.

Medical ideas also moved through translation, commentary, trade, conquest, and scholarly exchange. The history is not a simple relay in which one civilization hands knowledge to the next unchanged.

### South, Central, and East Asian traditions

Ayurvedic, Chinese, Tibetan, Persianate, and other medical traditions developed complex bodies of theory, diagnosis, materia medica, and practical care. They changed over time and differed by region, school, practitioner, and institution. Avoid treating any of them as a timeless, uniform system or reducing them to a handful of modern stereotypes.

The movement of texts and practitioners across languages and political borders matters. Translation can preserve, transform, or distort technical concepts. A sourcebook should identify the period and tradition being represented rather than using a broad cultural label as if it were a complete clinical description.

### Medieval and early modern medicine

Hospitals, charitable institutions, religious orders, universities, courts, urban authorities, military organizations, and household practitioners all contributed to care in different regions. Their purposes could include hospitality, isolation, long-term support, teaching, treatment, or the care of particular groups. The modern hospital should not be projected backward onto every historical institution.

The printing press and expanding scholarly networks altered the circulation of medical knowledge. Anatomy, surgery, pharmacology, public health, and professional organization changed through observation, dispute, institutional reform, and new instruments. Progress was neither uniform nor inevitable.

### The scientific and industrial transformations

The development of experimental physiology, pathology, microscopy, anesthesia, antisepsis, germ theory, vaccination, radiography, blood transfusion, and modern nursing transformed many forms of care. These developments occurred at different times and through contested institutions. They also depended on manufacturing, transport, training, records, and public trust.

Industrial warfare exposed the consequences of mass casualties, infection, shock, evacuation delays, and the need for organized medical services. It also created ethical failures and abuses. A historically grounded game should show both the emergence of capabilities and the human costs of the systems that produced them.

### Twentieth-century and contemporary medicine

Modern medicine is a network of specialties and institutions: emergency medicine, trauma surgery, anesthesia, intensive care, infectious disease, rehabilitation, mental health, nursing, public health, medical imaging, laboratory medicine, and many more. Outcomes depend not only on a clever clinician but also on supply chains, staffing, clean facilities, blood products, communications, transport, and follow-up.

Contemporary care is powerful but not omnipotent. Time matters, resources can be unavailable, diagnoses can be uncertain, interventions carry risks, and survival does not guarantee recovery without lasting impairment.

## 3. Medicine in war: from camp followers to trauma systems

Military medicine changes with the organization of armies, the weapons and environments they face, the state of medical knowledge, and the ability to move casualties. There is no universal historical pattern. Some forces developed formal medical organizations; others relied heavily on unit members, household networks, religious institutions, local communities, or ad hoc arrangements.

### Before modern evacuation systems

In many historical settings, the wounded depended on comrades, attendants, local healers, household care, religious institutions, and whatever transport was available. The ability to retrieve someone from a battlefield could be as important as the skill of the practitioner. Weather, terrain, enemy control, distance, status, and social relationships influenced who received care.

Historical battlefield treatment could include wound cleaning, dressing, splinting, attempts to control bleeding, removal of foreign material, and surgery. Knowledge and outcomes varied widely. Do not depict historical surgeons as uniformly incompetent, nor romanticize procedures that carried severe risks in the absence of anesthesia, antisepsis, transfusion, or modern infection control.

### Early modern armies and professionalization

Standing forces and expanding state institutions created new demands for surgeons, hospitals, supply systems, records, and evacuation. Military medical organizations developed unevenly. Naval medicine faced confinement, long voyages, scurvy and other diseases, and limited opportunities for evacuation. Campaign medicine was constrained by transport, water, sanitation, weather, and the number of patients.

### Nineteenth- and twentieth-century transformation

Railways, motor transport, communications, anesthesia, antisepsis, blood transfusion, antibiotics, organized nursing, and improved surgical techniques changed the possibilities of casualty care. The world wars accelerated both medical innovation and mass institutional capacity, while also demonstrating the scale of preventable suffering.

Modern combat casualty systems use a chain of care: immediate aid, collection and movement, forward treatment, evacuation, definitive treatment, and rehabilitation. Exact names and procedures vary by country, service, conflict, and current doctrine. For game design, model the chain and its bottlenecks rather than copying a single real military protocol.

### The casualty chain

A casualty may pass through several connected layers:

- **Point of injury:** the first moments, where danger, cover, communication, and immediate recognition dominate.
- **Buddy or self-aid:** limited actions by the casualty or nearby personnel, constrained by training, consciousness, equipment, and threat.
- **Combat medic or first responder:** rapid assessment, immediate priorities, documentation, and coordination.
- **Casualty collection point:** a temporary location for sorting, reassessment, and onward movement.
- **Forward medical facility:** more capable stabilization and treatment close enough to support operations.
- **Evacuation platform:** ground, air, water, or future transport with its own capacity, exposure, and delay.
- **Definitive care:** surgical, intensive, specialist, or other hospital-level treatment.
- **Rehabilitation and return:** physical and psychological recovery, assistive technology, changed duties, or transition out of service.

The chain is only as strong as its connections. A highly skilled medic cannot compensate indefinitely for a blocked evacuation route, absent blood supply, broken communications, contaminated water, exhausted staff, or a facility without power.

## 4. Triage: decisions under scarcity

Triage is a structured way to prioritize assessment and treatment when needs exceed immediately available resources. It is not a declaration of whose life matters. It is a context-dependent allocation decision made under uncertainty, using defined protocols and repeated reassessment.

A game should represent triage as a changing state, not a permanent label. A patient can deteriorate, improve, reveal new information, or become unreachable. A HUD can support the medic, but it must not pretend to know facts it has not measured.

### A readable triage model

For game purposes, use a small set of clearly explained states. The labels below are illustrative game vocabulary, not a replacement for any real triage system:

- **Immediate:** a time-critical threat appears treatable with resources currently available.
- **Urgent:** serious injury or illness needs prompt care, but the immediate threat is less acute.
- **Delayed:** care can safely wait for a defined interval only while the patient remains stable and is reassessed.
- **Expectant / resource-limited:** survival is unlikely with the resources currently available, or treatment demand exceeds capacity. This status requires strict governance, repeated review, and humane care; it must never mean abandonment.
- **Deceased / no signs of life:** a status requiring appropriate assessment under the applicable rules and context. In a game, distinguish confirmed death from an uncertain or potentially reversible state.

Do not make color alone carry meaning. Use text, symbols, confidence, timestamps, and accessible contrast. Color conventions differ among systems and can be inaccessible to some players.

### What the HUD should show

A useful medic HUD can organize information into layers:

1. **Identity and consent:** known identity, pronouns or preferred form of address where known, medical directives, allergies, and consent status. In combat, identity may be uncertain or unavailable.
2. **Observed condition:** visible bleeding, responsiveness, breathing observations, movement, burns, wounds, and other directly observed signs.
3. **Measured data:** pulse or heart-rate estimate, oxygen saturation, blood pressure, temperature, ECG interpretation, and sensor quality—only when the device actually has a functioning sensor.
4. **Inferred risks:** suspected shock, internal injury, airway risk, infection, crush injury, or other concerns, each marked as an inference with confidence and supporting observations.
5. **Immediate priorities:** a concise queue of time-critical concerns, contraindications, and reassessment prompts.
6. **Treatment history:** who did what, when, with which device or medication, and what changed afterward.
7. **Evacuation and destination:** transport availability, travel time estimate, receiving facility capacity, and handoff status.
8. **Uncertainty and failure:** sensor dropout, conflicting readings, stale data, damaged equipment, unknown patient history, and model confidence.

The HUD is decision support, not an oracle. It must make uncertainty visible and allow the medic to challenge an automated recommendation. A damaged sensor should not silently generate a plausible-looking vital sign.

### Triage as a playable decision

A compelling triage encounter asks the player to balance urgency, reversibility, available skill, equipment, transport, and the needs of other casualties. The player should see why a decision was made and what changed afterward. Reward accurate reassessment and communication, not merely the number of interventions performed.

Do not reward a player for withholding care from a group based on identity, faction, wealth, citizenship, or narrative importance. If a setting explores discriminatory medical institutions, make that an explicit ethical and political subject rather than a hidden scoring rule.

## 5. Bleeding, mangled limbs, and the hypersonic scalpel

Severe limb trauma is a dramatic and historically important battlefield problem. It is also a place where games can easily teach dangerous misconceptions. Real-world hemorrhage control, tourniquet use, wound management, and amputation require appropriate training and current professional protocols. This book does not provide a procedural guide.

### Tourniquets and hemorrhage control

**Established:** modern emergency and combat-care systems recognize that catastrophic external bleeding from an extremity can be rapidly fatal, and trained responders may use purpose-designed tourniquets as part of an appropriate protocol. A tourniquet is not a generic bandage, not a treatment for every wound, and not a device to apply casually. It has indications, risks, documentation requirements, and a need for reassessment and definitive care.

For game rules, model:
- whether the bleeding is life-threatening and externally accessible;
- whether the injury is in a location where the selected device is appropriate;
- whether the character has a suitable device and qualification;
- whether the intervention is effective, incomplete, or uncertain;
- the elapsed time and the need for transfer to definitive care;
- complications and later reassessment as abstract clinical states, not a step-by-step procedure.

A medic should not be forced to choose between “do nothing” and “amputate.” Most severe limb injuries are not automatic indications for amputation.

### Amputation as a rare, consequential event

Historically, battlefield amputation has been associated with catastrophic injury, severe infection, and attempts to save a life when other options were unavailable. Outcomes depended heavily on the era, injury, available anesthesia, infection control, surgical skill, and later care. It was never a consequence-free shortcut.

In a modern or future setting, emergency amputation outside a definitive medical facility should be an exceptional fictional scenario, not a routine combat action. A game may represent an irretrievably destroyed limb, an unstable casualty, and an urgent decision to preserve life, but should avoid turning the act into a simple animation or an easily repeatable player ability. The character's consent where possible, command pressure, available expertise, pain management, blood loss, infection risk, later surgery, prosthetics, rehabilitation, and psychological impact all matter.

### The hypersonic scalpel

**Fictional raWWar equipment concept:** the medic carries a hypersonic scalpel—an advanced instrument whose working edge moves or vibrates at extraordinary frequency and is engineered for highly controlled cutting. Its defining game property is not simply “sharpness”; it is controlled interaction with tissue and materials.

The phrase “so sharp you can't feel it cut” is a setting claim, not a biological guarantee. A cutting edge does not automatically prevent pain. Pain depends on nerve activation, tissue damage, anesthesia, shock, awareness, and many other factors. The scalpel must not be described as inherently painless or as a substitute for anesthesia, analgesia, sterile technique, surgical judgment, or bleeding control.

Give the tool constraints that make it believable:
- It requires power, calibration, maintenance, and intact safety interlocks.
- It can fail, overheat, lose calibration, or be unsuitable for a particular material or tissue.
- Its cutting performance does not confer diagnostic ability.
- It cannot reverse crush damage, restore severed nerves, replace lost blood, or make a nonviable limb viable.
- It is not a magic universal disassembler; its use is governed by qualification, consent, clinical indication, and setting law.
- The game should abstract any emergency surgical use rather than teaching real-world cutting or amputation technique.

A compelling mechanic is a **precision budget**: the device can offer extraordinary control when its sensors, calibration, power, and operator qualification are all intact. Damage or haste reduces confidence and increases risk. This is a game abstraction, not a clinical performance claim.

## 6. The heart, CPR, defibrillation, and the medic's battery

A common fictional trope treats a heart as a motor that can be restarted by applying electricity. Real physiology is more complicated.

**Established:** CPR is a set of emergency measures intended to maintain some circulation when the heart is not pumping effectively. It does not itself “charge” the heart. Defibrillation delivers a controlled electrical shock for certain dangerous shockable cardiac rhythms; it is not appropriate for every cardiac arrest rhythm. A flatline (asystole) is not ordinarily corrected by delivering a defibrillation shock. A patient who has no pulse or signs of life requires urgent assessment and care under the applicable emergency protocol.

A game should distinguish:
- **Circulatory support:** interventions that help maintain blood flow.
- **Rhythm assessment:** determining what electrical activity is present, if reliable monitoring is available.
- **Defibrillation:** treatment for eligible shockable rhythms, not a universal restart button.
- **Pacing:** a different intervention intended to provide electrical impulses in selected situations; it is not synonymous with defibrillation.
- **Reversible causes:** underlying problems that may need treatment; electricity alone cannot correct them all.
- **Death determination:** a clinical and legal process that varies by jurisdiction and circumstances, not a visual effect or a single HUD icon.

### The medic's battery

**Fictional raWWar equipment concept:** a medic's power cell supports the HUD, monitoring, powered instruments, communications, and approved medical devices. It may connect to a dedicated, regulated defibrillator or pacing module where the setting's technology and medical rules permit. It must not be portrayed as a bare battery that a medic touches to a casualty to “power the heart.”

Give the battery meaningful trade-offs:
- life-support and monitoring reserve versus tool use;
- emergency device charge versus communications and HUD uptime;
- damaged or depleted cells;
- verified device compatibility and safety interlocks;
- limited energy under cold, heat, water exposure, or battle damage;
- a clear distinction between a powered medical device and improvised electrical contact.

A dramatic scene can involve a marine with no detectable pulse, a medic checking the monitor, another character maintaining support, and the device reporting an uncertain or non-shockable rhythm. The challenge is not to press a dramatic shock button; it is to recognize what the system can and cannot do, address the situation within training, and coordinate the next step. In a fictional future, advanced devices may add capabilities, but those capabilities must be labeled and bounded.

## 7. Injury, illness, and the patient as a whole

Combat trauma is not limited to visible wounds. A credible system includes:
- blunt and penetrating trauma;
- burns, blast effects, and environmental exposure;
- fractures, crush injury, and compartment-related risks;
- blood loss and circulatory shock;
- airway and breathing compromise;
- traumatic brain and spinal injury;
- eye, ear, and sensory injury;
- poisoning, infection, and disease;
- dehydration, heat illness, cold injury, and exhaustion;
- psychological trauma, acute stress, grief, and moral injury;
- chronic pain, disability, and long-term rehabilitation.

A wound's severity is not always visible. A character may look mobile while suffering a serious internal injury, or may be unable to fight while remaining medically stable. Avoid making pain, disability, or visible disfigurement a shorthand for weakness or moral status.

### Infection and the invisible campaign

Before modern infection control and antimicrobial medicine, a wound that appeared survivable could later become life-threatening. Modern medicine has changed the risk but has not eliminated infection, antimicrobial resistance, or the need for clean environments and follow-up. In isolated habitats, ships, frontier bases, and long campaigns, infection prevention and supply continuity can be strategically important.

### Psychological and social recovery

Survival is not the end of a medical story. Patients may experience grief, fear, sleep disruption, chronic pain, disability, altered identity, and difficulty returning to former roles. Teammates and medics can also be affected by repeated exposure to casualties and moral injury. A humane game gives characters time, relationships, accommodations, and meaningful choices during recovery.

## 8. Medical roles, qualifications, and institutional trust

A medic role should be qualification-driven. A soldier with a basic first-aid qualification, a combat medic, a nurse, a physician, a surgeon, a specialist technician, and a public-health officer do not have interchangeable scope or authority.

Model:
- training and currency of qualification;
- scope of practice and supervision;
- equipment authorization;
- familiarity with the patient's species, physiology, or implants;
- ability to communicate with the patient;
- medical command and escalation;
- documentation and handoff;
- duty of care and professional ethics.

In a multi-species or posthuman setting, compatibility matters. A device calibrated for one physiology may be dangerous for another. A HUD should identify unknown biology rather than silently assume that all patients share human anatomy.

Medical institutions are political institutions as well as technical ones. Who receives care? Who controls the hospital? Can neutral medical teams move across front lines? Are medical transports protected? Can a patient refuse treatment? How are prisoners and civilians treated? How does a force investigate medical misconduct? These questions belong in raWWar's warfare domain.

International humanitarian law provides protections for wounded and sick persons, medical personnel, medical units, and transports under applicable conditions. The precise legal status depends on the conflict, parties, facts, and applicable treaty and customary law. A game should not imply that a medical badge grants unlimited immunity or that every real-world context has identical rules. Legal detail belongs in a dedicated law-of-war sourcebook and should be checked against authoritative current sources.

## 9. Fantasy healing: magic is a setting rule, not a medical fact

Fantasy healing can be wondrous without erasing consequences. The designer's first decision is what healing magic actually changes. Does it accelerate natural repair, replace lost tissue, restore function, remove infection, reverse death, transfer injury, or alter the soul? Each capability creates different social, military, economic, and ethical consequences.

### Five useful models of healing magic

1. **Acceleration:** magic speeds a process the body could already perform. It may not restore missing structures or overcome every disease.
2. **Reconstruction:** magic rebuilds tissue or organs. Define what information, material, energy, and time it requires.
3. **Purification:** magic removes a poison, infection, curse, or foreign influence. Define how it distinguishes harmful from beneficial processes.
4. **Transfer:** injury or suffering is moved to another person, object, or place. This creates consent, exploitation, and political questions.
5. **Restoration:** magic returns a person to an earlier bodily state. Define which state, how memories and identity persist, and what happens to aging, disease, implants, and prior adaptations.

### The resurrection boundary

Resurrection is not just a stronger healing spell. It changes mortality, inheritance, warfare, justice, religion, family, population, and the meaning of sacrifice. If resurrection is common, why do rulers die? Can a person refuse return? Is the restored person continuous with the deceased? What happens when the body is missing, memory is incomplete, or several institutions claim authority?

A setting that includes resurrection should establish limits, costs, failure modes, consent rules, and social consequences before treating it as an ordinary combat consumable.

### Fantasy healing and gameplay balance

Avoid a simplistic ladder in which a low-level healer closes scratches and a high-level healer erases every consequence. Distinct capabilities create better choices:
- stabilization can buy time without completing recovery;
- healing can restore function but require rest;
- cleansing can address a specific cause without repairing damage;
- reconstruction can restore a lost structure but demand rare resources;
- resurrection can carry profound limits and consequences.

Magic can coexist with mundane medicine. A healer may need diagnosis, anatomy, sterile tools, patient history, and rehabilitation even when magic is available. The existence of a spell does not automatically imply universal access, perfect knowledge, or unlimited capacity.

## 10. Future medicine: plausible extensions and fictional breakthroughs

Future medicine should be designed from constraints, not from a list of miraculous gadgets. A battlefield medic in a powered suit, a deep-space habitat, or an alien ocean settlement still depends on energy, materials, diagnostics, training, maintenance, communication, and a receiving system capable of continuing care.

### Near-future and extrapolated systems

**Extrapolated:** more capable wearable monitoring, decision-support systems, robotic assistance, telemedicine, advanced prosthetics, improved wound materials, personalized diagnostics, and more integrated medical records may extend current directions. Their usefulness depends on validation, privacy, interoperability, reliability, and clinician oversight.

**Speculative:** rapid whole-body repair, reliable regeneration of complex organs, routine reversal of severe brain injury, or universal diagnosis from a handheld scanner require breakthroughs and should not be presented as inevitable.

**Fictional:** a hypersonic scalpel with extraordinary controlled cutting performance, autonomous trauma pods, cellular reconstruction fields, or reliable battlefield revival can exist in raWWar's future—but the setting must define what they can do, what they cannot do, and what they cost.

### The autonomous medical assistant

An AI assistant may summarize observations, flag trends, translate, check inventory, maintain records, or recommend a next assessment. It should disclose uncertainty, sensor provenance, model limitations, and the source of its recommendation. It should not silently convert an uncertain inference into a diagnosis or make unreviewable decisions about who receives care.

If an autonomous system can initiate treatment, the setting must define its authorization, scope, emergency limits, audit trail, fail-safe behavior, and accountability. See [Autonomous Systems and Machine Agency](autonomous-systems-and-machine-agency.md).

### Medicine away from a planetary hospital

A ship or remote base faces delayed evacuation, limited storage, constrained power, radiation or environmental exposure, supply shortages, and small teams with competing duties. Medical planning becomes a logistics and governance problem:
- Which capabilities are carried, and why?
- How are supplies stored, tracked, rotated, and replenished?
- What happens when the only trained clinician becomes a patient?
- How does a crew support long-term disability or chronic illness?
- Can a remote specialist help when communication is delayed or unavailable?
- What medical services are guaranteed to civilians, contractors, prisoners, and visitors?

A credible future medic is a member of a larger care network, even when that network is distant or damaged.

## 11. A playable medic system for raWWar

The medic role should be mechanically distinct from the combat role while remaining part of the same squad and campaign. It should reward observation, prioritization, communication, and judgment rather than encourage players to perform every procedure as a rapid button sequence.

### The encounter loop

1. **Secure and observe:** establish whether the scene can be approached and identify immediate environmental hazards.
2. **Identify:** confirm who the casualty is, what is known, and what remains uncertain.
3. **Assess:** gather observations and sensor readings, checking their quality and age.
4. **Prioritize:** assign a provisional triage state and identify time-critical concerns.
5. **Intervene:** choose an authorized action supported by the medic's qualification, available equipment, and the setting's clinical model.
6. **Reassess:** compare the patient's state with the prior state; do not assume an action succeeded because an animation completed.
7. **Coordinate:** request help, transport, supplies, or specialist advice; communicate priorities to the squad.
8. **Handoff:** transfer identity, findings, actions, response, uncertainty, and outstanding risks to the next care team.
9. **Follow through:** track recovery, rehabilitation, return to duty, long-term disability, or death with dignity and narrative consequence.

### The HUD as a living picture

The HUD should show a compact casualty card and allow deeper inspection:
- patient identity and affiliation, if known;
- injury/illness observations;
- vital-sign trends with source and confidence;
- provisional triage status;
- active concerns and time since last reassessment;
- treatment and device history;
- medic qualifications and scope;
- inventory, battery reserve, and device condition;
- transport status and receiving capacity;
- patient consent or known directives;
- uncertainty, conflicting signals, and unavailable data.

The HUD should never label a patient “dead” solely because a sensor has failed. Use states such as **unassessed**, **assessment uncertain**, **critical**, **under treatment**, **awaiting evacuation**, **handoff complete**, and **death confirmed under setting rules**. The exact state machine can be developed with raWWar's broader systems architecture.

### A simple game-state model

Each casualty can be represented by:
- **Threats:** a set of active medical problems, each with severity, urgency, and uncertainty.
- **Stability:** a changing summary of whether the patient is improving, holding, or deteriorating.
- **Function:** mobility, awareness, sensory capability, and other relevant capabilities.
- **Resources:** time, supplies, device charge, transport, and qualified personnel.
- **Care history:** interventions, observations, reassessments, and handoffs.
- **Recovery outlook:** likely short-term and long-term outcomes, with uncertainty.

Do not collapse all of this into one health number. A single number cannot distinguish a stable fracture from a rapidly worsening airway problem, or a patient who is physically stable but psychologically overwhelmed.

### Progression and meaningful expertise

Medic progression can unlock:
- better observation and more reliable interpretation;
- expanded qualifications and authorized equipment;
- improved triage coordination;
- specialist knowledge for burns, trauma, infectious disease, prosthetics, or alien physiology;
- faster and more reliable handoffs;
- leadership of a casualty collection point or medical facility;
- research and adaptation of equipment to new environments.

Progression should not make a medic omniscient or turn every patient into a guaranteed success. Expertise improves recognition and decision quality; it does not repeal physiology, uncertainty, or resource limits.

## 12. Scenario patterns

### The broken evacuation chain

A squad has several casualties, but the route to the forward facility is blocked. The medic must assess, reassess, communicate changing priorities, and coordinate a safe movement plan. The challenge is the system around the medic, not a sequence of graphic procedures.

### The silent monitor

A casualty's HUD reports no reliable pulse. The player discovers that the sensor is damaged and must distinguish missing data from a confirmed clinical state. A second source of assessment changes the decision. This scenario teaches that interface certainty and clinical certainty are not the same.

### One battery, several needs

The medic's power cell is low. The HUD, communications, powered instrument, and a dedicated medical device all compete for reserve. The player must understand device priority, ask for support, and avoid treating the battery as an unlimited source of miraculous treatment.

### The mangled limb

A casualty has catastrophic limb trauma. The player must recognize severe bleeding, use appropriate authorized equipment within the setting's rules, document the intervention, arrange evacuation, and confront the possibility of permanent impairment. Emergency amputation is not a default option and should only appear as a rare, exceptional scenario governed by the setting's explicit clinical and ethical assumptions.

### The civilian clinic

A combat unit reaches a settlement where civilians, prisoners, and combatants all need care. Medical supplies are limited, local clinicians possess knowledge the squad lacks, and command pressure conflicts with impartial care. The outcome depends on trust and cooperation as much as equipment.

### The returning medic

After a campaign, the medic must deal with fatigue, grief, moral injury, and a patient who cannot return to the role they held before the battle. The scenario tests whether the game recognizes care as a continuing responsibility rather than a temporary combat buff.

## 13. Design principles and anti-patterns

Prefer:
- a care chain over an isolated healing animation;
- visible uncertainty over omniscient HUD labels;
- distinct qualifications over universal medic abilities;
- stabilization, definitive treatment, and recovery as different states;
- meaningful scarcity over arbitrary cooldowns;
- patient dignity and consent over disposable casualty tokens;
- noncombat medical institutions as active world actors;
- evidence labels and explicit limits for future technology;
- disability, prosthetics, and rehabilitation as lived futures rather than failure screens.

Avoid:
- treating every injury as a hit-point subtraction;
- equating electricity with restarting any stopped heart;
- making a scalpel inherently painless;
- presenting amputation as routine or consequence-free;
- implying that a single scan can diagnose every injury;
- allowing an AI or HUD to hide uncertainty;
- treating resurrection as a costless consumable;
- rewarding discrimination in triage;
- depicting historical medicine as uniformly ignorant or modern medicine as infallible.

## 14. Research and source discipline

This volume is a domain framework, not a fully referenced medical history. Future focused volumes must use current, authoritative sources for the specific period and clinical subject. Separate primary historical evidence, later medical histories, current professional guidance, and fictional design choices.

Research should include, as appropriate:
- archaeological and bioarchaeological studies;
- critical editions and translations of historical medical texts;
- histories of hospitals, nursing, surgery, public health, and military medicine;
- official military medical histories and current doctrine, with national and temporal scope stated;
- professional clinical guidelines and emergency-care organizations;
- international humanitarian law and authoritative legal commentary;
- disability history, patient narratives, and rehabilitation research;
- peer-reviewed work on medical devices, robotics, AI, and future biotechnology.

Do not copy modern translations, copyrighted clinical manuals, or proprietary training materials into the sourcebook. Summarize in original language and cite the specific edition or guideline used. Clinical guidance changes; any real-world factual detail intended for implementation must be rechecked against current authoritative sources.

## 15. Planned medicine sourcebook series

This foundation is the entry point, not the final word. Expand into focused volumes with their own descriptors, covers, bibliographies, and cross-links:

1. **Healing Across the Centuries:** a global, periodized history of medicine, caregiving, surgery, public health, hospitals, and changing explanations of disease.
2. **Battlefield Medicine and the Casualty Chain:** military medical organizations, evacuation, triage history, trauma systems, and the changing relationship between combat and care.
3. **Surgery, Amputation, and Prosthetic Futures:** historical surgery, anesthesia, infection control, limb loss, prosthetics, rehabilitation, and future restoration.
4. **Medicine at Sea, in Space, and on the Frontier:** naval medicine, remote settlements, ships, habitats, delayed evacuation, and constrained supply.
5. **Fantasy Healing and the Laws of Restoration:** magic systems, resurrection, healing economies, consent, limits, and worldbuilding consequences.
6. **Future Medicine and Human-Machine Care:** robotics, AI decision support, regenerative medicine, synthetic organs, autonomous medical facilities, and explicit evidence labels.
7. **Medical Ethics, Law, and Protected Care:** neutrality, impartiality, consent, prisoners, civilians, medical personnel, records, and accountability.
8. **Alien and Posthuman Physiology:** cross-species diagnosis, incompatible anatomy, unknown pathogens, augmented bodies, and the limits of human assumptions.

The first follow-up should be *Healing Across the Centuries*, with separate regional and period studies rather than a single supposedly universal history. The second should be *Battlefield Medicine and the Casualty Chain*, which can deepen the player medic role and the HUD design.

## 16. Glossary

- **Casualty:** a person injured, ill, missing, or otherwise affected in a conflict context; usage varies by institution.
- **Definitive care:** treatment intended to address the underlying condition beyond immediate stabilization.
- **Evacuation:** movement of a patient to a facility or level of care better able to meet their needs.
- **Handoff:** transfer of responsibility and relevant patient information between caregivers.
- **Hemorrhage:** blood loss; severity depends on location, rate, physiology, and context.
- **Palliation:** care intended to relieve suffering and improve quality of life.
- **Rehabilitation:** support to restore or adapt function and participation after illness or injury.
- **Stabilization:** measures intended to reduce immediate danger or prevent deterioration while further care is arranged.
- **Triage:** prioritization of patients when needs exceed immediately available resources; it is dynamic and context-dependent.
