> Mounted formations are relationships among riders, mounts, equipment, terrain, pace, and command—not infantry shapes with animals attached.

A reusable LOD 2 manual for mounted forces across historical, contemporary ceremonial, roleplaying, and speculative settings. It distinguishes cavalry, mounted infantry, couriers, scouts, pack and draft animals, chariot forces, and other mounted organizations rather than treating them as one universal arm. This is historical and simulation design material, not riding, animal-handling, or military training.

## 1. Scope and classification

“Mounted” describes a relationship between a person and an animal or conveyance. “Cavalry” describes a military role or institution whose meaning changes by period and region. Mounted infantry use mounts primarily to move and dismount to fight; cavalry may fight from the mount, dismount, or combine methods. Scouts, messengers, baggage handlers, artillery teams, pack trains, and draft animals may be mounted or animal-supported without being cavalry.

A useful record distinguishes:
- primary mission and expected mode of contact;
- rider, mount, equipment, and support organization;
- whether the mount is ridden, driven, led, or used as a pack animal;
- training, remount, veterinary, and replacement systems;
- legal status, recruitment, ownership, and social obligations;
- the historical source and uncertainty behind any reconstruction.

Do not map modern categories backward without qualification. Terms such as horse archer, cataphract, dragoon, hussar, lancer, and mounted rifleman belong to particular institutional and historical contexts.

## 2. The mounted formation as a coupled system

A mounted element has at least two living participants where a rider and mount are distinct. Their condition and behavior are related but not interchangeable. The mount has its own fatigue, health, fear responses, training, needs, and limits; the rider has separate skill, fatigue, injury, judgment, and authority. A formation model that treats the animal as a replaceable vehicle erases a central part of the system.

Represent the formation through:
- rider and mount identities, including their pairing history;
- role, equipment, load, and readiness;
- spacing, orientation, pace, route, and intended transition;
- command relationships and signal reach;
- water, forage, rest, veterinary support, and remount availability;
- environmental conditions and uncertainty;
- dismounted personnel, handlers, and support elements.

A rider may be ready while the mount is not, or vice versa. Pairing changes should be explicit events with welfare and training implications.

## 3. Formation families and their purposes

Mounted formations are selected for a mission and environment, not because a named shape is inherently superior. Common abstract arrangements include column, file, line, echelon, screen, dispersed scouts, reserve, convoy, and mixed mounted/dismounted groups. These are organizational descriptions, not instructions for real-world tactical employment.

Compare each arrangement through frontage, depth, control, visibility, route capacity, separation risk, ability to regroup, and support burden. A narrow route may force a column and create delays. Poor visibility may break visual cohesion. A mixed group may move at the pace of its least mobile essential element.

## 4. Historical variation: from chariots to mounted armies

Chariot warfare, cavalry, mounted archery, armored horsemen, dragoons, and modern mounted patrols are not stages in a single inevitable progression. They reflect different animals, harness and vehicle technologies, terrain, social organization, logistics, weapons, and political economies.

Chariots require suitable terrain, teams, vehicles, maintenance, and specialist crews. Their social and military role varied widely; they should not be represented as simply an early tank. Mounted archery depends on a larger training and remount system, not a magical individual skill. Heavy cavalry's equipment and social organization varied across regions and centuries. Early-modern dragoons illustrate how mounted mobility and dismounted combat could be combined in one institutional role.

## 5. Steppe systems and remount depth

Pastoral and steppe polities could draw on extensive horse knowledge, breeding networks, seasonal mobility, and multiple mounts per rider. These systems should not be reduced to a claim that all steppe peoples fought identically or possessed limitless horses. Access to pasture, water, herd health, political alliances, climate, and campaign duration constrained mounted power.

A simulation should distinguish:
- mounts physically present from mounts fit for a particular task;
- individual remounts from the herd and breeding base;
- local forage from carried feed;
- seasonal pasture access from year-round supply;
- rider skill from institutional capacity to replace losses;
- strategic mobility from the ability to sustain a force at destination.

See the focused Mongol, Parthian, and Sasanian volumes for distinct historical contexts rather than treating “steppe cavalry” as one timeless template.

## 6. Mounts, breeds, and responsible representation

Avoid assigning a single universal “warhorse” profile. Size, temperament, endurance, gait, conformation, training, tack, and suitability varied. Breeding populations were shaped by ecology, trade, selective breeding, conflict, and local preference. Historical labels may not correspond neatly to modern breeds.

When evidence is incomplete, state the uncertainty. Do not turn a breed name into a deterministic stat block or infer a mount's behavior from national or ethnic stereotypes. Fictional species should have explicit biological and ecological rules rather than inheriting every assumption about horses.

## 7. Terrain, weather, and route capacity

Ground conditions can change mounted movement dramatically. Slope, surface firmness, mud, loose stone, vegetation, water crossings, bridges, streets, obstacles, and visibility affect pace, footing, load, and risk. Weather affects both rider and mount. Heat, cold, dehydration, and poor shelter are operational and welfare concerns, not decorative debuffs.

Physics owns authoritative motion, collision, material, and environmental simulation. This book supplies the domain requirements: a route must be evaluated for the actual mount, load, group composition, and conditions. Never assume a path traversable by a person or wheeled vehicle is equally suitable for mounted movement.

## 8. Cohesion, spacing, and communication

Mounted groups need enough space to reduce interference and allow animals to move without constant collision or forced contact. The appropriate spacing depends on conditions and training; the book does not prescribe real-world distances. In a simulation, use scenario-authored tolerances and expose why a formation is constrained.

Signals may use voice, gesture, flags, horns, runners, or communications equipment depending on setting. Sound, weather, darkness, terrain, and animal response affect whether a signal is noticed. Separate order issued, signal received, meaning understood, and action completed. Do not grant the entire formation omniscient awareness.

## 9. Dismounting and mixed-mode formations

Dismounting is a state transition, not a visual toggle. It changes the force's mobility, protection, load distribution, animal handling needs, command structure, and exposure. A mounted element may need designated handlers, secure holding areas, water, and a plan for rejoining. Dismounted personnel should not cause mounts to disappear from the simulation.

Model transitions explicitly: mounted movement, halt, controlled dismount, animal care/holding, dismounted task, regrouping, remount preparation, and renewed movement. Each transition has prerequisites, responsible roles, time, interruption behavior, and failure states. The details should be authored for the fictional or historical scenario, not presented as real training.

## 10. Animal welfare and veterinary support

Animal welfare is part of operational capability and an ethical obligation. Injury, exhaustion, disease, poor shoeing or tack fit, inadequate water, overloading, exposure, and stress can make a mount unfit or cause lasting harm. The system must not reward a player for pushing an animal past safe limits.

A credible force includes handlers, farriers or equivalent specialists, veterinary knowledge, recovery space, feed and water distribution, quarantine where needed, and decisions about withdrawal from service. Veterinary care is not interchangeable with human casualty care; the medical sourcebooks cover human medicine, while animal-specific expertise must be represented separately and competently.

## 11. Logistics: the horse behind the horse

Mounted forces consume more than rider rations. Feed, forage, water, tack, repair materials, grooming supplies, veterinary resources, replacement animals, transport, and trained personnel all constrain operations. Local grazing may reduce carried feed but can damage landscapes, compete with civilian food production, expose animals to disease, and slow movement.

The Art of Resupply should be used to model demand, transport, allocation, and last-mile delivery. Bases and Encampments should represent stabling or picket areas, water access, waste management, veterinary space, feed storage, and protection from weather. A formation's theoretical speed is not its sustainable campaign speed.

## 12. Command, organization, and social institutions

Mounted forces have been organized through state service, aristocratic obligation, tribal and clan networks, paid service, household retinues, conscription, and mixed systems. Do not collapse these into a universal feudal model. Ownership of animals, equipment, and service obligations can shape political power and military readiness.

Command may be distributed among leaders responsible for riders, mounts, scouts, handlers, and support. A mounted unit's cohesion can be affected by kinship, patronage, pay, training, status, and trust—but none should be inferred from ethnicity alone. Model institutions and incentives in their specific setting.

## 13. Mounted formations in a ghost-training system

A raWWar training presentation may render a translucent target arrangement showing assigned slots, route corridors, facing, pace bands, dismount points, and regroup states. The player receives feedback about the task; Gestures supplies reusable embodied movement semantics; Physics evaluates physical motion; raWWar defines mission context and the evaluation rubric.

The player should not be required to imitate a real animal-handling procedure. A game may assess high-level coordination—maintaining a designated position, responding to a signal, recognizing a blocked route, or completing a safe transition—without teaching riding or combat techniques. Accessibility alternatives must exist for haptic, visual, auditory, and motor differences.

## 14. Scoring and simulation contract

Publish scoring dimensions before a drill. Possible dimensions include:
- correct rider/mount pairing and role assignment;
- formation membership and slot occupancy;
- route feasibility and transition completion;
- cohesion and communication acknowledgement;
- load and readiness constraints;
- animal welfare thresholds and timely withdrawal;
- support and resupply continuity;
- uncertainty handling and response to changed conditions.

Do not reward speed at the expense of welfare or safety. If tracking confidence is insufficient, pause or mark the affected measure unscored rather than inventing a failure.

A simulation schema should preserve stable identities, pairing events, observed versus commanded state, confidence, timestamps, condition history, and provenance. Keep authored doctrine separate from physical state and player performance.

## 15. Failure, recovery, and aftermath

Mounts may become separated, frightened, injured, exhausted, ill, or unavailable. Riders may be injured or incapacitated. Routes may close; water and forage may fail; handlers may be lost; a remount system may be depleted. Recovery requires decisions about rest, care, regrouping, evacuation, replacement, and the welfare of animals that cannot return to service.

Do not turn every failure into a combat death. A mount may survive and require long recovery, retire from service, be transferred to civilian care, or remain with a handler. Track the consequences of campaigns on animal populations, local communities, and landscapes.

## 16. Scenario frames

- **Broken road:** a mixed mounted column encounters a route that no longer supports its planned load; players must preserve identity, welfare, and communications while revising the plan.
- **Remount shortage:** an expedition reaches a supply point with fewer fit mounts than expected; explore allocation, recovery, and political obligations.
- **Dismounted interval:** a mounted unit must support a task on foot while its mounts require care and protection.
- **Seasonal pasture dispute:** military demand conflicts with local livelihoods and ecological limits; negotiation may matter more than force.
- **Courier uncertainty:** messages arrive late or contradictory; players must distinguish the order's origin, freshness, and acknowledgement.
- **Fictional xenofauna:** a setting introduces a non-equine mount; the scenario must specify biology, welfare needs, handling assumptions, and what remains unknown.

## 17. Evidence, sources, and research method

Historical claims should be supported by a bibliography that distinguishes contemporary texts, archaeology, iconography, later chronicles, modern scholarship, and experimental reconstruction. Images of mounted troops are not transparent photographs of actual practice. Literary descriptions may idealize, exaggerate, or serve political purposes. Modern reenactment can generate useful questions but is not direct proof of ancient behavior.

Research each case by period, region, institution, ecology, and available evidence. Record contested terminology and translation choices. Prefer specialist scholarship on animal history, veterinary history, equine archaeology, logistics, and the relevant military institution. Do not infer universal tactics from one famous battle.

## Cross-domain ownership and implementation boundary

raWWar owns mounted-force domain meaning, organization, scenario context, and evaluation. Physics owns physical laws and simulation; Gestures owns reusable embodied movement; the host owns execution, persistence, and player-device integration. SURPS composition remains opt-in. This sourcebook defines requirements and design knowledge; it does not claim a mounted simulation, animal model, haptic system, or training runtime is already implemented.
