# Science Fiction Warfare

**Category:** Speculative and future warfare  
**LOD:** 2  
**Status:** Living sourcebook · version 0.1.0  
**Scope:** Warfare in science-fiction settings, from near-future conflict to interstellar civilizations, with explicit separation between established science, plausible extrapolation, and invented technology.

## The future is a domain, not a prediction

Science-fiction warfare belongs in raWWar because the domain is about organized force, contested authority, protection, raiding, resistance, and consequence—not a particular century or technology level. This book is a framework for building and analyzing fictional conflicts without presenting imagined futures as forecasts.

Every entry should label its assumptions:
- **Established:** supported by current evidence or deployed systems.
- **Extrapolated:** a plausible extension of known principles, with uncertainty.
- **Speculative:** dependent on unresolved science, engineering, economics, or politics.
- **Fictional:** an invented rule of the setting, such as faster-than-light travel or artificial gravity.

A coherent setting may use all four, but must not blur them.

## The battlespace expands

Future conflict may span terrestrial cities, oceans, airspace, orbital infrastructure, moons, planets, habitats, asteroid settlements, deep-space routes, and networks that connect them. Each environment changes the meaning of distance, time, visibility, mobility, sustainment, and civilian protection.

Space is not simply an ocean with different ships. Orbital mechanics, energy, thermal management, communication delay, radiation, debris, propellant, and the absence of convenient concealment create constraints unlike terrestrial naval warfare. If a setting invents artificial gravity, reactionless drives, or instantaneous communication, those are setting rules with strategic consequences—not neutral conveniences.

## Political scale and actors

A future conflict may involve nation-states, alliances, corporations, city-states, planetary governments, habitat coalitions, independence movements, private security forces, autonomous systems, nonhuman polities, or post-state networks. Avoid assuming that technological advancement produces one inevitable political structure.

Define who claims authority, who recognizes it, who pays for force, who controls infrastructure, and who bears the consequences. A corporate security force, a planetary defense service, a rebel fleet, and a civilian rescue organization may all possess advanced equipment while having different legal status and public legitimacy.

## Strategic problems

The classic problems remain recognizable but change shape:
- **Deterrence:** what actions can be credibly threatened, and what signals can be trusted?
- **Escalation:** how does a local incident spread across alliances, orbits, networks, and populations?
- **Logistics:** where do energy, propellant, food, spare parts, computing, and skilled labor come from?
- **Command:** how do delay, autonomy, jamming, deception, and fragmented authority affect decisions?
- **Intelligence:** what can sensors observe, what remains ambiguous, and how can data be spoofed or misinterpreted?
- **Occupation:** how is a habitat or planet governed after military control is established?
- **Resistance:** how do communities preserve identity, communication, and mutual aid under coercive control?
- **Humanitarian protection:** how are civilians, life-support systems, evacuation routes, medical facilities, and ecological systems protected?

A victory that destroys the infrastructure required for survival may be a strategic defeat.

## Domains of future conflict

### Orbital and space infrastructure

Satellites, navigation, communications, observation, weather services, traffic coordination, repair depots, launch facilities, and habitats can be politically and economically indispensable. Treat infrastructure as networks with owners, maintainers, users, dependencies, and failure modes. Do not assume every object in orbit is a weapon or legitimate target.

### Autonomous and networked systems

Autonomy changes the speed and scale of decision-making, but does not erase responsibility. Specify the system's authority, constraints, training data, communications dependencies, auditability, failure behavior, and human oversight. Distinguish remote operation, automated assistance, delegated autonomy, and systems capable of initiating force. A machine's ability to act is not the same as its legal or moral authority to decide.

### Cyber and information conflict

Information operations can affect trust, coordination, identity, infrastructure, and political legitimacy. Separate espionage, disruption, sabotage, propaganda, censorship, and ordinary political persuasion. In fiction, make the technical assumptions explicit and show social consequences; avoid magical hacking that bypasses every constraint whenever the plot needs it.

### Planetary and habitat defense

A settlement's survival may depend on atmosphere, water, agriculture, power, thermal control, medical capacity, and repair crews. Defensive planning must account for cascading failure and the needs of noncombatants. A damaged habitat is not merely a damaged building: it may be a life-support emergency.

### Interstellar campaigns

If faster-than-light travel exists, define its cost, reliability, capacity, detection, access, and strategic geography. If it does not, distance and communication delay reshape political cohesion, reinforcement, intelligence, and command. Either model can support compelling campaigns, but they produce very different strategic worlds.

## Civilian life and ethics

Advanced technology does not make civilian harm abstract. A power station, orbital elevator, water recycler, agricultural dome, data center, or navigation network may sustain millions. The setting must consider distinction, proportionality, necessity, accountability, prisoners, medical care, evacuation, and post-conflict recovery.

Do not treat an entire species, planet, or artificial population as collectively guilty. Define factions and decision-makers precisely. Nonhuman intelligence and machine personhood should be explored as genuine ethical questions rather than convenient labels for expendable enemies.

## Tabletop and simulation design

Start with the political objective and the population at risk, then define the technology assumptions. Model travel and communication time, energy and consumables, infrastructure dependencies, sensor uncertainty, command authority, repair, morale, legitimacy, and escalation. Use a consistent scale: tactical encounters, theater campaigns, planetary crises, and interstellar strategy are not interchangeable levels of detail.

Scenario seeds include a habitat election under blockade; a rescue convoy mistaken for a military reinforcement; a moon seeking autonomy; an AI system refusing an unlawful order; a damaged orbital port with rival claimants; a frontier colony whose supply contract becomes political leverage; and a ceasefire threatened by an unverifiable sensor event.

## Research and design discipline

Use current space policy, orbital mechanics, systems engineering, security studies, humanitarian law, and infrastructure resilience as foundations. Label extrapolation clearly. For fictional technologies, publish the rules and limitations that make the setting internally consistent. The goal is not to predict the future; it is to make imagined futures intelligible, playable, and ethically serious.
