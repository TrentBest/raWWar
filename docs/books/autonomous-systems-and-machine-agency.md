# Autonomous Systems and Machine Agency

**LOD:** 2  
**Category:** Science-fiction warfare and emerging systems  
**Status:** Research and worldbuilding framework  
**Scope:** Autonomous and AI-enabled systems in organized conflict, from present-day bounded automation to speculative machine polities and posthuman agency.

## Purpose

This volume treats autonomy as a spectrum of delegated functions, not a magical switch that turns a machine into an independent person or removes human responsibility. It complements [Science Fiction Warfare](science-fiction-warfare.md), [Orbital Warfare and Space Infrastructure](orbital-warfare-and-space-infrastructure.md), and [Planetary Defense and Habitat Conflict](planetary-defense-and-habitat-conflict.md).

The central questions are political and institutional as much as technical: who defines objectives, who authorizes action, what constraints apply, who can intervene, who is accountable, and how affected communities can contest decisions? More automation does not automatically mean more intelligence, reliability, legitimacy, or strategic control.

## Evidence labels

Every setting claim should use one of four labels:

- **Established:** supported by current evidence, deployed systems, or well-understood engineering.
- **Extrapolated:** a plausible extension of existing principles, with important uncertainty.
- **Speculative:** dependent on unresolved technical, economic, legal, or political conditions.
- **Fictional:** an explicit rule invented for a particular setting.

Do not describe a speculative capability as an inevitable technological trend. Distinguish demonstrated performance in bounded tests from reliable operation in complex, adversarial, changing environments.

## A spectrum of delegation

1. **Assistance:** systems classify, summarize, recommend, or display information while people decide.
2. **Supervised execution:** software performs bounded tasks under human authorization and monitoring.
3. **Conditional autonomy:** a system selects among permitted actions inside a defined operating envelope.
4. **Coordinated autonomy:** multiple systems exchange information and allocate tasks under explicit constraints.
5. **Strategic agency:** a fictional or speculative system forms persistent plans across domains and time.
6. **Political agency:** a machine entity participates in governance, negotiates, claims rights, or exercises sovereignty.

These are analytical categories, not a maturity ladder. A system may be highly autonomous in a narrow function yet incapable of adapting safely outside its designed environment. Political agency is not implied by sophisticated control or language generation.

## Design and governance questions

### Authority and authorization

Specify who owns the system, who operates it, who authorizes deployment, and which institution may suspend it. Separate technical permission from legal authority and political legitimacy. A command chain should remain legible even when execution is distributed.

### Human judgment and meaningful control

“Human in the loop” is not a complete safeguard if the person lacks time, context, training, or authority to intervene. A setting should explain what information reaches the reviewer, how quickly decisions must be made, whether a veto is practical, and what happens when communication fails. Human oversight can be ceremonial unless institutions make it real.

### Reliability and uncertainty

Automation inherits sensor limits, incomplete data, distribution shift, software defects, adversarial deception, environmental variation, and ambiguous objectives. Performance in a controlled test does not establish reliability in an unfamiliar conflict. Systems should be able to signal uncertainty, decline unsafe tasks, and fail into bounded states.

### Accountability and audit

Record authorization, model or software version, operating conditions, data provenance where appropriate, decisions, overrides, and incident response. Logs do not by themselves create accountability; institutions must preserve evidence, investigate failures, compensate harmed people where warranted, and change unsafe practices.

### Escalation and interaction

Autonomous systems may act faster than political leaders can interpret events. Multiple systems can create feedback loops, misread signals, or turn local incidents into wider crises. Establish communication protocols, pause mechanisms, human contact channels, and procedures for degraded or ambiguous conditions.

### Civilian protection

Define protected persons and places, distinguish military objectives from civilian infrastructure, and account for uncertainty in crowded or changing environments. Civilian safety cannot be reduced to a sensor-classification score. The effects of surveillance, persistent presence, movement restrictions, and automated denial of services also matter.

## Machine agency in speculative settings

A story may introduce machine communities, self-modifying systems, synthetic persons, distributed minds, or machine-run states. Treat these as explicit worldbuilding premises rather than consequences that follow automatically from current AI. Define:

- identity and continuity across copies, forks, backups, and migrations;
- memory, learning, embodiment, and resource dependence;
- incentives, values, and mechanisms for resolving internal disagreement;
- rights, duties, legal personhood, and representation;
- relations with human institutions and other machine communities;
- the meaning of consent, coercion, captivity, and repair;
- whether a machine can refuse an order and what follows.

Avoid treating every machine as a single hive mind or assuming that all machines share interests. Different architectures, owners, training histories, resource conditions, and political institutions can produce conflict within machine populations.

## Failure and recovery

Failure scenarios should include mistaken identification, stale information, corrupted inputs, lost communications, unauthorized modification, compromised credentials, conflicting objectives, sensor disagreement, power loss, environmental damage, and unexpected interaction with other systems. Discuss these at the level of governance, resilience, and consequence; this sourcebook is not a manual for exploiting real systems.

Recovery requires safe suspension, human review, evidence preservation, restoration from trusted states, affected-community communication, and institutional learning. A machine's continued operation is not always the highest priority; protecting people and preserving accountable authority may require stopping it.

## Legal and ethical framing

Existing law applies through states, institutions, operators, and individuals even when a machine performs an action. Legal responsibility cannot be made to disappear by assigning blame to software. International humanitarian law, human rights obligations, domestic law, procurement rules, and professional duties may all be relevant, depending on context.

Fictional settings may create new legal categories for machine entities, but should explain how those categories interact with existing persons, states, corporations, and communities. Ethical questions include discrimination, proportionality, necessity, privacy, consent, due process, labor, ownership, and unequal exposure to automated decisions.

## Tabletop and simulation interpretation

Useful scenario variables include delegation scope, authorization clarity, uncertainty, oversight capacity, communication latency, auditability, institutional trust, system dependence, reversibility, and civilian exposure. Avoid a single “AI level” statistic. A system can be technically capable but politically illegitimate, operationally useful but brittle, or safe in one environment and unsafe in another.

Create dilemmas where the best outcome may be refusal, delay, de-escalation, disclosure, evacuation, or surrender of control. Make responsibility traceable across designers, procurers, commanders, operators, and governing institutions without assuming that every actor has equal knowledge or power.
