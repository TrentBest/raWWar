> A comparative world history of healing: caregivers, medical knowledge, institutions, surgery, public health, war, disability, and the changing meanings of recovery.

## Scope and method

This is a historical companion to [Medicine and Healing: History, Practice, and Play](medicine-and-healing.md). It surveys healing across long periods and multiple regions without pretending that the world's medical histories form one ladder of progress. It is a framework for further regional and period-specific research, not an exhaustive catalogue of every tradition or a substitute for specialist scholarship.

The subject is not only the history of medical theories. It includes who cared for the sick, who paid, who was excluded, where knowledge was kept, how practitioners learned, how patients described suffering, what communities understood as recovery, and how institutions shaped access to care.

### Evidence labels

- **Material evidence:** skeletons, teeth, preserved remains, tools, buildings, residues, and other archaeological traces.
- **Textual evidence:** prescriptions, case notes, medical treatises, legal records, letters, administrative documents, and religious or philosophical works.
- **Institutional evidence:** hospital records, military accounts, guild regulations, university curricula, charitable foundations, and public-health ordinances.
- **Retrospective interpretation:** later historians' reconstructions, which may be persuasive but must remain distinguishable from contemporary evidence.
- **Legend and memory:** stories of miraculous cures, exemplary healers, saints, ancestors, and heroic physicians. These are evidence of cultural memory, not automatic proof of clinical events.

A healed injury can establish survival and recovery of some function; it usually cannot identify the treatment used. A medical recipe can establish that a remedy was written down; it does not prove that it was effective or widely used. A famous physician's biography may be shaped by later admirers. State the limits of each source.

## 1. Before medical professions: care as a human practice

Care is older than written medical institutions. Archaeological remains show that some people survived serious injuries and lived with chronic conditions. Such evidence suggests that others supported them, but the exact form of care is often unknowable. A healed fracture does not tell us whether support came from family, neighbors, a specialist, or a wider community.

Caregiving likely included food, warmth, shelter, wound protection, assistance with movement, childbirth support, and social inclusion. These activities were not necessarily separated from ritual, spiritual practice, practical craft, or everyday household labor. The modern distinction between professional medicine and informal care cannot simply be projected into prehistoric settings.

### What archaeology can and cannot say

- Healed bone can show that an injury occurred and that some biological recovery followed.
- Dental remains can reveal patterns of disease, diet, wear, and intervention, but interpretation requires care.
- Burial context may show social treatment of the dead; it rarely gives a complete account of the person's life or care.
- Surgical-looking modifications can be difficult to interpret without comparative evidence.
- Plant residues and tools can suggest use, but identifying a specific treatment or intention may be uncertain.

For roleplaying, prehistoric healing should be grounded in local ecology, craft knowledge, kinship, mobility, and the practical needs of a community—not in the trope of a single all-knowing village shaman.

## 2. Early cities, writing, and specialist knowledge

Urbanization created new medical contexts: dense populations, specialized labor, food storage, water systems, occupational injury, and political institutions. Writing allowed some medical knowledge to be recorded and transmitted, but literacy was uneven and texts represent particular institutions and authors.

### Mesopotamian traditions

Mesopotamian records include medical prescriptions, diagnostic texts, incantations, legal materials, and administrative documents. They show that healing could involve practical remedies and ritual specialists, sometimes within overlapping explanatory systems. The surviving corpus is not a transparent record of ordinary household practice. It reflects what particular scribes and institutions chose to preserve.

### Ancient Egypt

Egyptian medical papyri preserve diverse material on wounds, symptoms, remedies, and bodily understanding. They should be read as historically situated documents, not as a single unified Egyptian medical textbook. Their genres, dates, scribal contexts, and later copying histories matter.

### South Asian traditions

South Asian medical knowledge developed through multiple textual, practical, and teaching traditions. Ayurveda is internally diverse and historically changing; its classical texts were transmitted, interpreted, revised, and applied in different social settings. The existence of a written medical canon does not mean all communities practiced the same medicine or that every text describes everyday care.

### Early Chinese traditions

Chinese medical writings emerged through long processes of compilation, commentary, debate, and adaptation. Concepts associated with qi, yin-yang, channels, pulse, and materia medica changed across texts and periods. Avoid presenting a modern summary of “traditional Chinese medicine” as if it were a timeless description of every historical practice.

## 3. Classical worlds and the movement of medical ideas

The Mediterranean, Near East, South Asia, and East Asia all supported changing bodies of medical thought and practice. Political conquest, trade, migration, translation, and scholarly exchange moved ideas, but transmission was selective. A text translated into a new language could be reinterpreted for a different audience.

### Greek and Hellenistic medicine

The Hippocratic corpus is a collection of works from different authors and contexts, not a single consistent doctrine. It contains observation, case histories, ethical reflection, and theories of bodily balance. Hellenistic centers supported anatomical and philosophical debate, though the surviving evidence is uneven and later accounts can be polemical.

### Roman medicine and military care

Roman medical practice varied by region, status, military role, and access to practitioners. Military forces required care for wounds and illness, but the presence of medical personnel or facilities does not prove uniform access or modern hospital standards. Camp organization, water, sanitation, supply, transport, and local labor were as important as individual skill.

### Medicine beyond the Mediterranean

The histories of South Asia, China, Central Asia, and other regions must not be treated as sidebars to Greek and Roman developments. Their texts and practices have their own chronology, debates, institutions, and exchanges. A comparative history should ask parallel questions without forcing every tradition into one set of European categories.

## 4. Late antiquity and medieval worlds

The medieval centuries were not a pause in medical history. Care developed through households, monasteries, mosques, hospitals, charitable institutions, courts, universities, markets, and itinerant practitioners. Their purposes differed: shelter, hospitality, isolation, long-term support, teaching, treatment, and religious service could overlap.

### Translation and scholarly exchange

Medical texts moved among Greek, Syriac, Arabic, Persian, Sanskrit, Chinese, Latin, and other languages through distinct networks. Translators and commentators did more than copy words: they selected terminology, reconciled disagreements, added observations, and situated older works in new intellectual systems. Translation history is therefore part of medical history.

### Islamic-world medical scholarship and institutions

Medical scholarship across the Islamic world was diverse and multilingual. Physicians, pharmacists, scholars, translators, hospitals, courts, and charitable institutions contributed in different places and periods. Do not reduce this history to a single golden age or assume that all regions shared identical institutions. Track named cities, patrons, texts, practitioners, and political conditions.

### Medieval Europe

European medical learning included universities, religious houses, civic authorities, surgeons, barber-surgeons, midwives, household healers, and practitioners outside formal institutions. Medical authority was contested. Training and access varied sharply by location, gender, class, legal status, and wealth.

### Africa and the wider Indian Ocean

Medical histories across Africa are not a single tradition. Knowledge developed through local ecologies, trade routes, urban centers, religious networks, migration, and colonial encounters. The Indian Ocean connected East Africa, Arabia, Persia, South Asia, and Southeast Asia through exchange in materials, people, and ideas. A useful sourcebook studies these connections without erasing local histories.

### The Americas before and after European conquest

Indigenous American communities possessed diverse medical knowledge rooted in local environments, social institutions, and intellectual traditions. European conquest, epidemic disease, forced migration, mission systems, and colonial administration transformed care and caused immense demographic and cultural disruption. Do not describe this as a simple exchange between two equal medical systems: power, coercion, and disease exposure shaped the encounter.

## 5. Early modern change: print, empire, and professional debate

Print expanded the circulation of medical texts, but access remained unequal. Anatomy, surgery, botany, pharmacology, and natural philosophy developed through observation and dispute. New instruments and anatomical claims did not automatically produce safer treatment. Medical practice remained shaped by apprenticeship, patronage, institutions, and the availability of materials.

### Anatomy and the body

Anatomical investigation altered medical understanding, but the history of anatomy also raises questions about consent, the treatment of the dead, social status, and whose bodies were used for study. A game or historical narrative should not turn dissection into a neutral technical achievement without its social context.

### Empire, trade, and materia medica

Global trade moved plants, minerals, drugs, medical texts, and practitioners. Colonial extraction often depended on unequal power and the appropriation of local knowledge. A responsible account credits knowledge holders and distinguishes documented exchange from later claims of discovery.

### Midwives, surgeons, and household medicine

Care was distributed among people whose work was not always recognized by formal institutions. Midwives, nurses, household caregivers, apothecaries, surgeons, and local healers had distinct roles that changed over time. Professional histories should not erase women's work or treat formal licensing as the only form of expertise.

## 6. The nineteenth century: institutions, infection, and public health

The nineteenth century saw major changes in pathology, physiology, anesthesia, antisepsis, bacteriology, statistics, nursing, and public health. These changes were uneven and contested. They depended on laboratories, hospitals, professional education, manufacturing, transport, municipal government, and public trust.

### Anesthesia and surgery

Anesthesia transformed the conditions under which surgery could be performed, but it did not remove risks or make all operations safe. The development of anesthetic practice involved experimentation, professional disagreement, equipment, monitoring, and evolving standards.

### Antisepsis, asepsis, and infection

Recognition of infection and the development of antiseptic and aseptic methods changed surgical practice. These concepts are related but not identical. A historically accurate account should avoid collapsing them into one sudden invention or attributing a complex transformation to one person alone.

### Nursing and organized care

Modern nursing developed through multiple traditions, institutions, reform movements, and professional debates. It should not be represented merely as the benevolent assistant to a physician. Nurses have held clinical, organizational, educational, and leadership responsibilities.

### Sanitation and public health

Clean water, sewage systems, food safety, housing, occupational conditions, vaccination, surveillance, and public education affect health at population scale. Public health is not simply a collection of individual treatments. It requires political decisions, infrastructure, public cooperation, and attention to unequal exposure.

## 7. War and the changing experience of injury

Warfare has repeatedly changed the scale, distribution, and visibility of injury. It has also exposed military medical systems to political priorities, logistical limits, and ethical conflict. Medical innovation may emerge during war, but innovation does not justify the suffering that prompted it.

### Premodern battlefields

Before modern evacuation and infection control, wounded people faced danger from the battlefield itself, delayed retrieval, blood loss, contaminated wounds, and limited transport. Care could be provided by comrades, attendants, surgeons, religious institutions, local communities, and family networks. The experience differed by status, geography, gender, and the outcome of the campaign.

### Gunpowder and changing wounds

Firearms altered the nature and scale of some injuries, but outcomes depended on weapon design, distance, ammunition, protective equipment, surgical knowledge, and available transport. Historical claims about particular projectiles or treatments should be sourced to the period and region.

### The nineteenth-century mass army

Larger armies and industrial transport created new opportunities and problems: mass casualty movement, crowded hospitals, infection, supply demands, recordkeeping, and the relationship between military and civilian institutions. Medical organizations developed under the pressure of these systems, not through isolated heroic inventions.

### World wars and modern trauma care

The world wars accelerated organized evacuation, blood services, surgical specialization, rehabilitation, prosthetics, and medical research. They also caused catastrophic injury, psychological trauma, displacement, and long-term disability on a vast scale. A balanced account includes the patients, families, nurses, orderlies, local populations, prisoners, and medical personnel—not only celebrated surgeons.

### Contemporary conflict

Modern military medicine combines trained responders, advanced transport, monitoring, surgery, intensive care, and rehabilitation. It still depends on security, infrastructure, staffing, supplies, and access. Conflict can destroy the very systems required to treat its casualties. The legal protection of wounded persons and medical services is a central subject, not an optional footnote.

## 8. Disability, prosthetics, and the meaning of recovery

The history of healing is also the history of people living with injury and disability. Prosthetic devices, mobility aids, hearing technologies, accessible environments, social support, and changing ideas about participation have shaped what recovery means.

A prosthesis is not simply a replacement part that makes a person “whole” again. It may be useful, uncomfortable, costly, culturally significant, or unwanted. Some people prefer assistive technology; others choose different adaptations. A respectful setting allows patients to define their own goals.

Military history often records amputations and decorated veterans while overlooking long-term care, poverty, employment, family labor, pain, and the politics of pensions. Those absences are part of the historical record and should be made visible.

## 9. Disease, epidemics, and population health

Disease can shape campaigns and societies more profoundly than battles. Epidemics, endemic illness, malnutrition, sanitation, migration, and environmental change affect readiness, labor, family structures, trade, and political legitimacy.

Historical epidemic accounts require careful handling. Diagnosis from retrospective descriptions is uncertain; modern disease names should not be assigned casually to historical symptoms. Population estimates and mortality figures may be disputed or politically shaped. Avoid presenting disease as a weaponized plot device without examining the people affected and the institutions responsible for response.

A game can represent:
- uncertain case counts and delayed reporting;
- competing explanations and rumor;
- uneven access to clean water and treatment;
- isolation and quarantine as policies with social costs;
- shortages of caregivers and essential supplies;
- trust, stigma, misinformation, and community cooperation;
- recovery and long-term effects after the acute crisis.

Do not use real outbreaks as an excuse for stigmatizing populations or assigning blame to an ethnicity, nationality, or social group.

## 10. Medicine, belief, and plural systems of care

People seek healing for bodily, psychological, spiritual, and social reasons. Medical systems exist within cultures and institutions; patients may use several forms of care at once. A historian can describe beliefs without endorsing every medical claim, and can examine modern clinical evidence without dismissing the social meaning of ritual or spiritual support.

For worldbuilding, distinguish:
- the patient's explanatory model;
- the practitioner's training and authority;
- the institution's accepted theory;
- the treatment's observed outcome;
- the historian's evidence;
- the game world's actual metaphysics, if magic or supernatural effects exist.

In a fantasy world, magic may be objectively effective. In a historical setting, a healer's belief in magic is a historical fact about that person or community, not proof that the supernatural event occurred.

## 11. Building historical medicine into raWWar

Historical medicine should change play through institutions and constraints, not merely through different names for healing items.

### A period-sensitive care model

For any campaign period, define:
1. Who provides care: household caregivers, healers, surgeons, military attendants, religious institutions, hospitals, or state services.
2. What they know: prevailing explanations, observed patterns, training, and uncertainty.
3. What they can do: tools, remedies, surgery, nursing, transport, and environmental control.
4. What they cannot do: limits imposed by knowledge, infection, pain, blood loss, materials, and time.
5. Who can access care: legal status, wealth, gender, military affiliation, geography, and social relationships.
6. What recovery means: survival, restored function, adaptation, chronic illness, disability, or death.
7. How the community remembers: records, memorials, pensions, stigma, ritual, and family narratives.

### Example: a wounded soldier in three different systems

**A premodern campaign:** the first challenge may be retrieval, shelter, basic wound care, and access to a practitioner. A patient who survives the battle may still face infection and a long recovery. The outcome depends on community support and the campaign's movement.

**An industrial-era campaign:** transport, crowded facilities, infection control, surgical capacity, and supply become central. The casualty may pass through several institutions, each with its own records and limitations.

**A future raWWar campaign:** the medic HUD can observe and organize information, but the casualty still depends on qualified personnel, functioning devices, transport, definitive care, and rehabilitation. Advanced equipment changes the available options; it does not erase the care chain.

### Historical NPC roles

Useful roles include household caregivers, midwives, herbal specialists, surgeons, barber-surgeons, nurses, orderlies, pharmacists, military medical officers, public-health inspectors, translators, hospital administrators, prosthetic makers, and patients who become advocates. Their authority and social position should be researched for the setting's time and place.

## 12. Sourcebook research protocol

Every regional or period companion should include:
- a bounded date range and geographic scope;
- a short historiographical note explaining major debates;
- primary-source genres and their limitations;
- modern scholarship from more than one perspective;
- terminology appropriate to the period;
- a timeline that distinguishes documented events from uncertain dates;
- named institutions and practitioners where evidence permits;
- treatment claims carefully separated from evidence of use;
- the experience of patients and caregivers, not only elite physicians;
- an annotated bibliography with edition and translation details.

Avoid the “first doctor” trap. Claims that one person invented a practice often simplify earlier evidence, parallel developments, and later reinterpretations. Prefer “one influential early account,” “a surviving example,” or “a major contributor” when that is what the evidence supports.

## 13. Playable research and campaign frames

### The contested remedy

A community faces an illness. Practitioners disagree about its cause, a scarce remedy is promoted by a powerful patron, and patients report mixed outcomes. Players investigate evidence, access, trust, and the consequences of a public decision. The goal is not to make a modern player magically know the answer; it is to model uncertainty honestly.

### The hospital ledger

A historical hospital's surviving records show admissions and expenditure but omit many patients and caregivers. Players reconstruct how the institution actually worked, whose care was recorded, and why the archive is incomplete.

### The battlefield evacuation

A retreat leaves wounded people between two jurisdictions. The scenario explores retrieval, local obligations, medical neutrality where applicable, transport, and the political consequences of abandoning or protecting patients.

### The prosthetic workshop

A veteran, craftsperson, physician, and patron disagree about the purpose of a new device. The central question is not whether the device is a perfect replacement, but how the patient wants to live and what the community is willing to support.

### The epidemic archive

Reports conflict, case definitions change, and public authorities face pressure to reopen trade routes. Players distinguish evidence, uncertainty, rumor, and political interest while protecting people and maintaining essential services.

## 14. Historical design principles

- Do not describe the past as a simple march from ignorance to modernity.
- Do not treat any civilization's medicine as uniform or timeless.
- Do not infer clinical efficacy from the existence of a remedy in a text.
- Do not infer universal access from the existence of a hospital.
- Do not erase caregivers whose labor was informal, gendered, unpaid, or socially marginalized.
- Do not mistake later legend for contemporary evidence.
- Do not portray disability as a failed ending.
- Do not let a game's modern interface give historical characters knowledge they could not possess.
- Do not turn war-driven medical innovation into a celebration detached from its casualties.
- Do show exchange, translation, adaptation, disagreement, and the politics of institutions.

## 15. Relationship to the medicine series

This book is the first historical deepening of [Medicine and Healing: History, Practice, and Play](medicine-and-healing.md). It should be followed by regional and period-specific volumes, not treated as a finished global encyclopedia.

Planned related volumes:
- **Battlefield Medicine and the Casualty Chain** — military care, evacuation, triage, and trauma systems.
- **Surgery, Amputation, and Prosthetic Futures** — the history of surgery, limb loss, prosthetics, and rehabilitation.
- **Medicine at Sea, in Space, and on the Frontier** — care where transport and supply are constrained.
- **Fantasy Healing and the Laws of Restoration** — the history-inspired design of magic, healing, and resurrection.
- **Future Medicine and Human-Machine Care** — emerging and speculative capabilities, with explicit evidence labels.
- **Medical Ethics, Law, and Protected Care** — consent, impartiality, and medical protection during conflict.

The historical series should expand through focused regional studies—for example, medicine in ancient Egypt, Greek and Hellenistic medicine, Roman military and civilian care, medical scholarship across the medieval Islamic world, South Asian traditions, Chinese medical history, African medical histories, Indigenous American healing traditions, early modern global exchange, and modern public health. These are research directions, not claims that each is one internally uniform system.

## 16. Glossary

- **Asepsis:** practices intended to prevent contamination by microorganisms.
- **Antisepsis:** measures intended to reduce or inhibit microorganisms in living tissue or wounds.
- **Materia medica:** the body of substances used in medicine and the knowledge concerning them.
- **Palliative care:** care focused on relieving suffering and supporting quality of life.
- **Pathology:** the study of disease processes and their effects.
- **Prosthesis:** an artificial device that replaces or supports a missing or impaired body part.
- **Public health:** organized action to protect and improve the health of populations.
- **Historiography:** the study of how history is researched, written, debated, and revised.
