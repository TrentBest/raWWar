# Roman Civil Wars

**LOD:** 2  
**Category:** Historical warfare  
**Status:** Research-oriented sourcebook  
**Scope:** From the Social War (91–87 BCE) through the Augustan settlement (27 BCE), with antecedents and later comparisons clearly identified.

## Purpose and boundaries

This volume studies civil war as a crisis of political membership, lawful command, military allegiance, and control of the Roman state. It complements [Roman Republican Warfare](roman-republican-warfare.md), which provides the broader institutional frame, and [The Roman Imperial Army](roman-imperial-army.md), which treats the later standing military system. It is not a catalogue of every Roman internal conflict.

“Civil war” is not a neutral label in every ancient source. Participants claimed to defend the Republic, restore lawful government, avenge injury, protect allies, or preserve order. Those claims were political arguments, not self-validating descriptions. The analysis distinguishes the language of legitimacy from the material realities of recruitment, finance, coercion, and violence.

## Chronological framework

### The Social War and the question of citizenship

The conflict of 91–87 BCE grew from disputes over Italian allies' political status and access to Roman citizenship. The insurgent communities were not simply foreign enemies: they had long supplied military manpower and participated in Roman expansion while contesting unequal political membership. The war's settlement transformed the citizen body and the political geography of Italy. It is an essential antecedent to later civil conflict because it exposed how military contribution, civic rights, and sovereignty could become inseparable.

### Marius, Sulla, and armed intervention

The rivalry and competing commands of the late second and early first centuries BCE culminated in Sulla's march on Rome, the civil wars, proscriptions, and dictatorship. Avoid the simplistic story that a single “Marian reform” suddenly created a professional army loyal only to generals. Recruitment, land, patronage, command, and political competition changed over time and varied by context. Soldiers' interests mattered, but so did civic identity, unit cohesion, pay, promises, and perceptions of lawful authority.

### Pompey, Caesar, and the collapse of negotiated politics

The conflict beginning in 49 BCE cannot be reduced to a duel of personalities. It involved constitutional deadlock, elite competition, provincial commands, veteran settlement, debt and finance, allied communities, and the political consequences of extraordinary military power. The crossing of the Rubicon became a potent symbol, but the underlying crisis had accumulated over decades. Campaign narratives must be read critically: Caesar's own commentaries are both invaluable evidence and acts of political self-presentation.

### The Ides, the triumvirs, and the final contest

Caesar's assassination did not restore the Republic. The subsequent struggle among the assassins, Caesar's heirs and allies, and rival commanders produced shifting coalitions, proscriptions, the wars around Philippi, and the contest between Octavian and Antony. The conflict ended not with a simple battlefield decision but with the defeat of Antony and Cleopatra, the absorption of rival power bases, and the construction of a new political order.

### The Augustan settlement

The Principate preserved republican forms while concentrating decisive authority. Demobilization, veteran colonies, provincial command arrangements, fiscal capacity, and control of armed forces helped make the settlement durable. The transition should be studied as institutional reconstruction after civil war, not merely a change of title.

## How civil wars worked

- **Legitimacy:** competing claims to lawful command, senatorial authority, popular support, emergency necessity, and constitutional restoration.
- **Manpower:** citizen levies, allied communities, veterans, provincial recruits, auxiliaries, and local political networks.
- **Finance:** treasury access, provincial revenues, loans, confiscation, requisition, and the credibility of future rewards.
- **Command:** personal loyalty and patronage interacted with formal office, delegation, military reputation, and coalition bargaining.
- **Territory:** control of Italy, strategic provinces, ports, grain supply, roads, and recruitment regions shaped the ability to continue a war.
- **Information:** proclamations, letters, speeches, coinage, public ritual, and histories framed the same events in incompatible ways.
- **Civilian experience:** confiscation, forced contributions, displacement, proscription, siege, enslavement, and local accommodation were unevenly distributed.

## Evidence and interpretation

The principal narrative sources are late and politically situated: Caesar, Cicero, Sallust, Appian, Plutarch, Suetonius, Cassius Dio, and others preserve indispensable material but differ in purpose, access, chronology, and retrospective framing. Inscriptions, coins, papyri, archaeology, and prosopography help test narrative claims. A source's vividness is not proof of its neutrality.

Treat casualty totals, speeches, motives, and dramatic scenes cautiously. Where modern scholarship disagrees, preserve the disagreement rather than manufacture certainty. The term “proscription” should be used precisely for politically authorized lists and killings, while not every act of confiscation or revenge should be folded into that category.

## Tabletop and simulation interpretation

For play, represent a civil war as a contest among institutions and coalitions rather than a simple two-sided battle. Useful variables include command legitimacy, coalition cohesion, provincial revenue, veteran obligations, urban provisioning, recruitment access, elite defections, and war weariness. A tactical victory may worsen strategic legitimacy; a negotiated settlement may be more consequential than a battle.

Do not assign fixed loyalty to an entire people, region, or military unit. Model allegiance as contextual and changing, with local actors pursuing security, status, survival, and political goals. The consequences for noncombatants should be visible in campaign state.
