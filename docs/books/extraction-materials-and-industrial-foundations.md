# Extraction, Materials, and Industrial Foundations

> The Singularity Workshop · raWWar Domain Sourcebook · LOD 2 · Version 0.1.0

A comparative study of resource extraction and industrial transformation, from terrestrial agriculture, mines, forests, and refineries to extraterrestrial prospecting, in-situ resource use, and the economics of materials across different worlds.

## Purpose and use

This volume is a reusable historical, institutional, and simulation-design reference. It distinguishes documented history, current evidence, extrapolation, speculation, and fictional setting rules. It complements the broader [Warfare and Organized Force](warfare-and-organized-force.md) foundation and the [sourcebook catalogue](index.md).

## 1. Scope and terminology

Military power depends on industrial foundations that precede the battlefield by years or generations. This book follows raw resources through discovery, extraction, beneficiation, refining, fabrication, certification, transport, reuse, and disposal. It treats industry as a social and ecological system, not a bottomless technology tree.

The phrase “rare earth elements” is a conventional scientific and industrial term for a defined group of elements; it does not mean those elements are unique to Earth. For comparative worldbuilding, distinguish the established term from the more explicit phrase **rare on Earth** when discussing local abundance. A material can be common in one planetary body, concentrated in another, and uneconomic to recover in either.

## 2. Abundance is not availability

Geological abundance, concentration, chemical form, deposit size, depth, accessibility, extraction difficulty, processing requirements, energy cost, environmental impact, legal access, and market demand are different variables. An element may be abundant in a crust but dispersed at low concentration. Another may be concentrated in a deposit that is too deep, unstable, remote, or politically inaccessible.

For each resource, separate total estimated inventory from identified deposit, measured reserve, economically recoverable reserve, permitted production, actual throughput, and delivered material. These are not interchangeable quantities. Prices, technology, infrastructure, law, and knowledge can change what counts as recoverable.

## 3. Terrestrial resource foundations

Earth's industrial history draws on agricultural soils, forests, stone, clay, sand, salt, ores, fossil carbon, groundwater, and many other resources. Extraction has always depended on local knowledge, labor, tools, transport, energy, political authority, and environmental conditions.

The military consequences are indirect as well as immediate. Timber supports ships, buildings, fuel, and tools; metals support weapons, machinery, rails, vehicles, and communications; agricultural production sustains populations and workers; and energy systems power factories and transport. No single material explains military power. The system of complementary inputs matters.

## 4. Mining and quarrying through history

Mining ranges from surface collection and shallow workings to deep mines, placer operations, quarries, and highly mechanized extraction. Different eras require different combinations of labor, drainage, ventilation, structural support, hauling, surveying, smelting, and administrative control.

Historical mining should be studied through material remains, tools, slag, settlement patterns, written accounts, trade routes, labor conditions, and ownership. Production estimates are often uncertain. Avoid projecting modern corporate structures or geological categories onto ancient societies without evidence.

## 5. Metallurgy and material transformation

Ore is not finished metal. Extraction is followed by sorting, crushing, concentration, smelting or chemical processing, refining, alloying, casting, forming, heat treatment, machining, finishing, inspection, and repair. Each stage has yield losses, energy needs, skilled labor, emissions, and equipment requirements.

The military value of a material depends on its properties and the ability to shape it reliably. Strength, toughness, density, corrosion resistance, thermal behavior, conductivity, fatigue, joining, and repair all matter. A rare high-performance alloy may be less useful than a common material with mature supply chains and local repair capacity.

## 6. Strategic materials and the language of rarity

The familiar category “rare earth elements” should be explained accurately rather than used as a claim of Earth-exclusive scarcity. In a multi-world setting, use explicit qualifiers such as rare on Earth, concentrated on Luna, abundant in a carbonaceous asteroid class, or costly to refine in a particular industrial region. Do not assume an element is rare everywhere because it is difficult to obtain in one place.

Strategic importance is contextual. A material becomes strategically sensitive when supply is concentrated, substitution is difficult, processing is specialized, inventories are small, demand is inelastic, or access is politically contested. A common element can become a bottleneck through refining capacity or transport. A geologically scarce element can be unimportant if substitutes are adequate.

## 7. Industrial ecology and the factory system

Mines and refineries are only the beginning. Industrial capacity includes power generation, water, chemicals, furnaces, machine tools, precision measurement, skilled labor, logistics, waste treatment, safety systems, maintenance, finance, standards, and technical knowledge. A modern shipyard cannot be summoned by possessing steel alone.

Factories depend on networks of suppliers. A small component, catalyst, bearing, sensor, or specialized machine tool can constrain an entire production chain. Model dependencies and substitution pathways rather than treating “industry” as one generic building.

## 8. Shipbuilding and maritime industry

Shipbuilding combines hull materials, propulsion, power, electronics, navigation, safety systems, weapons or mission equipment, accommodation, yards, dry docks, cranes, skilled trades, testing, certification, and long-term maintenance. The fleet's material base includes not only vessels but ports, repair facilities, fuel systems, trained crews, and replacement components.

Wooden, iron, steel, composite, and advanced-material vessels belong to different industrial ecologies. The transition between them changes forestry, mining, metallurgy, labor, capital, ports, and state procurement. For force organization see [Formations II: Naval Forces](formations-ii-naval-forces.md) and [Formations X: Armadas and Flotillas](formations-x-armadas-and-flotillas.md).

## 9. Aircraft and aerospace industry

Aircraft production depends on lightweight structures, engines, turbines or motors, electronics, sensors, control systems, precision machining, composites, testing, maintenance, and a highly trained workforce. The industrial base is not just the assembly plant. It includes certification, spare parts, software support, fuel or electrical infrastructure, and lifecycle sustainment.

Aircraft and spacecraft should not be collapsed into one category. They share some industrial capabilities but operate under different environments and physical constraints. See [Formations III: Air Forces](formations-iii-air-forces.md) and [Formations VI: Orbital and Space Forces](formations-vi-orbital-and-space-forces.md).

## 10. Planetary bodies as distinct resource environments

A planetary system offers varied environments: planets, moons, dwarf planets, asteroids, comets, rings, and interplanetary dust. Their compositions and accessibility differ. Surface abundance is not the same as recoverable industrial feedstock. Gravity, temperature, vacuum, radiation, geology, volatile retention, dust, and orbital location shape extraction and processing.

Mercury is a rocky terrestrial planet with a metal-rich interior and extreme surface conditions; it is not generally a sea of liquid mercury. The element mercury is a volatile metal under ordinary terrestrial conditions and is not what gives the planet its name. A fictional setting may invent unusual deposits or environments, but label those rules as fictional rather than presenting them as established planetary science.

## 11. Asteroids, comets, and prospecting

Asteroids vary widely in composition and structure. Some are rich in silicates and metals; some contain carbon-bearing materials; some retain water or other volatiles. Classification from remote observations is uncertain and requires ground truth. A spectral signature does not guarantee a mineable deposit or an economically useful yield.

Prospecting is a staged information problem: identify candidates, characterize uncertainty, estimate composition and structure, evaluate accessibility, determine processing needs, and compare delivered value against mission cost and risk. In a mature setting, fleets of survey craft and autonomous instruments may create a resource atlas. Their data still require calibration, provenance, validation, and update history.

## 12. Extraterrestrial extraction and in-situ resource use

In-situ resource utilization (ISRU) means using local material to support operations where it is found. Potential uses include water extraction, oxygen production, construction feedstock, shielding, and propellant production, but feasibility depends on actual deposits, chemistry, energy, equipment, maintenance, and scale. A laboratory demonstration is not a planetary industrial base.

Different bodies may be valuable for different reasons. Water can matter more than metal for a remote outpost; a metal-rich asteroid may be less useful if it lacks volatiles or is too difficult to process. The right question is not “which asteroid is richest?” but “which supply chain can reliably turn this body into useful delivered products?”

## 13. Refining, separation, and stockpiling beyond Earth

Raw feedstock must be sorted, crushed or otherwise prepared, separated, refined, and converted into standardized products. Processing in space may avoid lifting some mass out of a deep gravity well, but introduces power, heat rejection, maintenance, microgravity handling, radiation, contamination, and worker-safety challenges.

A central processing facility can pool specialized equipment, skilled operators, quality assurance, and storage. It can also become a single point of failure or a political chokepoint. Distributed processing offers resilience but duplicates capital and maintenance. Compare total delivered cost and resilience, not just extraction cost.

## 14. Recycling and material circulation

A mature industrial system recovers material from ships, stations, habitats, machinery, packaging, and waste streams. Recovery is limited by mixed composition, contamination, joining methods, degradation, energy, labor, and quality requirements. Some components are remanufactured; others are down-cycled; some cannot be economically recovered.

A resource ledger should track primary feedstock, process yield, scrap, recovered material, quality class, energy use, waste, and provenance. “Closed loop” should be treated as a performance goal with measurable losses, not a magical state of zero waste.

## 15. Labor, ownership, and extraction politics

Extraction creates communities and conflicts over land, water, labor, royalties, safety, pollution, and political control. Mines can be operated by states, private firms, cooperatives, local communities, chartered companies, or mixed institutions. Ownership of a deposit does not automatically settle rights over transport corridors, environmental effects, indigenous or local claims, or downstream processing.

In space, legal regimes and governance may be unsettled or fictional. Define property, licensing, liability, environmental stewardship, worker status, and dispute resolution. Do not assume that physical remoteness removes politics or creates a legal vacuum.

## 16. Industrial capacity and military mobilization

A state or empire cannot convert all civilian industry into military output without consequences. Mobilization competes with housing, food, medicine, transport, energy, civilian maintenance, and consumer goods. Conversion requires design changes, tooling, skilled labor, contracts, standards, and time. It can create shortages and political resistance.

Model industrial capacity by production rate, input dependencies, workforce, utilization, maintenance, quality, lead time, surge capacity, and recovery after disruption. A factory's nameplate capacity is not its sustainable output.

## 17. Environmental and planetary stewardship

Extraction can alter landscapes, water systems, atmospheres, habitats, and long-term settlement prospects. On unfamiliar worlds, ecological ignorance increases the risk of irreversible harm. Even apparently lifeless bodies may have scientific, cultural, or future-use value. In inhabited systems, local communities may regard resource access as sovereignty, survival, or sacred obligation.

Environmental cost is not an optional moral score appended after production. It affects health, stability, infrastructure, legitimacy, and future resource availability.

## 18. Simulation contract and scenarios

A deposit or industrial site should record body/location, resource identity, estimated quantity, confidence, concentration, physical form, access conditions, ownership, legal status, extraction rate, processing yield, energy and water demand, workforce, equipment dependencies, waste streams, transport links, environmental constraints, and update provenance.

Scenarios include a high-grade deposit without refining capacity; a refinery with a missing catalyst; a water-rich moon whose access is politically contested; a shipyard competing with civilian infrastructure; an asteroid survey whose confidence proves overstated; and a resource-rich region whose transport costs erase its apparent advantage.

## 19. Sources and provider boundaries

Use geological surveys, mineralogical research, industrial statistics, archaeology, mining history, environmental records, labor histories, corporate filings, and planetary-science mission data. Separate direct measurements, model estimates, speculative resource claims, and fictional worldbuilding.

## Editorial and simulation principles

- Preserve provenance, uncertainty, ownership, authority, and the difference between nominal capacity and usable capacity.
- Treat civilian life, labor, environment, and political legitimacy as part of the system rather than externalities that can be ignored.
- Separate physical state from legal status, commercial ownership, organizational command, and permission to act.
- Use Established, Extrapolated, Speculative, and Fictional labels whenever future technology is discussed.
