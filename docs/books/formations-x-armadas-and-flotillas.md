> A fleet is not a crowd of ships. It is a changing organization of people, vessels, authorities, information, and support.

This LOD 2 sourcebook studies large maritime formations from small flotillas to armadas and multinational fleets. It covers organizational scale, fleet composition, command, escorts, transports, auxiliaries, maritime services, dispersed operations, concentration, sustainment, civilian systems, and simulation contracts. It is not a real-world tactical employment or weapons manual.

## 1. Scope and vocabulary

Fleet, armada, flotilla, squadron, task force, convoy, and expeditionary group are terms whose meanings vary by language, period, state, and institution. Do not impose one universal size hierarchy. A flotilla may be a small group of craft, a formal command, or a specialized service organization. Armada may describe a large fleet or a historically specific institution.

For every case, record the source's own terminology, organizational level, authority, and effective date. A formation's name does not establish its size or capability.

## 2. Historical method

Naval organization has changed with ship technology, political economy, shipbuilding, ports, navigation, communications, finance, and labor. Ancient oared fleets, early modern sailing armadas, industrial battle fleets, and modern carrier or submarine groups should not be arranged as simple steps toward one ideal.

Separate a ruler's intended fleet from vessels actually assembled, available crews, readiness, supply, and performance. Preserve the perspectives of sailors, conscripts, enslaved rowers, merchants, port workers, coastal populations, and opponents where evidence allows.

## 3. Fleet as a system

A fleet is a connected system of:
- vessels and their crews;
- command organizations and legal authorities;
- transport, escort, patrol, rescue, and service roles;
- ports, anchorages, depots, repair facilities, and communications;
- provisions, fuel, water, spares, medical support, and personnel rotation;
- charts, navigation, weather, observation, and uncertainty;
- civilian shipping, fishing, trade, and coastal communities.

A fleet can remain afloat while losing the support system that makes it sustainable.

## 4. Scale and nested organization

Model nested organization without assuming identical structures. A flotilla may be a subunit of a squadron, an independent local force, or a group defined by craft type. Squadrons may be administrative, operational, training, or geographic. Task forces are often assembled around a mission and may combine multiple services.

Use explicit parent-child and temporary attachment relationships. Preserve each vessel's permanent identity, owner, crew, and maintenance record when it joins or leaves a formation.

## 5. Armada composition

A large armada may include combatant vessels, scouts, escorts, transports, tankers or replenishment vessels, repair ships, hospital ships, tenders, survey craft, communications relays, landing support, rescue services, and civilian-chartered shipping.

Composition should be derived from mission, duration, geography, available infrastructure, political coalition, and support needs. A large count of combatant vessels does not prove the fleet can transport, repair, feed, or medically support itself.

## 6. Flotillas and local maritime networks

Flotillas are useful as a scale and organization concept because small craft can form persistent local systems: harbor defense, river and estuary services, coastal patrol, fishing protection, rescue, survey, transport, or military missions. They may rely on shore facilities more than large ocean-going ships.

Do not equate small craft with low importance. Local knowledge, access, maintenance, and relationships with communities can matter more than displacement or armament.

## 7. Escort, screen, and service relationships

An escort relationship is an organizational commitment with a scope, duration, authority, and supported entity. It is not merely a ship drawn beside another on a map. A convoy may include commercial operators, military escorts, rescue services, port authorities, and cargo owners with different legal positions.

For simulation, model protection commitments, availability, communications, service dependencies, and withdrawal conditions at a high level. Detailed real-world screening geometry, attack planning, targeting, or weapons employment is outside scope.

## 8. Concentration, dispersion, and cohesion

A fleet may concentrate for shared command, maintenance, ceremony, transfer, or a common mission; it may disperse across routes and stations to serve a wider region. Concentration can simplify coordination but increase congestion and shared vulnerability. Dispersion can widen presence but increase communications, support, and coordination burdens.

Treat cohesion as more than physical proximity. It may depend on shared command, information, logistics, trust, timing, and the ability to reunite. Avoid presenting one formation geometry as universally optimal.

## 9. Command and coalition authority

Fleet command can involve national authorities, coalition leaders, service commanders, vessel masters, port authorities, commercial owners, and safety regulators. Operational coordination does not erase legal or contractual boundaries.

Record who may issue orders, who controls a vessel, who can refuse an unsafe request, who is responsible for embarked civilians, and how disagreements are resolved. A chartered merchant vessel does not automatically become a warship because it sails with a fleet.

## 10. Communications and maritime awareness

Fleet-wide understanding is assembled from observations, reports, navigation data, schedules, weather, and intelligence of varying quality. Information can be delayed, stale, contradictory, or unavailable. A common display must show provenance and uncertainty.

Distinguish observed position from predicted position, planned route from actual track, and a communications relationship from command authority. A vessel outside contact is not automatically lost, hostile, or destroyed.

## 11. Sustainment and fleet endurance

Fleet endurance depends on provisions, potable water, fuel or other energy, ammunition, maintenance, spare parts, medical services, crew rest, waste handling, and access to ports or replenishment. Different vessels have different endurance and compatibility.

The [Art of Resupply](the-art-of-resupply.md) owns the wider logistics discipline. This volume specifies maritime organizational dependencies and the difference between cargo embarked, cargo available, and support actually delivered.

## 12. Ports, anchorages, and repair ecosystems

Ports are not interchangeable map symbols. Their depth, berths, cranes, storage, labor, utilities, security, ownership, environmental restrictions, and political access shape fleet capacity. Anchorages and temporary service areas also depend on weather, seabed, navigation, and local rights.

Fleet repair is an industrial ecosystem: diagnosis, parts, skilled labor, dry-docking or other facilities, testing, certification, and return to service. A damaged vessel may be recoverable but unavailable for a particular mission.

## 13. Convoys, commerce, and civilian shipping

Naval formations interact with commercial routes and civilian vessels. Convoys may be military, commercial, humanitarian, or mixed. Record cargo ownership, crew status, legal protection, charter terms, inspection authority, and destination.

Do not classify every merchant vessel as a military asset or every convoy as a combat formation. The protection and disruption of commerce can affect food, medicine, energy, migration, and the wider civilian economy.

## 14. Amphibious and expeditionary interfaces

Armadas may carry or support land formations, but transport does not make the fleet and embarked force one organization. Preserve command, identity, custody, and support responsibilities across embarkation, transit, transfer, and shore reception.

[Formations VII](formations-vii-amphibious-and-littoral-forces.md) owns the sea-to-shore organizational transition. [Formations IX](formations-ix-engineering-and-construction-forces.md) covers engineering organizations and temporary infrastructure that may support ports and crossings.

## 15. Historical comparison cases

Compare fleets by their political economy and institutional context: ancient Mediterranean fleets and allied contingents; medieval and early modern maritime coalitions; Iberian armadas; Dutch and English commercial-military systems; industrial-era battle fleets; twentieth-century carrier and amphibious task organizations; and contemporary multinational maritime operations.

For each case, ask who paid, who built and maintained vessels, how crews were recruited, what ports were available, what the fleet was expected to achieve, and whose account survives.

## 16. Science-fiction fleets and non-oceanic domains

Space armadas may be a fictional organizational label, but they are not naval fleets in a vacuum. Orbital mechanics, energy, heat rejection, communication delay, radiation, debris, propellant, life support, and industrial support determine what formations can do. A formation diagram must not imply ships can stop, turn, or hold station like surface vessels.

Use [Formations VI: Orbital and Space Forces](formations-vi-orbital-and-space-forces.md) and [Orbital Warfare and Space Infrastructure](orbital-warfare-and-space-infrastructure.md) for domain-specific boundaries. Mark future capabilities Established, Extrapolated, Speculative, or Fictional.

## 17. Civilian impact, law, and escalation

A fleet's movement can affect ports, fisheries, shipping lanes, marine ecosystems, coastal livelihoods, and humanitarian access. Naval power also intersects with sovereignty, maritime law, rescue obligations, neutrality, blockade, sanctions, and escalation.

Avoid turning civilians into anonymous cargo or the sea into an empty arena. Record affected communities and legal context.

## 18. Simulation contract

A fleet/armada/flotilla descriptor should include:
- formation identity, type, effective dates, and source terminology;
- parent organization and temporary attachments;
- vessels, crews, owners, and embarked populations as separate entities;
- command, legal authority, and coalition relationships;
- mission scope and duration;
- support, repair, port, and replenishment dependencies;
- communications, navigation, and information confidence;
- civilian and environmental interfaces;
- readiness dimensions, limitations, and uncertainty;
- dissolution, transfer, and historical provenance.

Readiness should be decomposed: a fleet may be navigationally ready but short of stores, medically constrained, politically unauthorized, or unable to maintain its vessels.

## 19. Playable scenarios and evaluation

Scenario frames include a flotilla supporting a remote archipelago; an armada assembled from unequal coalition contributions; a convoy whose commercial and military authorities disagree; a fleet forced to choose between concentration and distributed presence; and a port network whose repair capacity becomes the campaign's limiting factor.

Evaluate coordination, truthful information, sustainable endurance, coalition legitimacy, civilian protection, and mission adaptation—not simply the number of vessels assembled.

## 20. Sources and ownership

Use naval archives, ship logs, port records, archaeology, maritime labor histories, commercial records, legal documents, and testimony. Official fleet lists rarely reveal readiness, crew condition, or actual logistical reach.

raWWar owns fleet organization, doctrine as historical and fictional domain knowledge, campaign context, and simulation requirements. Physics owns physical and environmental models. SURPS remains opt-in.

## Editorial rule

An armada is not a number of hulls. It is a temporary political, industrial, human, and logistical achievement—and it can fail even when every ship remains afloat.
