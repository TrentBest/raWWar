# Orbital Industry and Strategic Resource Networks

> The Singularity Workshop · raWWar Domain Sourcebook · LOD 2 · Version 0.1.0

A systems-level study of off-world industrial infrastructure, asteroid prospecting and capture concepts, processing centers, depots, transport networks, and the physics, governance, and resilience constraints that shape a solar-system economy.

## Purpose and use

This volume is a reusable historical, institutional, and simulation-design reference. It distinguishes documented history, current evidence, extrapolation, speculation, and fictional setting rules. It complements the broader [Warfare and Organized Force](warfare-and-organized-force.md) foundation and the [sourcebook catalogue](index.md).

## 1. Scope and evidence labels

This book treats orbital industry as infrastructure that supports civilian life, commerce, exploration, and military forces. It covers shipyards, stations, elevators, prospecting networks, processing facilities, depots, and bulk transport. It does not assume any of these systems currently exist at industrial scale.

Use four labels throughout: **Established** for demonstrated physics or deployed capability; **Extrapolated** for plausible engineering extensions with significant unresolved constraints; **Speculative** for concepts whose feasibility depends on major advances or uncertain economics; and **Fictional** for invented setting rules. A facility illustration is not evidence of feasibility.

## 2. The industrial network, not the single megastructure

An orbital economy is a network of specialized sites: mines, survey craft, capture and handling systems, processing plants, foundries, machine shops, fuel depots, power stations, habitats, shipyards, ports, and receiving terminals. Each site has dependencies and a service area. The network's throughput is limited by its narrowest critical stage, not by the largest structure.

Centralization can improve specialization and utilization but creates congestion, long queues, political leverage, and common-mode failure. Distributed facilities increase resilience and local autonomy but require duplicated capital, trained personnel, spare parts, and quality assurance.

## 3. Space elevators and launch infrastructure

A space elevator is a speculative megastructure requiring extraordinary material strength, dynamic stability, station keeping, maintenance, traffic management, and protection from environmental hazards. It should not be treated as an ordinary tower extended upward. Different concepts—tether elevators, launch loops, orbital rings, skyhooks, and momentum-exchange systems—have distinct physical architectures and risk profiles.

A setting should state which concept exists, what material and energy assumptions support it, how it is maintained, who controls access, and what happens during failure. No single elevator solves planetary access, deep-space transport, or interplanetary logistics by itself.

## 4. Stations, shipyards, and repair ecosystems

Stations provide some combination of habitation, power, communications, storage, laboratories, manufacturing, docking, repair, medical care, and transfer services. Shipyards add fabrication, assembly, inspection, testing, and lifecycle maintenance. Their capacity depends on material flow, skilled labor, tooling, energy, thermal management, and reliable transport.

A shipyard's military importance may be less about building warships than keeping cargo craft, tugs, rescue vehicles, tankers, habitats, and industrial equipment operational. Civilian and military uses may share infrastructure, creating legal and escalation questions.

## 5. Asteroid prospecting as an information service

A distributed survey network identifies candidate bodies and measures composition, shape, spin, mass estimates, surface conditions, and orbital state. Measurements have uncertainty and may be stale or biased by sensor limitations. Resource claims need provenance, calibration, independent confirmation, and an audit trail.

The prospecting fleet can be more valuable as a continuously updated information network than as a collection of individual mining missions. Its data support investment, safety, routing, insurance, public governance, and military resource planning.

## 6. The catcher or receiving-field concept

A large receiving facility that accepts material from remote extraction sites is a compelling fictional industrial architecture. The “baseball glove” metaphor is useful for visualization: incoming cargo is guided into a controlled receiving envelope, captured by a system designed to absorb or redirect its momentum, inspected, and transferred to processing or storage.

This is not a literal giant net that can safely catch arbitrary asteroids. Capture requires a defined target mass, velocity, uncertainty envelope, structural load path, energy dissipation or recovery system, and contingency for a missed or damaged arrival. The facility's scale and cost grow with the kinetic energy and momentum it must manage. A safer architecture may process material at the source and ship standardized, lower-mass cargo rather than move whole asteroids.

## 7. Momentum, energy, and propulsion

A cargo object's momentum is mass multiplied by velocity; its kinetic energy grows with mass and with the square of speed. Even a modest velocity mismatch becomes consequential for a very large body. A receiving facility must not simply “stop” cargo without accounting for where the momentum and energy go.

Ion and other electric propulsion systems accelerate reaction mass using electrical power. Solar-electric propulsion converts captured sunlight into electricity, then uses that electricity to accelerate propellant. It does not directly turn sunlight into braking force without expelling reaction mass or interacting with an external field. Low-thrust propulsion can gradually change a trajectory over long periods, but requires propellant, power, time, thermal management, and navigation. Deceleration is a planned maneuver, not an automatic property of the trip.

A cargo tug may provide course correction and trajectory shaping; it cannot ignore orbital mechanics. Any scenario should state initial and target orbits, transfer windows, mass, thrust, power, propellant, uncertainty, and the receiving system's capture envelope. Physics owns numerical dynamics and simulation.

## 8. A safer logistics architecture

A plausible architecture separates prospecting, extraction, local processing, bulk transport, receiving, and final fabrication. Remote survey craft characterize targets. Specialized equipment extracts selected feedstock. Processing near the source may separate waste and convert raw material into standardized cargo. Tugs move bounded loads along planned transfers. Receiving stations inspect and sort shipments; central facilities refine, store, and distribute materials.

The architecture should compare whole-body transport with local processing, modular cargo, propellant production, and multiple smaller shipments. It should also compare a central “catcher” with distributed receiving nodes. The best choice depends on energy, mass, traffic, maintenance, risk, and governance—not on a single visual metaphor.

## 9. Processing centers and material silos

A large processing center may separate incoming feedstock into standardized material streams and store them in distinct inventories. In a mature fictional system, these streams could include structural metals, volatile compounds, silicates, carbon-bearing feedstock, water, and specialized products. Exact composition varies by source body and requires characterization.

Silos are not passive containers. They require containment, monitoring, thermal control where needed, contamination control, inventory certification, fire or reaction safety, maintenance, and custody. A processing center is also a labor community and political institution. Who owns the output? Who sets prices? Who gets priority during shortage? Who can inspect the facility?

## 10. Regular freight and scheduled distribution

Once output is standardized, scheduled freight can move products to orbital yards, habitats, planetary receiving facilities, and other industrial nodes. Regular services reduce uncertainty and improve planning, but create dependencies on route availability, fleet maintenance, traffic coordination, and port capacity.

A system should distinguish cargo on hand, cargo booked, cargo launched, cargo in transit, cargo received, cargo inspected, and cargo released to inventory. A shipment arriving is not the same as a shipment being accepted and usable.

## 11. Planetary receiving and momentum management

A facility near a planet may receive cargo into an orbit or transfer system before any descent to the surface. Atmospheric entry, landing, and surface transport are separate systems with their own energy and safety constraints. A “railgun” analogy can suggest a high-throughput transfer launcher, but a real electromagnetic launcher must manage acceleration, reaction forces, power, heat, payload survivability, and the destination's velocity and trajectory.

Do not imply that a launcher can simply fire cargo into a planetary “ballpark” without navigation or orbital mechanics. A receiving network may instead use orbital depots, tugs, momentum exchange, controlled reentry systems, or surface launch infrastructure, depending on setting technology. Mark advanced concepts as speculative or fictional.

## 12. Thermal management and waste heat

Industrial facilities convert energy and generate waste heat. In vacuum, convection is unavailable; heat must be conducted to radiators or managed through other mechanisms. Large processing centers therefore need radiating area, thermal storage or cycling, fluid loops, redundancy, and protection from micrometeoroids and contamination.

A facility's energy budget is not complete until it accounts for waste heat. More power can increase production while making thermal rejection the bottleneck. Heat signatures may also matter for detection, environmental effects, and military security, but this book does not provide concealment or targeting procedures.

## 13. Governance, property, and labor

Industrial infrastructure can be privately owned, publicly operated, cooperatively governed, chartered, or jointly administered. Resource rights, orbital access, liability, collision responsibility, labor protections, environmental stewardship, and emergency authority need explicit institutions.

A central receiving station can become a chokepoint without firing a weapon: it can control certification, storage, repair, insurance, and access to critical materials. This creates political leverage and demands oversight, redundancy, transparent accounting, and dispute resolution. Workers and local residents are stakeholders, not scenery.

## 14. Military dependence and resilience

Military forces depend on civilian industrial networks for fuel, repair, components, communications, medical care, food, and transport. Military demand can crowd out civilian needs or provide stable contracts that support otherwise fragile facilities. Dual-use infrastructure creates legal and ethical complexity; civilian status must not be erased by the presence of military customers.

Resilience includes spare capacity, alternative routes, repair crews, distributed stocks, compatible interfaces, emergency power, independent communications, and transparent priority rules. Redundancy is useful only when it is actually independent; two facilities sharing the same power source or software supply chain may fail together.

## 15. Simulation contract

Each industrial node should declare location and reference frame; owner and operator; function; throughput; input and output materials; capacity and utilization; power and thermal budgets; labor and qualification requirements; storage; maintenance state; transport interfaces; legal authority; environmental constraints; failure modes; redundancy; uncertainty; and dependencies.

Each cargo record should include mass, composition estimate, quality class, origin, custody, trajectory state and epoch, uncertainty, propulsion and service dependencies, arrival window, capture compatibility, and final inspection status. Do not merge physical state with commercial ownership or political authorization.

## 16. Playable scenarios

Scenario frames include a survey network that overestimates a deposit; a centralized refinery that becomes unavailable; a cargo tug arriving outside its receiving envelope; a processing center forced to choose between civilian water infrastructure and military alloy demand; a labor dispute that halts a critical maintenance service; and a distributed network deciding whether to accept a dangerous shipment.

Evaluate throughput, truthful uncertainty, safety culture, resilience, fair access, repairability, and governance. A spectacular megastructure is not successful if it cannot be maintained or trusted.

## Editorial and simulation principles

- Preserve provenance, uncertainty, ownership, authority, and the difference between nominal capacity and usable capacity.
- Treat civilian life, labor, environment, and political legitimacy as part of the system rather than externalities that can be ignored.
- Separate physical state from legal status, commercial ownership, organizational command, and permission to act.
- Use Established, Extrapolated, Speculative, and Fictional labels whenever future technology is discussed.
