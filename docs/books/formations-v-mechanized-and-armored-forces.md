# Formations V: Mechanized and Armored Forces

> A mechanized formation is a coordinated organization of people, machines, support systems, and decisions—not a collection of vehicle icons.

A reusable LOD 2 manual for mechanized and armored organizations across historical, contemporary, roleplaying, and speculative settings. It studies crews, vehicles, dismounted personnel, command, maintenance, recovery, supply, infrastructure, and civilian consequences as one connected system. It is historical and simulation design material, not a real-world operational or vehicle-crew training manual.

## 1. Scope and vocabulary

Mechanized forces use powered vehicles to move, protect, supply, sense, or support personnel and equipment. Armored forces place particular emphasis on protection and the ability to operate under threats, but armor is a design property and mission context, not a complete organizational identity. A force may be mechanized without being heavily armored; an armored vehicle may serve transport, reconnaissance, engineering, command, recovery, medical, or logistics roles rather than direct combat.

Distinguish:
- **Armored formation:** an organization whose vehicles and support are designed around protected mobility and mission-specific armored capabilities.
- **Mechanized infantry:** infantry whose organization depends on combat-capable or protected vehicles for movement and, depending on doctrine, support during action.
- **Motorized infantry:** infantry transported by motor vehicles, without assuming those vehicles provide the same protection or tactical role as infantry fighting vehicles.
- **Combined-arms formation:** an organization coordinating complementary capabilities rather than relying on one platform category.
- **Support formation:** maintenance, recovery, transport, engineering, medical, communications, supply, and other elements that preserve operational continuity.

Historical labels change. Avoid projecting present-day categories onto earlier armored cars, tanks, assault guns, self-propelled artillery, or improvised fighting vehicles without explaining the difference.

## 2. The formation as a socio-technical system

A vehicle formation contains people, machines, software, communications, fuel, ammunition, spare parts, tools, recovery assets, routes, and institutional authority. Its performance depends on the weakest essential links and on the ability to adapt when those links fail.

Model at least five connected layers:
1. **People:** crews, dismounted personnel, commanders, maintainers, medics, recovery teams, logisticians, and civilian specialists.
2. **Platforms:** vehicles, mission equipment, sensors, communications, protection, mobility, and power systems.
3. **Organization:** roles, command relationships, task assignments, attachment, detachment, and replacement.
4. **Sustainment:** fuel or energy, ammunition, food, water, maintenance, repair, recovery, medical support, and transport.
5. **Environment:** terrain, weather, infrastructure, threat, visibility, civilian activity, and legal constraints.

A formation should not be marked ready solely because its vehicles exist or its personnel are present. Readiness is a time-stamped assessment of mission-specific capability with known limitations.

## 3. Historical development without a false technology ladder

Mechanized warfare developed through multiple overlapping histories: industrial transport, internal-combustion engines, tracked and wheeled mobility, armor metallurgy, radio, artillery, aircraft, engineering, industrial production, and changing military institutions. Early armored cars, tractors, tracked carriers, tanks, and motor transport answered different problems. They did not form a single inevitable progression toward one universal vehicle.

Compare cases by date, region, industrial capacity, doctrine, terrain, political purpose, and available evidence. A vehicle's appearance in a photograph does not establish how common it was, how it was maintained, or how effectively it operated. Official doctrine may describe intended practice rather than actual field behavior.

## 4. Formation families and mission composition

A mechanized formation may combine:
- protected transport and infantry elements;
- armored maneuver elements;
- reconnaissance and observation assets;
- command, communications, and liaison elements;
- engineering and obstacle-support capabilities;
- maintenance, repair, and recovery teams;
- medical and evacuation capacity;
- fuel, ammunition, water, food, and general transport;
- air-defense, electronic, or other specialist support where the setting warrants it.

This is a domain taxonomy, not a prescribed tactical organization. A scenario should define its own composition, authority, constraints, and objectives. Do not imply that every force requires every category or that a named historical organization had a modern combined-arms structure.

## 5. Crew, vehicle, and dismounted identity

The crew is not a hidden property of the vehicle. Record people and platform as separate entities with explicit assignments and histories. A vehicle may survive while its crew is injured, missing, captured, or reassigned; a crew may survive while its vehicle is disabled or destroyed. Neither should silently inherit the other's state.

Useful relationships include:
- assigned crew, role, qualification, and duty status;
- vehicle identity, configuration, condition, and maintenance history;
- embarked personnel and their current dismounted status;
- attached specialists and temporary support relationships;
- command authority and the provenance of orders;
- evacuation, replacement, recovery, and reassignment events.

Dismounted infantry remain persistent participants when they leave a vehicle. They do not become cargo, vanish from the world model, or automatically reappear at a destination. Their location, condition, equipment, communication, and relationship to the parent formation remain explicit.

## 6. Vehicle roles and platform tradeoffs

A platform's role is determined by design, configuration, organization, and actual task—not by silhouette alone. Transport capacity, protection, mobility, sensor suite, communications, power generation, maintainability, and crew workload create tradeoffs. Additional armor can increase mass and affect range, bridge limits, transportability, ground pressure, and repair burden. More sensors can increase information while also increasing power, cooling, training, and data-management demands.

Avoid universal stat rankings. Compare platforms against a declared mission profile, environment, support network, and threat assumptions. A vehicle that performs well in one context may be unsuitable in another.

## 7. Geometry, spacing, and route capacity

Mechanized formations occupy physical space and impose loads on roads, bridges, ramps, tunnels, turning areas, and surfaces. Route feasibility depends on the actual platform dimensions, mass, ground interaction, turning envelope, clearance, convoy composition, weather, and infrastructure condition.

Physics owns authoritative motion, collision, materials, terrain interaction, and environmental simulation. raWWar owns the formation's mission meaning and the domain constraints that should be evaluated. This book does not prescribe real-world vehicle spacing, route selection, or movement procedures.

A route planner should expose uncertainty and assumptions. A map line is not proof that every vehicle can pass. If a bridge's capacity is unknown, the model should preserve that unknown rather than silently treating it as safe.

## 8. Command, communications, and coordination

Orders should carry issuer, recipient, scope, intent, priority, timestamp, expiry or supersession rules, and acknowledgement state. Separate order issued, transmitted, received, understood, and acted upon. A radio icon or network connection does not imply shared understanding.

Mechanized formations may depend on radio, wired systems, visual signals, messengers, data links, or autonomous coordination. Each has failure modes: interference, terrain masking, damaged antennas, power loss, incompatible systems, latency, deception, overload, and ambiguous authority. Local initiative should be modeled as a bounded institutional permission, not as universal obedience or omniscience.

When command links fail, the scenario should define which decisions remain local, what information is available, and how conflicting orders are resolved. Preserve audit history for automated recommendations and human overrides.

## 9. Dismounting, remounting, and combined movement

Dismounting changes the formation's composition and capability. It affects protection, mobility, communications, load, observation, medical exposure, and command relationships. The vehicle remains a persistent object with a crew and status; dismounted personnel remain independent entities linked to their parent element.

Represent movement and task states explicitly: embarked, moving, halted, dismount transition, dismounted, reorganization, re-embarkation, and separated or delayed. Scenario-authored transitions should identify responsible roles, time, prerequisites, interruption behavior, and failure outcomes. This book does not teach real dismount drills or vehicle procedures.

Combined movement is a coordination problem. People, vehicles, support elements, and vulnerable civilian traffic may have different speeds, access requirements, and risk tolerances. The simulation must not assume that the fastest platform determines the formation's sustainable pace.

## 10. Fuel, energy, and operational endurance

Fuel is not an abstract percentage detached from activity. Consumption varies with platform, load, terrain, idle time, temperature, maintenance condition, and mission profile. Hybrid or electric fictional platforms introduce charging, generation, storage, thermal, and power-distribution dependencies rather than eliminating logistics.

Track:
- fuel or energy type and compatibility;
- quantity, quality, contamination, and confidence;
- storage and transfer capacity;
- consumption estimates and observed usage;
- reserve policy and mission-critical loads;
- delivery time, route capacity, and handling constraints;
- competing demands from vehicles, sensors, communications, and facilities.

A vehicle with energy remaining may still be unavailable because of a damaged power system, unsafe condition, missing crew, or failed support equipment. Endurance should be estimated with uncertainty and reassessed as conditions change.

## 11. Ammunition, stores, and controlled resources

Ammunition and mission stores have identity, compatibility, condition, storage, custody, and accounting requirements. A generic inventory number hides distinctions between types, platform compatibility, environmental condition, and legal control. This book does not provide weapon loading, targeting, or employment instructions.

Simulation should separate carried capacity, usable stock, reserved stock, damaged stock, issued stock, and replenishment in transit. A transfer requires an authorized source, recipient, record, and confirmation. Loss, abandonment, capture, disposal, and unexploded hazards have persistent consequences.

The Art of Resupply owns the broader logistics model. raWWar defines the military-domain categories and scenario consequences without duplicating a general inventory or transport engine.

## 12. Maintenance, repair, and readiness

Maintenance is a continuous organizational function, not a post-battle button. Vehicles require inspection, scheduled service, fault diagnosis, repair, parts, tools, facilities, trained personnel, and time. Some faults permit limited operation; others make a platform unsafe or mission-inappropriate. The model must distinguish these states.

Readiness should be decomposed into dimensions such as:
- mobility and steering;
- propulsion and power;
- protection and structural integrity;
- sensors and communications;
- mission equipment;
- crew availability and qualification;
- safety systems;
- supply and support compatibility.

A single readiness percentage may be useful as a summary, but it must be derived from visible underlying conditions and a declared mission threshold. Do not let a high aggregate score conceal a mission-critical failure.

## 13. Recovery, salvage, and battlefield repair

Disabled does not always mean destroyed. A vehicle may be immobilized, damaged but recoverable, abandoned, captured, burned out, stripped for parts, or reduced to wreckage. These states affect people, logistics, terrain, and future missions.

Recovery requires suitable equipment, trained teams, access, time, spare capacity, and an acceptable risk decision. The simulation should represent recovery as a contested and uncertain support activity, not an instantaneous interaction prompt. Salvageable components retain identity and condition; removal changes the wreck and the source platform's record.

Do not reward reckless recovery or erase recovery personnel from the risk model. Medical evacuation and recovery of equipment are different missions with different priorities.

## 14. Repair, industrial depth, and replacement

A force's long-term capability depends on factories, depots, machine tools, technical documentation, skilled labor, component supply, transport, and quality assurance. Replacing a lost vehicle is not instantaneous even when the setting has a large economy. Production bottlenecks, sanctions, damaged infrastructure, scarce materials, incompatible parts, and political allocation all matter.

A campaign model should connect field maintenance to regional and strategic industry. Track repair queues, parts provenance, cannibalization, depot capacity, transport delay, and configuration drift. A repaired vehicle may return with different equipment or limitations; preserve its service history.

## 15. Medical support and human consequences

Mechanized environments create hazards for crews, passengers, dismounted personnel, maintainers, recovery teams, and civilians. Injury, fire, smoke, crush hazards, exposure, fatigue, hearing damage, and psychological trauma affect the whole organization. Medical capacity must not be reduced to a vehicle's health bar.

Use [Medicine and Healing](medicine-and-healing.md) and [Battlefield Medicine and the Casualty Chain](battlefield-medicine-and-the-casualty-chain.md) for casualty-care domain content. Preserve the distinctions between medical assessment, triage context, evacuation, treatment, and long-term recovery. Do not turn a player-facing HUD into a clinical authority or teach dangerous real-world procedures.

## 16. Bases, depots, and the support footprint

Mechanized formations need more than a parking area. Their support footprint may include maintenance bays, inspection areas, fuel and energy infrastructure, parts stores, recovery equipment, crew rest, sanitation, medical services, communications, security, transport interfaces, and waste handling. Different vehicle types impose different infrastructure demands.

[ Bases and Encampments](bases-and-encampments.md) and [Fortifications and Strongholds](fortifications-and-strongholds.md) cover site organization, protection, services, and endurance. A base should be modeled as a staffed service network with finite capacity, dependencies, local impacts, and continuity plans—not a passive point on the map.

## 17. Civilian infrastructure and environmental cost

Roads, bridges, railways, ports, fuel systems, power grids, workshops, and communications networks are shared with civilian life. Military use can displace traffic, damage infrastructure, consume scarce resources, create pollution, and expose workers and residents to danger. Infrastructure is not an empty tactical resource.

Model ownership, access rights, repair responsibility, civilian demand, damage, restoration, and long-term effects. A force may gain short-term mobility while undermining the transport or energy network it later needs. Local communities retain agency; they are not merely scenery or anonymous supply nodes.

## 18. Autonomous and networked vehicle systems

Modern and speculative platforms may include driver assistance, remote operation, automated navigation, sensor fusion, and autonomous functions. These capabilities require clear boundaries: what the system senses, what it infers, what it cannot know, who authorizes action, and who is accountable for failure.

Separate:
- observed sensor data from inferred state;
- recommendation from command;
- automated control from delegated authority;
- system confidence from actual correctness;
- operator acknowledgement from meaningful supervision;
- communications availability from lawful authorization.

A system should expose stale data, sensor faults, uncertainty, and degraded modes. Human oversight is not meaningful if the person cannot understand, interrupt, or contest the system. See [Autonomous Systems and Machine Agency](autonomous-systems-and-machine-agency.md) for the broader agency and accountability framework.

## 19. Training, ghost formations, and evaluation

A training visualization may render target vehicle slots, assigned roles, route envelopes, support relationships, and transition states as translucent ghost objects. The player should understand the intended task and why a placement is accepted or rejected. Gestures owns reusable embodied movement semantics; Physics owns physical motion; raWWar defines the scenario and scoring rubric.

Score distinct dimensions rather than one opaque number: role assignment, membership, spacing against scenario-defined tolerances, route feasibility, command acknowledgement, transition completion, support continuity, maintenance awareness, and response to changed conditions. Publish the rubric before play. Tracking uncertainty should pause or mark affected measures unscored rather than penalize the player for missing data.

This is a simulation design pattern, not a real vehicle-crew or tactical training program. Provide visual, audio, text, and accessible control alternatives.

## 20. Failure, degradation, and recovery of the formation

A formation can lose communications, separate from support, run short of fuel, suffer a disabled vehicle, lose crew members, encounter blocked infrastructure, or receive contradictory orders. Failure should be localized where possible: one vehicle's fault need not erase every other entity's state, but it may create a dependency failure for the larger organization.

Preserve:
- last known and current observed positions;
- crew and passenger status;
- vehicle configuration and fault history;
- command and acknowledgement history;
- supply state and estimated endurance;
- recovery and evacuation tasks;
- uncertainty and sensor provenance;
- decisions, responsible authority, and consequences.

A formation may reorganize, detach, withdraw, wait, recover, or dissolve. Those are explicit organizational events, not invisible teleportation or automatic respawn.

## 21. Scenario frames

- **Bridge capacity unknown:** the formation's route data is incomplete; players must recognize the difference between a planned route and a verified crossing.
- **Maintenance backlog:** several vehicles are present but not all are mission-ready; the scenario explores prioritization, parts, staffing, and honest readiness reporting.
- **Separated dismounts:** a transport vehicle and its dismounted personnel lose contact; preserve identity, independent status, and command uncertainty.
- **Recovery under constraint:** a disabled platform competes for limited recovery capacity with medical evacuation and civilian access.
- **Fuel-quality dispute:** deliveries arrive with uncertain provenance or compatibility; the task is evidence, custody, and safe allocation rather than a simple refill.
- **Depot under pressure:** repair, civilian power demand, medical support, and transport compete for limited infrastructure.
- **Autonomy disagreement:** an automated system recommends an action based on stale observations; participants must inspect provenance, authority, and accountability.

## 22. Evidence, historical method, and design research

Historical claims should identify period, region, institution, source type, and confidence. Distinguish contemporary records, technical manuals, photographs, production data, after-action reports, memoirs, later histories, and modern reconstructions. Each source has limits: official documents can be aspirational; memoirs selective; photographs staged; equipment tables incomplete; later summaries shaped by national memory.

Avoid treating a famous vehicle or battle as representative of an entire war. Record disagreements and uncertainty. For contemporary systems, distinguish publicly documented capability from promotional claims, prototypes, fielded equipment, and speculation. Do not infer classified performance.

## 23. Simulation contract and ownership boundaries

A mechanized formation data contract should preserve stable identifiers for people, vehicles, units, support assets, locations, and tasks. It should represent roles, assignments, configuration, condition, readiness dimensions, command provenance, supply state, maintenance events, recovery links, timestamps, and confidence.

Ownership remains explicit:
- **raWWar:** warfare-domain meaning, historical content, organization, scenario context, and evaluation.
- **Physics:** physical laws, motion, collision, terrain, materials, and environmental simulation.
- **Gestures:** reusable embodied movement and interaction semantics.
- **The Art of Resupply and related raWWar content:** domain logistics requirements and scenarios; shared runtime logistics should remain in its proper provider.
- **Host/runtime:** execution, persistence, networking, device integration, and user-facing orchestration.
- **SURPS:** optional shared descriptors, identity, LOD conventions, and composition contracts.

This sourcebook defines knowledge and requirements. It does not claim that a vehicle simulation, maintenance engine, logistics runtime, HUD, or autonomous controller is already implemented.

## 24. Glossary and research checklist

**Armored:** protected by armor; does not by itself identify mission or organization.

**Mechanized:** organized around powered vehicles for movement or mission support; distinguish from motorized transport.

**Crew:** people assigned to operate or support a platform, represented independently from the platform.

**Dismounted:** personnel operating outside their assigned vehicle while retaining identity and organizational relationships.

**Readiness:** mission-specific assessment derived from personnel, platform, supply, support, and environmental conditions.

**Recovery:** an organized effort to retrieve, stabilize, repair, or evacuate a disabled asset.

**Sustainment:** the continuing network of people, resources, services, and infrastructure that preserves capability.

Before adding a case study, record its date range, region, force institution, platform roles, crew organization, support structure, industrial context, doctrine-versus-practice evidence, civilian impact, and contested interpretations.

## Cross-domain ownership and implementation boundary

Formations V extends the formation family without collapsing mechanized, mounted, naval, or air organizations into one template. It treats vehicles as persistent assets and people as independent agents. Physics owns physical simulation; Gestures owns embodied movement; raWWar owns warfare-domain context and evaluation; SURPS composition remains opt-in. Nothing in this sourcebook authorizes real-world operational instruction or implies runtime implementation.
