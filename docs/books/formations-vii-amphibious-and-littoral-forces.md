# Formations VII: Amphibious and Littoral Forces

> An amphibious formation is a coordinated system that crosses domains while preserving people, authority, sustainment, and mission identity.

This LOD 2 sourcebook studies formations whose organization depends on the connection between maritime and land environments. It includes amphibious forces, littoral organizations, riverine and estuarine operations, coastal support, port and beach logistics, and the transition between afloat and ashore systems. It is a domain reference and simulation-design framework, not a real-world landing plan or tactical manual.

## 1. Scope and definition

Amphibious and littoral formations are organized groups whose mission, movement, sustainment, or authority crosses the maritime-shore boundary. The littoral is not a narrow strip of beach. It includes coastal waters, ports, estuaries, islands, river mouths, shore infrastructure, nearshore terrain, and the communities and economic systems connected to them.

An amphibious formation may be temporary and mission-specific or persistent as a standing joint organization. It may include naval vessels, landing craft, aircraft, ground forces, engineers, medical services, logistics, communications, civilian agencies, and host-community partners. Its identity is not reducible to the ship carrying it or the unit that first steps ashore.

## 2. Evidence labels and historical care

Use four labels for claims about technology and future settings:
- **Established:** supported by historical evidence, known physical principles, or documented capabilities.
- **Extrapolated:** a plausible extension whose operational details remain uncertain.
- **Speculative:** dependent on unresolved engineering, economics, governance, or social assumptions.
- **Fictional:** invented as an explicit setting rule.

Historical accounts must distinguish plans, orders, capabilities, actual practice, later recollection, and propaganda. Avoid presenting a single famous landing as the universal template. Amphibious warfare varies with geography, technology, institutions, weather, local populations, and political purpose.

## 3. The formation as a cross-domain system

A useful model separates:
1. **People and organizations:** crews, embarked forces, shore units, specialists, medical teams, civilians, authorities, and partners.
2. **Platforms and assets:** ships, boats, landing craft, aircraft, vehicles, engineering equipment, communications, and support facilities.
3. **Spaces and transitions:** sea areas, waterways, ports, beaches, marshes, urban waterfronts, roads, and inland connections.
4. **Command and authority:** mission command, ship command, landing-force leadership, safety authority, host-state authority, and civil governance.
5. **Sustainment:** cargo, fuel, water, food, repair, medical evacuation, transport capacity, and shore distribution.
6. **Information:** charts, surveys, weather, hydrography, infrastructure condition, civilian movement, and uncertainty.

These layers must remain separately inspectable. A force can retain combat power while losing access to the port, medical chain, communications, or political permission required to continue its mission.

## 4. The sea-to-shore transition

The defining challenge is a transition between environments with different movement constraints, infrastructure, hazards, and command relationships. Personnel and materiel may pass through multiple platforms and custody states before reaching a destination.

Represent each transition explicitly: embarked, staged, loaded, in transit, transferred, received, distributed, returned, or evacuated. The labels are illustrative state categories, not a prescribed operating sequence. Record custody, responsibility, time, condition, and uncertainty at each handoff.

A unit does not become a new unit simply because it changes transport. Preserve identity, personnel relationships, equipment assignment, and command provenance across the transition.

## 5. Formation families

Amphibious and littoral formations can include:
- embarked ground-force organizations;
- maritime transport and support groups;
- landing and transfer services;
- coastal engineering and infrastructure teams;
- riverine and estuarine units;
- air and surface transport coordination;
- medical and evacuation networks;
- port, harbor, and shore-support organizations;
- civil-military liaison and humanitarian support;
- autonomous survey, transport, and service assets.

These are functional families, not a universal order of battle. A small island relief mission, a historical expedition, a river crossing, and a fictional planetary shoreline may require very different structures.

## 6. Embarked forces and platform independence

Embarked personnel remain people and members of their own organizations. A vessel provides transport, protection, services, and constraints; it does not own the identity or agency of everyone aboard.

Track embarked strength, duty status, qualifications, equipment custody, medical needs, and disembarkation status separately from the vessel's condition. A platform may be delayed or unavailable while its embarked organization remains intact. Conversely, a unit may be ashore while still depending on afloat support.

## 7. Landing craft, boats, and transfer assets

Transfer assets connect systems that may have incompatible interfaces, capacities, or operating limits. Record capacity, accessibility, payload constraints, maintenance state, operator, communications, and the support needed to load and unload.

Avoid treating a landing craft as a generic teleport between ship and land. Transfer capacity is finite; turnaround, handling, weather, damage, and shore access affect throughput. Detailed real-world launch, approach, beaching, and tactical employment procedures are outside this sourcebook's scope.

## 8. Coastal geography and environmental conditions

Coastal environments are dynamic and varied. Shoreline shape, tides, currents, waves, sediment, reefs, wetlands, cliffs, river flow, weather, visibility, and seasonal change affect access and infrastructure. A beach, harbor, estuary, and urban waterfront are not interchangeable terrain tiles.

Physics owns authoritative environmental and physical simulation. raWWar owns the formation's organizational interpretation, mission context, and scenario requirements. A visualization should expose the source and age of terrain or environmental data rather than imply that a static map is current truth.

## 9. Ports, harbors, beaches, and shore infrastructure

A port is a system of channels, berths, cranes, storage, roads, utilities, security, labor, governance, and maintenance. A beach is not automatically a logistics facility. Shore access may depend on public infrastructure, private ownership, environmental restrictions, local authorities, and civilian use.

Represent infrastructure as persistent assets with condition, capacity, ownership, dependencies, and service history. Damage or closure can have consequences far beyond the immediate formation. Avoid reducing a functioning town or port to an uninhabited objective marker.

## 10. Riverine, estuarine, and inland-water formations

Rivers and estuaries create their own connected environments. Water depth, flow, seasonal flooding, bridges, banks, navigation, sediment, local transport, and settlement patterns shape movement and support. Inland waterways can connect coastal and interior systems, but do not behave like open sea.

Riverine formations may combine boats, shore elements, engineers, medical support, local knowledge, and land transport. Preserve the distinction between river transport, naval forces, amphibious formations, and ordinary ground units that happen to operate near water.

## 11. Command across organizations

Amphibious formations often bring together organizations with different chains of command, legal authorities, professional cultures, and safety responsibilities. Coordination does not automatically create unified command.

Record who owns each decision, who may request action, who must approve it, who controls a platform, and who has responsibility for civilians and infrastructure. Distinguish legal authority, mission command, technical control, safety authority, and liaison. A ship commander, embarked commander, shore authority, and humanitarian coordinator may have overlapping but non-identical responsibilities.

## 12. Communications and shared understanding

The formation depends on a common but imperfect picture of people, assets, weather, shore conditions, infrastructure, and task status. Information may be delayed, contradictory, inaccessible, or deliberately withheld. Different organizations may use different terminology and reporting standards.

Every important report should preserve source, timestamp, location reference, confidence, and whether it is observed, inferred, planned, or unknown. A shared display is not proof that all participants agree or possess the same authority.

## 13. Sustainment and throughput

Amphibious sustainment is a chain of capacity-limited transfers. Cargo must be assembled, loaded, transported, received, sorted, stored, and delivered to the actual point of need. Each interface can become a bottleneck.

Track demand, priority, handling time, storage, compatibility, loss, spoilage, repair, and onward distribution. Distinguish cargo present afloat from cargo available ashore and cargo delivered to the receiving unit. The [Art of Resupply](the-art-of-resupply.md) owns the wider logistics discipline; this volume supplies littoral-specific interfaces and cases.

## 14. Engineering, recovery, and infrastructure work

Engineering organizations may assess, repair, clear, protect, or restore infrastructure. Their tasks interact with utilities, transport, environmental systems, local labor, and public access. Engineering work is not a magical action that instantly changes terrain or restores a port.

Represent work as a bounded project with people, equipment, permissions, materials, hazards, dependencies, and verification. Recovery of vehicles and equipment should preserve ownership, condition, custody, and maintenance history. Do not provide real-world breaching, demolition, obstacle-clearing, or sabotage instructions.

## 15. Medical support and casualty movement

Medical support crosses the same interfaces as every other service, but with time-sensitive human consequences. A casualty may move through shipboard care, transfer assets, shore facilities, civilian hospitals, and onward recovery. Each handoff must preserve identity, consent where applicable, observations, treatment history, and responsibility.

A medical evacuation route is not guaranteed by the existence of a vessel or facility. Weather, capacity, access, communications, and clinical needs matter. Use [Battlefield Medicine and the Casualty Chain](battlefield-medicine-and-the-casualty-chain.md) for triage, care, medic roles, and medical simulation boundaries.

## 16. Civilian populations and coastal economies

Coastal spaces are lived-in environments. Ports support employment, food, transport, energy, fishing, tourism, migration, and emergency services. Communities may be displaced, isolated, endangered, or economically disrupted by the presence or actions of a formation.

Track civilian presence and services as more than scenery or a generic morale modifier. Distinguish residents, workers, visitors, displaced people, public authorities, private operators, and humanitarian organizations. Include consent, property, access, environmental effects, cultural sites, and continuity of essential services.

## 17. Humanitarian, disaster-response, and evacuation missions

Amphibious capabilities may support disaster relief, evacuation, medical assistance, infrastructure restoration, and delivery of supplies. Such missions still require coordination, local consent, prioritization, accountability, and respect for civilian leadership.

A uniformed organization does not automatically become the lead civil authority. Distinguish assistance requested by local authorities from unilateral intervention. Model the limits of transport capacity, information, medical resources, and local distribution networks. Humanitarian scenarios should not become combat scenarios by default.

## 18. Security, law, and escalation

The legal setting may involve territorial sovereignty, port state authority, navigational rights, environmental rules, armed conflict law, neutrality, rescue duties, and agreements with local governments. The relevant legal framework depends on context; this sourcebook is not legal advice.

Ambiguous movement, contested access, civilian infrastructure, and mixed military-commercial use can create escalation risks. Include communication, verification, mediation, notification, and restraint as meaningful options. Do not assume that every coastal population, government, military, or company is a unified actor.

## 19. Historical comparison without a single template

Historical amphibious operations differ across ancient, medieval, early modern, industrial, and contemporary settings. Their organization reflects available ships, boats, ports, local transport, political institutions, disease environments, labor systems, and the capacity to sustain forces after arrival.

Compare cases through explicit dimensions: purpose, participants, geography, transport, command, supply, local agency, environmental conditions, duration, outcomes, and source quality. Distinguish planned capacity from demonstrated performance. Avoid reading modern doctrinal categories backward into periods that did not share them.

## 20. Littoral formations in fictional and future settings

Fantasy and science-fiction settings can extend the domain with unusual coastlines, floating settlements, artificial seas, underwater habitats, mobile ports, or shore-to-orbit interfaces. Label invented physical assumptions and keep their consequences internally consistent.

A floating city is not simply a ship; an underwater habitat is not simply a submerged fort; a planetary ocean may have different gravity, atmosphere, tides, chemistry, and biology. Extrapolated or fictional environments should change the formation's infrastructure and support assumptions rather than merely its visual style.

## 21. Autonomous and uncrewed systems

Uncrewed surface, underwater, aerial, and shore systems may support observation, transport, maintenance, or communication. Their role, authority, sensor confidence, operating limits, and accountability must be explicit.

An autonomous system does not become a commander because it can navigate or transmit. Preserve delegated scope, human oversight, safe-state behavior, communications loss, identification uncertainty, and audit history. See [Autonomous Systems and Machine Agency](autonomous-systems-and-machine-agency.md) for the wider agency and responsibility framework.

## 22. Training, ghost formations, and assessment

Training visualizations can represent embarked organizations, transfer links, shore receiving capacity, infrastructure dependencies, task ownership, and uncertainty as ghost elements. Keep physical location distinct from organizational membership and cargo custody.

Assessment may evaluate coordination, reporting quality, handoff integrity, sustainment awareness, civilian-impact recognition, and response to changing conditions. Score only against information available to the participant at the time. Missing or unreliable tracking should produce an explicit unknown or unscored state, not an invented failure.

Gestures owns reusable embodied interaction; Physics owns physical and environmental simulation; raWWar owns domain context, scenarios, and assessment meaning.

## 23. Simulation states and event history

A formation contract should preserve stable identities for people, units, platforms, transfer assets, infrastructure, cargo, and authorities. Useful state includes role, operator, command relationship, location, environment, capacity, condition, custody, access, support dependencies, confidence, and timestamp.

Events may include embarkation, assignment, loading, transfer, receipt, task request, authorization, infrastructure assessment, service interruption, evacuation, repair, and mission handoff. Keep event provenance and corrections. A cargo item can be aboard a vessel, transferred to shore, and still not delivered to the intended recipient.

## 24. Failure, fragmentation, and recovery

Failures include weather disruption, unavailable transfer assets, port closure, damaged infrastructure, communication loss, incompatible cargo, medical evacuation delays, disputed authority, and interrupted shore distribution. Failures may be local or cascade across the formation.

Model graceful degradation, alternate services, mutual aid, repair, return, and mission termination. Do not treat a disrupted landing or transfer as automatic destruction of every embarked unit. Preserve people, platforms, cargo, and organizational identity independently.

## 25. Scenario frames

- **Harbor service interruption:** a port loses a critical service while commercial and emergency users still depend on it.
- **Island relief network:** multiple communities require supplies through limited transport and local distribution capacity.
- **River access dispute:** local authorities, transport operators, and a visiting formation disagree about access and priorities.
- **Evacuation capacity:** available transport cannot move everyone at once; transparent prioritization and civilian authority matter.
- **Embarked force delay:** the transport platform is unavailable, but the embarked organization remains intact and must reassess its commitments.
- **Shore infrastructure assessment:** incomplete reports conflict about the condition and ownership of a critical facility.
- **Autonomous survey handoff:** a robotic system reports uncertain environmental data that human teams must validate.
- **Environmental restoration:** a mission must balance urgent access with the long-term effects on wetlands, fisheries, and public infrastructure.

## 26. Research method and implementation boundaries

Research should combine maritime and military history, coastal geography, logistics, civil engineering, environmental science, public administration, humanitarian practice, and law. Record region, period, source type, author perspective, and uncertainty. Separate doctrine, plans, actual operations, retrospective narratives, and popular memory.

Ownership:
- **raWWar:** amphibious and littoral formation meaning, organization, historical context, scenarios, and evaluation.
- **Physics:** physical motion, water and weather environments, terrain, materials, and environmental simulation.
- **Gestures:** reusable embodied interaction and movement semantics.
- **The Art of Resupply:** general logistics concepts; this volume supplies cross-domain transfer requirements.
- **Bases and Encampments / Fortifications:** persistent facilities, camps, and defensive infrastructure.
- **Host/runtime:** persistence, networking, device integration, and execution.
- **SURPS:** optional shared identity, LOD, descriptors, and composition conventions.

This sourcebook does not claim that amphibious simulation, navigation, logistics, autonomous control, or infrastructure restoration has been implemented. It excludes real-world landing plans, tactical procedures, targeting, sabotage, and methods for harming people or infrastructure.

## 27. Glossary and research checklist

**Amphibious:** involving coordinated activity across maritime and land environments.

**Littoral:** the connected coastal region where maritime, land, infrastructure, and human systems interact.

**Embarked:** carried aboard a platform while retaining separate organizational and personal identity.

**Transfer asset:** a platform or service that moves people or materiel between otherwise distinct systems.

**Throughput:** the rate at which a constrained process or network can transfer useful people, goods, or services.

**Custody:** responsibility for an item or person during a defined period or handoff.

**Shore infrastructure:** facilities and networks that support access, movement, utilities, services, and public life along a coast.

**Graceful degradation:** preserving safe, reduced function when a component or relationship fails.

Before adding a case study, record the period and region, mission purpose, participants, command authorities, platforms, shore interfaces, environmental assumptions, transfer capacities, logistics, medical chain, civilian dependencies, legal context, source quality, and unresolved uncertainty.

## Cross-domain ownership and implementation boundary

Formations VII treats the sea-to-shore boundary as a system of people, organizations, assets, infrastructure, and handoffs. It is not a synonym for naval warfare or a chapter of land formations. raWWar owns the domain knowledge and scenarios; Physics owns physical simulation; Gestures owns embodied interaction; logistics and facility providers own their respective shared domains; SURPS remains opt-in.
