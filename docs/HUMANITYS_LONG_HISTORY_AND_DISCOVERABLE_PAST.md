# raWWar — Humanity's Long History and the Discoverable Past

> **The campaign is not the whole story. It is where the player enters a civilization that has already lived for a very long time.**

Status: Candidate historical framework. Earth is humanity's origin. The campaign-era Empire, Empress, warp drives requiring days to initialize and prepare for a jump, major opposing factions, and continuous galactic war are established setting facts. The chronology, named historical powers, technologies, and specific ancient events below remain open until deliberately authored.

## 1. The scale of the promise

We need a connected history from humanity's present to the player's era: Earth, permanent off-world life, expansion, interstellar settlement, civilization networks, hegemonies, imperial institutions, rebellions, civil wars, successor states, and the political order the player inhabits.

The timeline must be long enough for people to forget why a war began, for an industrial standard to outlive the government that created it, for a colony to become a rival civilization, for a dynasty to claim an inheritance that its own archives disprove, and for a wreck to remain dangerous long after every living witness is gone.

This is not a prediction of the future. It is a framework for speculative history. Resist turning present-day nation-states, economics, ideologies, interfaces, or engineering assumptions into permanent templates. The future may transform them beyond recognition. The method remains: changes have causes, systems have constraints, and consequences persist.

## 2. One chronology, many histories

[Human Galactic History](../data/human-galactic-history.json) establishes a broad era framework from Earth's present to the campaign-era galactic order. Absolute dates remain deliberately open.

| Historical layer | What changes | What survives |
|---|---|---|
| Earth-origin era | Humanity begins its branching future from Earth | Earth as origin, memory, symbol, disputed inheritance |
| Permanent off-world societies | Habitats become communities and institutions | Life-support standards, legal disputes, off-world identities |
| Inner-system network | Settlements specialize and depend on routes and infrastructure | Industrial dependencies, access rights, old contracts |
| Deep-space threshold | Human settlement reaches other star systems by mechanisms not yet fixed | Divergent calendars, institutions, language, technical practices |
| First interstellar polities | Affiliation, autonomy, secession, absorption, and reunification compete | Shared ancestry and incompatible claims |
| Galactic web | Routes, hubs, and networks integrate many systems unevenly | Strategic chokepoints, inequality, old infrastructure |
| First galactic hegemonies | A power establishes authority across a wide region | Tribute, law, military structures, legitimacy claims |
| Imperial fractures | Rebellion, succession crises, civil wars, and regional breaks reshape power | Veterans, lost worlds, propaganda, broken routes, rival archives |
| Successor civilizations | New powers inherit selected institutions and memories | Familiar interfaces with different meanings, disputed heritage |
| Campaign-era order | The Empress's Empire and its opposing factions fight a continuing war | Unresolved history becomes present-day consequence |

These are eras, not a railroad. There may be several hegemonies, overlapping civil wars, regional renaissances, failed expansions, and technological reversals. One region's golden age may coincide with another's occupation or collapse.

## 3. Science fiction without present-day blinders

We need causal rigor without claiming today's worldview predicts the distant future.

- **Do not preselect one inevitable breakthrough.** Warp drives exist in the campaign era, with days of initialization and preparation; their exact physics remain open. Earlier history can explore competing pathways.
- **A capability is not a universal solution.** Faster travel would not automatically remove capacity, access, energy, maintenance, safety, control, or ownership constraints.
- **Inventions have adoption histories.** Discovery, demonstration, qualification, industrialization, training, deployment, and routine maintenance are distinct events.
- **Institutions may be unfamiliar.** Future governance, identity, labor, computation, family, property, warfare, and collective decision-making need not resemble modern equivalents.
- **Humanity will not develop uniformly.** A remote society may be extraordinary in one domain and dependent on inherited or improvised systems in another.
- **The old world can be alien without being supernatural.** Obsolete interfaces, transformed meanings, incompatible data formats, changed environments, unfamiliar social protocols, and incomprehensible scale can make human history feel strange.
- **Not every ancient artifact is a weapon or an upgrade.** It may be evidence, infrastructure, a legal claim, a cultural practice, a maintenance procedure, or a warning about a forgotten failure.

A technology should change who can build and maintain it, which skills matter, what infrastructure it needs, how it changes travel and command, what failure looks like, and which institutions gain or lose power. When science remains speculative, document assumptions and consequences separately from established facts.

## 4. The long political arc

The Empire needs a history, not just a throne. Its authority might emerge from protection, route security, infrastructure, law, conquest, dynastic legitimacy, resource control, debt, negotiated federation, technological asymmetry, or a crisis in which people accepted powers they later regretted granting.

Rebellions should not all be morally identical. Some demand representation; some seek independence, a replacement ruler, restoration of a predecessor, or a future the old political language cannot describe. Civil wars divide families, command chains, industrial standards, supply networks, and historical records—not only territory.

For every major conflict, establish:
1. What changed materially or politically before violence began?
2. Who benefited from the existing order, and who paid its costs?
3. What did each actor believe was happening?
4. What did logistics, communication, industry, and geography permit?
5. What turned tension into an irreversible crisis?
6. What did the fighting destroy, displace, standardize, or make possible?
7. Who wrote the official account, who preserved a rival account, and why?
8. What remains unresolved in the player's era?

A war's declared cause may differ from its actual trigger. Both matter.

## 5. The galaxy remembers physically

History leaves persistent traces:
- A forgotten colony leaves foundations, altered terrain, orbital debris, buried utility networks, and incompatible industrial equipment.
- A lost fleet leaves a debris field whose trajectories and damage patterns contradict the published battle report.
- A successor faction still uses inherited connectors and manufacturing standards while rejecting its predecessor's doctrine.
- A destroyed ship preserves maintenance logs showing that an ignored equipment failure preceded a supposedly decisive battle.
- An abandoned station still executes an obsolete evacuation protocol because no one formally revoked it.
- A civil war survives as incompatible calendars, duplicated legal registries, contested memorials, different names for the same event, and families holding different pieces of the record.

Authoritative world history, physical evidence, surviving records, faction interpretations, and player knowledge are separate things. Their differences are part of the story.

## 6. Discovery opens a much larger history

Imagine the player reaches a devastated colony or boards an old warship. At first, they see material evidence: wreckage, damage direction, emergency seals, abandoned equipment, personal objects, and signs that the official story is incomplete. The memory core is not simply a button that plays a cinematic. It is a physical system that may need power, repair, access, translation, and interpretation.

Once accessible, it might expose a vast, navigable archive: command traffic, engineering events, crew and civilian records, sensor history, supply manifests, medical logs, private messages, scientific observations, legal orders, and personal memories. The player can search by person, unit, system, location, date, event, material, or political claim. A timeline can be reconstructed from sources that disagree. A maintenance record can link to a colony's industrial history; the colony's evacuation can link to a succession crisis; that crisis can undermine the legal basis of a present-day war.

The feeling of near-limitless depth comes from interconnected records and meaningful avenues of inquiry—not endless prose. Each record can suggest adjacent records, missing volumes, redacted branches, other archives, and unresolved contradictions.

### Layered access

1. **Immediate evidence:** Visible damage, markings, objects, environmental clues.
2. **Operational reconstruction:** Logs, sensor data, maintenance history, crew lists, and causal timelines.
3. **Historical context:** Connections to older people, institutions, conflicts, and lineages.
4. **Contested interpretation:** Conflicting accounts, hidden orders, propaganda, and uncertainty.
5. **Deep archive:** Searchable records with provenance, cross-references, and paths into related history.

The player never has to read everything. They can follow one thread, return later, compare evidence, or leave it untouched. Access is gameplay: power restoration, repair, security, translation, permissions, and decisions about custody may require work.

## 7. An archive must preserve uncertainty

Distinguish what happened, what a recorder captured, what survived, what was altered, what the author knew, what a faction later claimed, what the player verified, and what remains unresolved.

Summaries should link to supporting records where available. Conflicting sources should remain visibly in conflict rather than silently selecting a winner. The player might prove one account is propaganda—or discover that the evidence cannot establish a definitive answer.

Information can change the world: weaken an Imperial claim, reveal an evacuation route, locate a forgotten settlement, expose a commander's crime, validate a grievance, or create a research question. Recovered knowledge still requires qualified people, tools, materials, testing, and production before it becomes a deployed capability.

## 8. Reveal history by relevance, not encyclopedia order

Campaign authors decide which historical questions matter; which discoveries are available, hidden, damaged, or contested; what the player knows initially; which records are required versus optional; which discoveries change objectives or politics; what can be missed safely; and which facts are canon or campaign-variant.

One player may investigate the technical cause of a ship's destruction; another identifies the people aboard; another traces a legal claim; another exposes the political order that caused the battle. These paths can converge without being identical.

## 10. The next canon pass

First establish major era boundaries and causal event order. Then decide which events are fixed and which can vary. Only then name dynasties, empires, rebellions, civil wars, and successor factions—and connect their history to ship architecture, industrial standards, settlement geography, language, doctrine, archives, and the current Imperial crisis.

Do not lock a precise far-future date merely to make the timeline look authoritative. Build the causal history and the duration its consequences require; assign dates when they improve coherence and play.

> **The player discovers surviving pieces of a history that continued long before them—and will continue after they leave.**
