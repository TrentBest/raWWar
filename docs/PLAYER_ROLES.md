# raWWar — Player Roles

**Status:** Living role catalogue; not a finalized class system.  
**Owner:** raWWar Experience design.  
**Audience:** Designers, content authors, and engineers modelling meaningful participation.  
**Evidence rule:** A role described here is an intended player opportunity, not proof of an implemented occupation or qualification system.

This document records roles already described by the creator. It is not a final class system.

## Soldier

The soldier is the foundational player role.

Possible activities include training, qualifying on equipment, accepting missions, deploying with a squad, marching with the unit, combat, downtime, recreation, and specialized work.

## Officer / Commander

Commanders operate from a first-person command environment.

They oversee staff and advisors, direct research, design bases, arrange facilities and training capacity, commission military reviews, address troops, make strategic decisions, and potentially enter combat personally.

## Cooperative participant

Cooperative play allows multiple players to inhabit complementary roles within the same campaign.

The design should avoid making every cooperative player a clone of the same combat role.

## Researcher

Researchers operate laboratories and conduct experiments.

The player may physically perform procedural tasks and record findings.

## Mechanic

Mechanics repair damaged equipment and vehicles through physical interaction.

## Construction squad

Construction players transport and assemble structural components.

## Logistics / Transport

Players can move materials such as construction plates to where they are needed.

## Pilot

Pilots operate aircraft.

## Spacecraft crew

Players can occupy weapons stations, tactical displays, helm positions, command positions, and other ship systems.

## Fleet command

Players can command ships and fleets from inside the command structure of a vessel.

## Recreation

Downtime supports social play and diegetic games such as arcade machines and simulated air hockey.

The important design rule is that these roles should feel like occupations inside the world, not menu selections that teleport the player into isolated mini-games.

## Additional roles and progression concepts

### Intelligence / Sabotage

Players can qualify as spies or saboteurs, and commanders can deploy them as part of military operations.

### Ground crew

Ground crew keep aircraft and other advanced systems operational. Staffing is a qualification problem: equipment can remain unavailable when enough qualified personnel are not present.

### Staff and facility personnel

Large bases contain the people required to operate facilities, maintain infrastructure, perform research, move resources, and enforce procedures. Their qualifications contribute to organizational readiness.

### Qualifications as the common progression system

Roles are not intended to form a fixed class list. Qualifications are the mechanism by which soldiers gain access to new occupations, equipment, facilities, and opportunities.

## Civilian and commercial participation

The galaxy is not exclusively a military institution. Civilian and commercial lives take place in the same persistent universe as military campaigns, sharing its locations, history, organizations, infrastructure, events, and consequences. A war can reshape a region without making military service the only meaningful way to inhabit that region—or the galaxy.

The following are candidate role examples drawn from the creator's direction. They extend the living catalogue; they are not a finalized occupation list or claims of implemented systems.

### Freight pilot

A freight pilot personally operates a vessel and transports cargo between locations. Their experience may involve route choice, navigation, cargo handling, delivery commitments, hazards, and the changing conditions of the route.

### Passenger-ship captain and crew

A captain is responsible for a passenger vessel and its voyage. Crew members may perform specialized work aboard it. A journey through the outer rim can be a meaningful civilian undertaking even while military events unfold elsewhere—or affect the route.

### Pirate

A pirate may conceal a vessel in an asteroid field, exploit vulnerable routes, evade pursuit, or otherwise operate outside lawful organizations. This is a possible way to exist in the same world, not a separate outlaw-only simulation.

### Commercial operator and owner

A player may own or direct a cruise line, freight carrier, shipping company, shipyard, or other organization. They may prefer to make organizational decisions rather than personally pilot each vessel or perform each task. Potential areas of responsibility include routes, schedules, contracts, service, fleet investment, staffing, maintenance, risk, and reputation. These are design domains to explore, not settled economic rules.

### Employee, contractor, and customer

Players may participate in organizations without owning or directing them. They may work aboard a vessel, maintain equipment, fulfil a delivery contract, provide a specialist service, or travel as a customer. These relationships should create meaningful participation rather than treating non-owners as background characters.

## Agency is independent of occupation

An occupation describes a person's relationship to work or responsibility. It does not dictate how much of an organization or activity the player must personally control.

The same shipping operation could be experienced at several scales:

- **Direct operator:** personally pilots a vessel or performs a task.
- **Captain or supervisor:** directs a crew and is accountable for a voyage or operation.
- **Employee or contractor:** contributes a defined service within another person's organization.
- **Owner or executive:** establishes priorities, invests resources, delegates work, and accepts organizational consequences.
- **Competitor:** responds to other organizations and competes for customers, routes, contracts, or reputation.
- **Customer or observer:** travels, purchases a service, witnesses events, or chooses where to participate without controlling the organization.

These are relationships to an activity, not mutually exclusive player classes. A player may move between them over time. The player should not be forced to personally operate every asset simply because the world simulates that asset, nor should choosing organizational control detach the player from the shared world.

The same principle applies to military organizations. A player might serve in a unit, command it, own a supplier, contract to transport its materials, or be a civilian affected by its operations. These perspectives can intersect while retaining different responsibilities and incentives.

## Shared-world obligations

Different scales of agency must not create separate authoritative versions of the universe. If a company schedules a vessel, that voyage belongs to the same world in which pilots, crew, passengers, competitors, pirates, and military forces act. Changes in conflict, infrastructure, access, supply, or local conditions may affect all of them.

Delegation and abstraction may change what the player directly operates and how decisions are presented. They must not erase the underlying people, assets, events, or consequences. Where an organization acts through personnel or automated processes, the design should eventually make responsibility and outcomes legible rather than pretending the player personally performed every action.

## Illumination Needed — organizations and economic agency

The creator has established that players may prefer to compete as shipping or cruise-line operators rather than personally captain ships. The detailed rules remain open. Before implementation, illuminate:

- what it means to found, own, join, leave, or inherit an organization;
- how authority, delegation, accountability, and succession work;
- which decisions are made directly by players and which can be delegated;
- how vessels, crews, facilities, contracts, customers, and other assets relate to an organization;
- how prices, wages, operating costs, scarcity, trade, credit, and profit work, if they exist;
- how organizations compete and cooperate without reducing every interaction to a menu or leaderboard;
- how piracy, law, insurance, security, and military intervention affect civilian operations;
- how an organization persists and acts when its owner or members are offline.

Until these questions are illuminated, do not invent a complete economy, ownership model, or management interface. Preserve the design distinction: one shared universe, multiple occupations, and multiple scales of agency.
