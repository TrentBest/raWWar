# raWWar — Faction Design

**Status:** Living design model.  
**Canon:** 13 major factions; all oppose the Empress's will in some meaningful way.  
**Candidate:** Names, detailed doctrines, histories, and exact relationships remain subject to refinement.

> **Thirteen factions is not thirteen teams. It is thirteen answers to the question: what should this galaxy become?**

## 1. The political premise

raWWar is set in a galaxy containing an Imperial order ruled by an Empress whose authority reaches across many star systems.

The Empress is powerful enough that the major factions cannot simply ignore her.

They nevertheless do.

The central political structure is therefore deliberately asymmetric:

**Empress / Empire**
versus
**Thirteen major factions**

The factions are not a neat alliance.

They compete with one another, make temporary agreements, betray one another, trade, raid, research, conquer, defend, and fight.

Some secretly cooperate with the Empress.

Some openly oppose her.

Some hate the Empire but still prefer it to another faction winning.

Some would replace the Empire with something arguably better.

Some would replace it with something considerably worse.

The galaxy is therefore not a two-sided war.

It is a constantly changing political ecosystem.

## 2. Why thirteen?

Three major houses are sufficient for a dramatic triangle, but a galaxy-spanning war should not feel as though the number of meaningful powers was chosen because it was easier to implement.

Thirteen gives the simulation room for:

- shifting coalitions;
- regional powers;
- asymmetric conflicts;
- proxy wars;
- temporary alliances;
- economic competition;
- ideological conflict;
- civil wars;
- betrayals;
- factions fighting while the player is occupied elsewhere;
- several factions having incompatible relationships with the same third faction.

The number is deliberately odd.

There should never be a requirement for the galaxy to resolve itself into two equal political blocks.

> **There is no final team balance. There is only the current state of the galaxy.**

Thirteen is also a design starting point, not a technological ceiling.

The Workshop should not require the universe to remain at thirteen factions forever.

## 3. The hidden alignment gradient

The player is introduced to faction selection physically.

There are thirteen doors.

The player is not shown:

- an alignment meter;
- a faction ranking;
- an Imperial loyalty score;
- a good/evil chart;
- a recommended faction;
- a warning that one door is politically dangerous.

Instead, the doors are presented as choices.

The order is meaningful.

Internally, the factions are arranged from:

**1 — most aligned with the Empress**

through

**7 — politically independent / difficult to classify**

to

**13 — openly hostile to the Empress**

The player is not told this.

If the player races to the first door because it is the first door they see, they may accidentally choose the faction most willing to serve Imperial interests.

If they deliberately explore all thirteen, they can discover the political spectrum through the physical offices, literature, people, architecture, recruitment material, military doctrine, and conversations.

This creates a subtle first lesson:

> **The game does not tell the player what a faction believes. The world shows them.**

The ordering is a hidden design property, not an explicit player-facing stat.

## 4. The thirteen factions

The following names are working names. Their roles are more important than the names at this stage.

### I — House Veyr
**The Imperial Loyalists**

Veyr believes stability is more valuable than freedom.

They cooperate with Imperial authority openly and believe the Empress is the legitimate guarantor of civilization.

They still oppose the Empress in the sense that Veyr has its own sovereignty, military, wealth, and ambitions. They want Imperial favor without surrendering their own power.

**Public face:** order, civilization, continuity.  
**Military character:** disciplined, conventional, heavily organized.  
**Economic character:** stable, hierarchical, Imperial trade.  
**Relationship with Empress:** openly cooperative.  
**Internal danger:** becoming an Imperial instrument.

---

### II — House Cael
**The Administrators**

Cael believes civilization survives through competent administration.

They cooperate with the Empire because Imperial bureaucracy provides scale, infrastructure, standards, and predictable law.

They resist Imperial interference when it becomes inefficient or destabilizing.

**Public face:** competence and prosperity.  
**Military character:** logistics, organization, defensive warfare.  
**Economic character:** infrastructure, trade, planning.  
**Relationship with Empress:** cooperative but transactional.  
**Internal danger:** believing every human problem can be administered away.

---

### III — House Oris
**The Industrialists**

Oris believes whoever controls production controls history.

Their power comes from factories, shipyards, mines, materials science, logistics, and manufacturing expertise.

They tolerate the Empress because war is profitable and Imperial contracts are enormous.

They resist when Imperial demands interfere with industrial independence.

**Public face:** progress through production.  
**Military character:** equipment superiority and sustained industrial warfare.  
**Economic character:** manufacturing and resource control.  
**Relationship with Empress:** commercially aligned.  
**Internal danger:** reducing people to production inputs.

---

### IV — House Sere
**The Technologists**

Sere believes knowledge should determine power.

They are researchers, engineers, experimentalists, and technological radicals.

They will work with the Empire when it funds discovery, but Imperial restrictions become intolerable when they interfere with research.

**Public face:** advancement and discovery.  
**Military character:** experimental equipment, sensors, automation, advanced systems.  
**Economic character:** technology licensing and research.  
**Relationship with Empress:** useful but increasingly difficult.  
**Internal danger:** treating ethics as an obstacle to discovery.

---

### V — House Avar
**The Traditionalists**

Avar believes civilizations require inherited institutions, rituals, family structures, and cultural continuity.

They are not necessarily Imperial loyalists.

They oppose rapid Imperial homogenization because they believe every culture has the right to remain itself.

**Public face:** heritage and continuity.  
**Military character:** disciplined, ceremonial, defensive, locally adapted.  
**Economic character:** estates, agriculture, heritage industries, local commerce.  
**Relationship with Empress:** respectful but resistant.  
**Internal danger:** allowing tradition to become stagnation.

---

### VI — House Talan
**The Free Merchants**

Talan believes people should be able to move, trade, negotiate, and become wealthy without asking permission from a distant throne.

They oppose Imperial economic controls while happily selling to almost anyone.

They can be allies, competitors, suppliers, smugglers, or enemies depending on circumstance.

**Public face:** freedom and opportunity.  
**Military character:** mobile forces, escorts, private security, commerce protection.  
**Economic character:** trade, finance, transport, information.  
**Relationship with Empress:** tolerated when profitable; resistant when controlled.  
**Internal danger:** allowing profit to become the only moral principle.

---

### VII — House Merid
**The Civic Compact**

Merid believes legitimate authority comes from the consent of the governed.

They are the political center of the thirteen in one important sense: they can negotiate with almost anyone while refusing to surrender their principle.

They oppose both Imperial absolutism and revolutionary authoritarianism.

**Public face:** law, representation, civic responsibility.  
**Military character:** professional defense forces and strong civil-military institutions.  
**Economic character:** mixed economy and public infrastructure.  
**Relationship with Empress:** formally diplomatic, fundamentally opposed.  
**Internal danger:** paralysis through endless negotiation.

---

### VIII — House Rhen
**The Frontier Powers**

Rhen controls difficult worlds and remote systems that survive largely because they have learned to survive without central authority.

They distrust both Imperial administrators and wealthy core factions.

Their people value self-reliance.

**Public face:** independence and survival.  
**Military character:** reconnaissance, irregular warfare, hardened local forces.  
**Economic character:** extraction, frontier manufacturing, local trade.  
**Relationship with Empress:** distant resistance.  
**Internal danger:** isolation and factionalism.

---

### IX — House Solenne
**The Humanists**

Solenne believes the purpose of civilization is to improve the lives of ordinary people.

They oppose Imperial exploitation and are willing to fight powerful factions that abuse civilians.

They can become the moral center of a region—or become dangerously paternalistic.

**Public face:** dignity, welfare, protection.  
**Military character:** defense, medical capability, civilian protection.  
**Economic character:** social investment and sustainable development.  
**Relationship with Empress:** humanitarian opposition.  
**Internal danger:** believing good intentions justify coercion.

---

### X — House Kharos
**The Militarists**

Kharos believes peace is maintained by strength.

They respect competent enemies and despise weakness.

They do not necessarily want the Empire destroyed. They want military power to belong to those capable of using it.

**Public face:** strength and readiness.  
**Military character:** elite troops, aggressive doctrine, constant preparedness.  
**Economic character:** military industry and strategic resources.  
**Relationship with Empress:** openly competitive.  
**Internal danger:** making war the answer to every problem.

---

### XI — House Nyr
**The Separatists**

Nyr believes the galaxy has become too large and too centralized for any empire to govern legitimately.

They want systems to govern themselves.

They can cooperate with almost anyone temporarily, but they resist permanent central authority.

**Public face:** sovereignty and self-determination.  
**Military character:** distributed defense, sabotage, local forces.  
**Economic character:** local production and resilient supply chains.  
**Relationship with Empress:** hostile.  
**Internal danger:** fragmentation.

---

### XII — House Voss
**The Revolutionaries**

Voss believes the existing political order cannot be repaired.

They seek fundamental transformation.

Some members genuinely want liberation.

Others want revenge.

Others simply want to see the old order burn.

**Public face:** liberation and revolution.  
**Military character:** insurgency, infiltration, sabotage, political warfare.  
**Economic character:** confiscation, redistribution, revolutionary mobilization.  
**Relationship with Empress:** openly hostile.  
**Internal danger:** replacing one tyranny with another.

---

### XIII — House Draven
**The Unbound**

Draven is openly committed to breaking Imperial power.

They do not pretend otherwise.

They believe the Empire is the fundamental enemy and are willing to accept enormous risk to destroy it.

They are the faction most likely to begin the game with the player already in political conflict with Imperial authority.

They are not necessarily the "good" faction.

They are simply the faction least willing to pretend that coexistence with the Empress is acceptable.

**Public face:** defiance.  
**Military character:** aggressive anti-Imperial warfare, unconventional operations.  
**Economic character:** survival under pressure and war mobilization.  
**Relationship with Empress:** openly hostile.  
**Internal danger:** becoming exactly as ruthless as the regime they oppose.

---

## 5. The factions are not good, neutral, and evil

The player should be able to look at the thirteen factions and eventually conclude that some are admirable, some horrifying, and many are both.

The design should preserve the useful dramatic simplicity of classic space opera without reducing the galaxy to a morality meter.

A faction can:

- protect civilians and commit atrocities;
- provide freedom and tolerate exploitation;
- produce technological miracles and conduct unethical experiments;
- defend cultural identity and oppress outsiders;
- oppose tyranny and become tyrannical;
- cooperate with the Empress while genuinely improving lives;
- fight the Empire while making ordinary life worse.

The important distinction is:

> **Political alignment is not morality.**

The hidden door ordering is about relationship to the Empress, not good versus evil.

## 6. The Empress's secret network

The Empress does not need all thirteen factions to be equally hostile.

She can influence the galaxy through:

- treaties;
- favors;
- debt;
- trade privileges;
- military protection;
- intelligence;
- marriages;
- appointments;
- technology access;
- resource contracts;
- covert operations;
- blackmail;
- patronage.

Some factions knowingly cooperate.

Some cooperate without understanding the full arrangement.

Some contain internal Imperial agents.

Some leaders may believe they are manipulating the Empress while she is actually manipulating them.

The player should discover these relationships through experience.

## 7. Faction relationships are a graph

Faction diplomacy should not be a simple table of permanent allies and enemies.

Each faction can have relationships with every other faction:

- trust;
- fear;
- respect;
- hatred;
- dependency;
- economic competition;
- military rivalry;
- ideological compatibility;
- intelligence penetration;
- treaty status;
- recent incidents;
- territorial disputes;
- historical grievances.

The resulting political structure is a graph.

A faction may fight one enemy while secretly trading with another.

A faction may support the player against the Empire while simultaneously planning to take the player's worlds.

## 8. The galaxy never waits for the player

The player is not the only actor.

While the player is:

- fighting;
- researching;
- traveling;
- building;
- negotiating;
- recovering;
- sleeping;
- commanding;
- exploring;

other factions continue acting.

They:

- plot;
- invade;
- defend;
- trade;
- research;
- build;
- recruit;
- sabotage;
- negotiate;
- betray;
- lose battles;
- win battles;
- fracture;
- recover.

A battle can begin and end somewhere the player never visits.

The player may later discover its consequences.

> **The galaxy does not generate conflict because the player arrived. Conflict was already happening.**

## 9. No victory ceremony

There is no universal "Congratulations, you won the sector" screen.

Winning something means the world changed.

The player may receive:

- territory;
- resources;
- equipment;
- political influence;
- personnel;
- research opportunities;
- prestige;
- access;
- debt;
- enemies;
- responsibility.

The player then decides what to do with those gains.

A conquered world still has people.

A captured resource still requires logistics.

A defeated faction may regroup.

A promoted commander may receive worse orders.

A victory can create the conditions for the next crisis.

The game therefore has no requirement for a conventional victory scene.

> **There is no victory screen because the war did not stop.**

## 10. Global simulation and focus

The galaxy is conceptually enormous.

The simulation should not require every location to receive identical processing frequency.

A useful model is:

**Galaxy representation → global tick → regional/system activity → focused simulation**

The entire galaxy receives continued simulation.

Focus changes fidelity and frequency.

### Global tick

The global layer maintains low-cost state for:

- faction relationships;
- ownership;
- major military movement;
- resource production;
- strategic logistics;
- research progress;
- diplomatic events;
- battles;
- population changes;
- political events;
- major discoveries;
- major losses.

### Regional/system tick

Systems with meaningful activity can receive more frequent processing.

Examples:

- active invasion;
- fleet movement;
- major research event;
- resource shortage;
- political crisis;
- large battle;
- player travel;
- player-owned infrastructure.

### Focused tick

Where the player or another observer has high interest, the simulation can resolve:

- individual units;
- crews;
- vehicles;
- procedures;
- physical logistics;
- local combat;
- detailed research operations;
- first-person interactions.

The key rule is:

> **Focus changes simulation resolution. It does not create simulation existence.**

A remote battle does not freeze because the player is looking elsewhere.

## 11. GPU representation

The galaxy may ultimately be represented as a GPU-friendly field or texture-like data structure.

This is a Candidate architecture, not yet a fixed implementation.

The conceptual hierarchy is:

**Galaxy field**
→ star-system state
→ faction presence
→ regional activity
→ local state
→ focused entities

A GPU representation could allow broad state to be evaluated in parallel while CPU/FSM processing is reserved for the relationships and procedures that require richer semantics.

This should be treated as an architectural opportunity, not a reason to force the game into a texture metaphor.

The authoritative world remains semantic data.

> **The texture is a representation of the galaxy. It is not the galaxy.**

## 12. Event horizons across the galaxy

The same Event Horizon idea applies politically as well as visually.

| Horizon | Example information |
|---|---|
| Personal | individual soldier, vehicle, room, conversation |
| Local | base, battle, convoy, facility |
| Regional | system, front, resource network, fleet movement |
| Galactic | faction strength, ownership, diplomacy, major wars |

A distant system can remain alive at strategic resolution.

If the player later travels there, richer state can become relevant.

The system must reconstruct that state from authoritative history and simulation rules rather than pretending nothing happened.

## 13. The first faction choice as gameplay

The faction selection sequence should itself be an Experience.

The player enters the faction headquarters.

They see thirteen physical doors.

Each office contains enough evidence to make the faction feel real:

- architecture;
- uniforms;
- symbols;
- literature;
- recruiting material;
- personnel;
- military displays;
- economic artifacts;
- historical records;
- propaganda;
- conversations;
- visible contradictions.

The player can choose immediately.

Or explore.

The game does not say:

**"This is the easiest faction."**

It says:

**"Here are thirteen doors."**

The player chooses.

Only later do they understand what that choice meant.

## 14. Faction as an Experience Manifest input

A faction choice should eventually resolve to a structured faction identity rather than hard-coded campaign branching.

Conceptually:

**Faction selection**
→ faction manifest
→ starting relationships
→ starting territory
→ doctrine
→ available capabilities
→ initial personnel
→ political context
→ Imperial relationship
→ opening conditions

This allows the same galaxy simulation to host all thirteen factions.

## 15. Design principles

1. **Thirteen is a starting political ecosystem, not a technological limit.**
2. **The factions are not two teams.**
3. **The Empress is not merely another faction leader.**
4. **Faction alignment and morality are separate dimensions.**
5. **The player should discover politics through the world.**
6. **The galaxy continues without the player.**
7. **Remote conflict remains real.**
8. **Focus changes fidelity, not existence.**
9. **Victory changes the world instead of ending it.**
10. **The same authoritative world supports Commander and Soldier perspectives.**
11. **The GPU may represent the galaxy; semantic state remains authoritative.**
12. **Political relationships are dynamic graphs, not fixed team colors.**

## 16. Still to illuminate

The following should remain open rather than being invented:

- the Empress's name, history, and exact power;
- the formal name of the Empire;
- the thirteen final faction names;
- exact faction territories;
- faction population sizes;
- exact military strengths;
- detailed faction technologies;
- faction-specific visual language;
- internal faction leadership;
- faction succession;
- faction civil wars;
- exact diplomacy mechanics;
- exact Imperial taxation/tithe model;
- exact global tick cadence;
- exact GPU data representation;
- how much remote combat is physically resolved versus statistically resolved;
- how history is compressed/reconstructed;
- whether factions can disappear completely;
- whether new factions can emerge from splinter groups.

These are design questions, not implementation excuses.
