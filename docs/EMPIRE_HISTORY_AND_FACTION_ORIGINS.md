# raWWar — Empire History & Faction Origins

**Status:** Living design model.  
**Canon intent:** Establish enough shared history to explain why the thirteen major factions exist and why they think the way they do.  
**Important:** Names, dates, exact rulers, and specific historical events remain refinable until illuminated further.

> **The factions did not appear because the game needed thirteen factions. They survived because the Empire created thirteen different answers to the same historical problem.**

---

## 1. The Empire existed before the thirteen factions

The galaxy was not born divided into thirteen political powers.

Long before the current war, human civilization expanded across many star systems.

Worlds developed independently.

Some became agricultural centers. Some became mining worlds. Some built shipyards. Some became scientific centers. Some became military strongholds. Some were little more than settlements clinging to survival at the edge of known space.

Distance mattered.

Even with warp travel, ships cannot safely form or maintain a warp bubble within a star's gravitational well. Routes must bend around stellar gravity, and long-distance travel requires preparation, navigation, and time.

The galaxy therefore never became physically small merely because civilization learned to travel between stars.

Political power followed the same geometry.

The first great expansion produced hundreds of local governments, trading leagues, military protectorates, hereditary houses, corporate territories, colonies, and independent worlds.

The Empire eventually emerged from this chaos.

It did not conquer an empty galaxy.

It inherited a civilization that was already politically alive.

---

## 2. The Consolidation

The earliest Imperial rulers did not initially call themselves absolute rulers.

The first Imperial project was consolidation.

Trade routes were standardized.

Navigation standards were established.

Major ports were protected.

Disputes between worlds were arbitrated.

Piracy was suppressed.

Common military procedures emerged.

Shared infrastructure became possible.

The early Empire became attractive because it solved problems that individual worlds could not solve alone.

For many generations, Imperial membership meant:

- safer trade;
- predictable law;
- protected routes;
- access to larger markets;
- coordinated defense;
- shared standards;
- technological exchange;
- assistance during disasters;
- a political mechanism for disputes between worlds.

The Empire's legitimacy was therefore not originally based solely on fear.

It was earned.

That matters.

The descendants of the original Imperial coalition can still point to the historical record and say:

> **Before the Empire, we were many worlds. Because of the Empire, we became a civilization.**

That argument remains powerful.

---

## 3. The Imperial Compact

As the Empire expanded, individual worlds increasingly surrendered pieces of sovereignty in exchange for Imperial guarantees.

The arrangement became known as the Imperial Compact.

Worlds retained:

- local law;
- cultural identity;
- local leadership;
- property;
- local military traditions;
- economic institutions.

The Empire controlled increasingly important shared functions:

- interstellar defense;
- major navigation infrastructure;
- strategic treaties;
- large-scale taxation;
- frontier security;
- major military mobilization;
- inter-system arbitration.

The Compact worked because the Empire needed the worlds and the worlds needed the Empire.

For a long time, the distinction between Imperial authority and local authority remained negotiable.

Then the galaxy became larger.

---

## 4. The Distance Crisis

Expansion created a problem that no constitution could completely solve.

The farther a world was from the Imperial core, the longer it took to communicate, travel, reinforce, inspect, govern, and repair.

A decision made in the capital could arrive after the circumstances that motivated it had already changed.

A military order could reach a battlefield after the battle was over.

A tax assessment could arrive after a harvest had failed.

A regulatory requirement could become impossible because the necessary equipment existed several systems away.

Local governments learned to improvise.

The Empire learned to demand compliance.

Both adaptations were rational.

Together they created the political fracture that would eventually produce the major factions.

---

# 5. The First Imperial Reforms

The Imperial court attempted to solve the Distance Crisis through centralization.

Standardization expanded.

Imperial administrators gained greater authority.

Military commands became more centralized.

Strategic industries received Imperial oversight.

Navigation became increasingly regulated.

Resource flows were measured.

Imperial representatives gained authority over local governments.

The reforms were initially successful.

They reduced corruption in some regions.

They improved logistics.

They made military mobilization faster where Imperial infrastructure was strong.

They also created a new political class:

**people whose careers depended upon Imperial administration.**

Those administrators became the ancestors of House Cael.

At the same time, military houses that benefited from Imperial contracts became more powerful.

Industrial houses became indispensable.

Research institutions became dependent upon Imperial funding.

Trade organizations began resisting regulation.

Remote worlds became increasingly suspicious of decisions made thousands of light-years away.

The Empire had begun producing the forces that would eventually resist it.

---

# 6. The Industrial Acceleration

A period of extraordinary industrial growth followed.

New materials.

Better propulsion.

Improved weapons.

Larger shipyards.

More efficient resource processing.

More capable automation.

More advanced medical systems.

The galaxy became capable of building things at a scale previous generations could barely imagine.

House Oris emerged from this period.

Oris did not begin as a noble house.

It began as a network of industrial interests that learned something politically important:

> **If everyone needs what you build, everyone eventually needs you.**

Oris accumulated factories, mines, shipyards, logistics networks, engineering schools, and financial influence.

The Empire welcomed this development.

Then discovered that it had created a power it could not easily command.

---

# 7. The Knowledge Accords

Scientific institutions eventually demanded protection from political interference.

The Empire responded by granting major research centers special privileges.

Research could cross borders.

Scientists could move between institutions.

Imperial funding supported long-term programs.

Some technologies were deliberately separated from ordinary political control.

House Sere emerged from these institutions.

Sere's founding belief was simple:

> **Knowledge must remain free enough to discover what power has not yet imagined.**

The Empire saw Sere as an asset.

Sere saw the Empire as a funding source.

Neither understood how quickly those relationships could become dependencies.

The first major disputes between Sere and the Imperial court were not about rebellion.

They were about access.

Who could know?

Who could build?

Who could test?

Who could forbid research?

Those questions never received permanent answers.

---

# 8. The Preservation Wars

Not every world wanted the future.

Some worlds had developed cultures over centuries or millennia.

Their institutions were stable.

Their rituals mattered.

Their family structures mattered.

Their local histories mattered.

Imperial standardization increasingly treated cultural difference as administrative friction.

House Avar emerged from resistance to cultural homogenization.

Avar's ancestors did not initially oppose the Empire.

They opposed the idea that civilization required everyone to become the same civilization.

The Preservation Wars were therefore less about territory than identity.

Avar survived by demonstrating that cultural continuity could coexist with modern military and industrial power.

Its later resistance to Imperial centralization became almost inevitable.

---

# 9. The Merchant Revolt

Imperial regulation eventually reached trade.

The stated purpose was security.

The practical result was control.

Licenses.

Routes.

Tariffs.

Protected monopolies.

Imperial contracts.

Restricted cargo.

Authorized ports.

Trade houses that had once crossed the galaxy freely found themselves increasingly dependent on Imperial permission.

House Talan grew out of the merchant networks that resisted this system.

The first Talan leaders were not revolutionaries.

They were merchants who refused to believe that a cargo needed a throne's blessing to cross a star system.

Talan learned to survive through mobility.

They built:

- transport fleets;
- financial networks;
- private security;
- information networks;
- trading relationships;
- alternative supply routes.

The Empire eventually discovered that Talan was too useful to destroy.

Talan discovered that being too useful was itself a form of protection.

---

# 10. The Civic Crisis

The centralization of the Empire created a political question that could not be solved by logistics:

**Who gives the Empire the right to rule?**

House Merid formed from worlds and institutions that believed legitimate government required consent.

Merid did not initially demand independence.

It demanded representation.

When representation repeatedly failed to produce meaningful limits on Imperial authority, Merid's position hardened.

Its doctrine became:

> **Law without consent is administration. It is not legitimacy.**

Merid therefore became the great institutional opponent of absolute Imperial authority.

It remains willing to negotiate because negotiation is part of its philosophy.

It remains opposed because surrendering the principle of consent would destroy the reason it exists.

---

# 11. The Frontier Compact

While the core worlds argued over constitutions, the frontier had another problem.

Survival.

Remote worlds could not wait for the Empire to solve every problem.

They built their own defenses.

They learned local resource extraction.

They repaired equipment that the core considered obsolete.

They trained people to perform several jobs.

They developed unconventional navigation routes.

They learned to live with long periods of isolation.

House Rhen emerged from these frontier societies.

Rhen's philosophy was not originally ideological.

It was practical:

> **If help takes too long to arrive, you become the help.**

The Empire considers Rhen difficult.

Rhen considers the Empire distant.

Both descriptions are accurate.

---

# 12. The Civilian Reckoning

Industrial expansion and military mobilization produced enormous wealth.

They also produced enormous suffering.

Some regions became prosperous while others became extraction zones.

Some populations received extraordinary medical and technological support.

Others became disposable labor.

House Solenne emerged from physicians, civic organizations, relief networks, educators, and civilian protection movements that increasingly believed civilization had lost sight of its purpose.

Solenne's founding question was:

> **If civilization exists, what is it for?**

Their answer was:

> **People.**

This makes Solenne one of the strongest ideological opponents of Imperial exploitation.

It also creates an internal danger.

Once a movement believes it knows what is best for everyone, protection can become paternalism.

---

# 13. The Professional Armies

The Empire's repeated conflicts produced another transformation.

Military institutions became increasingly sophisticated.

Officers became specialists.

Training became systematic.

Large military industries emerged.

House Kharos formed from military institutions that believed the Empire had become too dependent upon political administrators.

Kharos respected competence.

It respected discipline.

It respected strength.

It did not necessarily reject the Empire's existence.

It rejected the assumption that Imperial political authority automatically made Imperial military decisions correct.

Kharos therefore became a competitor rather than a simple rebel.

The Empress could command Kharos.

She could not assume Kharos would agree.

---

# 14. The Secession Crisis

The final stages of Imperial centralization produced open resistance in several remote systems.

House Nyr emerged from a coalition of worlds that concluded the galaxy had become too large for legitimate centralized government.

Nyr's doctrine was radical but not inherently revolutionary:

> **A world should govern itself.**

Nyr does not need to conquer the galaxy.

It wants the galaxy to stop trying to conquer itself.

This makes Nyr difficult to defeat politically.

There is little territory to conquer that would permanently eliminate the idea.

---

# 15. The Revolution

Some people concluded that reform was impossible.

House Voss emerged from those movements.

Voss did not merely want representation.

It wanted the existing political order destroyed.

Its earliest members included:

- political radicals;
- dispossessed workers;
- former soldiers;
- persecuted activists;
- failed reformers;
- people who had lost family to Imperial actions;
- opportunists;
- genuine revolutionaries.

Voss therefore contains a contradiction at its core.

Some members want liberation.

Some want revenge.

Some want justice.

Some simply want to see the old world burn.

The faction survives because all four groups can fight the same enemy.

---

# 16. The Unbound

House Draven represents the point at which opposition ceases to seek permission.

Draven's historical origin is tied to the most severe Imperial crackdowns.

Where other factions argued that the Empire should change, Draven concluded that the Empire itself was the problem.

Its founding generation survived Imperial campaigns that became legendary.

Their descendants inherited a political memory in which compromise with the throne was interpreted as surrender.

Draven therefore became the faction least willing to pretend that coexistence with the Empress was acceptable.

Its doctrine is not:

> **We will reform the Empire.**

It is:

> **The Empire must no longer exist in its present form.**

That conviction makes Draven dangerous.

It also makes Draven vulnerable to becoming exactly as ruthless as the regime it hates.

---

# 17. The Empress

The current Empress inherited a galaxy that was already divided.

She did not create the thirteen factions.

She inherited their rivalries.

Her genius is recognizing that those rivalries can be turned into Imperial power.

She does not need every faction to love her.

She needs them to remain unable to unite against her.

Her tools include:

- favor;
- debt;
- contracts;
- marriages;
- appointments;
- military protection;
- intelligence;
- technological access;
- resource access;
- political recognition;
- selective punishment;
- selective mercy.

Her greatest weapon may therefore be neither army nor fleet.

It is the ability to make two enemies believe that cooperating with her is safer than cooperating with each other.

---

# 18. Why the thirteen remain separate

The obvious question is:

**Why have these factions not simply united against the Empress?**

Because they disagree about what should replace her.

Veyr fears disorder.

Cael fears administrative collapse.

Oris fears loss of production.

Sere fears suppression of knowledge.

Avar fears cultural erasure.

Talan fears economic control.

Merid fears illegitimate authority.

Rhen fears dependence.

Solenne fears human exploitation.

Kharos fears weakness.

Nyr fears centralization.

Voss fears reform becoming another prison.

Draven fears compromise itself.

The Empress does not have to eliminate these disagreements.

She only has to keep them politically relevant.

---

# 19. The historical irony

Every major faction contains something the Empire genuinely needed.

The Empire needed:

- Veyr's stability;
- Cael's administration;
- Oris's production;
- Sere's knowledge;
- Avar's cultural continuity;
- Talan's trade;
- Merid's legitimacy;
- Rhen's frontier resilience;
- Solenne's concern for people;
- Kharos's military competence;
- Nyr's local autonomy;
- Voss's willingness to challenge broken institutions;
- Draven's refusal to accept permanent domination.

The tragedy is that each strength can become a weakness.

Stability becomes obedience.

Administration becomes control.

Production becomes exploitation.

Knowledge becomes experimentation without restraint.

Tradition becomes stagnation.

Trade becomes greed.

Representation becomes paralysis.

Independence becomes isolation.

Humanism becomes paternalism.

Strength becomes militarism.

Autonomy becomes fragmentation.

Revolution becomes tyranny.

Defiance becomes fanaticism.

That is why the factions are not morality colors.

They are historical philosophies under pressure.

---

# 20. The player inherits history

When the player selects a faction, they do not choose a blank starting condition.

They inherit:

- historical victories;
- historical defeats;
- debts;
- treaties;
- grudges;
- allies;
- enemies;
- cultural expectations;
- military doctrine;
- technological traditions;
- economic relationships;
- internal political factions;
- reputation;
- obligations.

The player can change that history.

They cannot pretend it never happened.

A newly appointed commander may inherit a war that began before they were born.

A world may hate the player's faction because of an event the player had nothing to do with.

An ally may trust the player because of a treaty signed generations earlier.

A soldier may carry a family grievance into a battle.

A sub-faction may refuse an order because its local population remembers what happened the last time the central government sent soldiers.

The galaxy therefore contains **political memory**.

---

# 21. Faction origin is a generator

The historical model should eventually generate faction and sub-faction dispositions rather than merely describe them.

Conceptually:

**Historical pressures**
→ **institutions**
→ **collective memory**
→ **values**
→ **dispositions**
→ **doctrine**
→ **capabilities**
→ **behavior**

This creates a causal chain between history and simulation.

A faction that values self-reliance should tend to build resilient local supply chains.

A faction that values production should tend to invest in factories.

A faction that values civilian welfare should tend to invest in medicine and infrastructure.

A faction that values military strength should tend to invest in training and weapons.

These are tendencies.

They are not hard-coded destinies.

---

# 22. What this history gives raWWar

The player should eventually be able to ask:

**Why does this faction exist?**

The world should have an answer.

**Why does this faction hate that faction?**

The world should have an answer.

**Why does this world have these factories?**

The world should have an answer.

**Why does this soldier believe what they believe?**

The world should have an answer.

**Why did the Empress issue this order?**

The world should have an answer.

The answer may sometimes be:

> **Because something happened here two hundred years ago.**

That is the beginning of a galaxy with memory.

---

## 23. Historical depth remains expandable

This history is deliberately deep enough to support the current game without pretending that every century has already been written.

Future historical records can add:

- Imperial succession crises;
- major wars;
- famous commanders;
- technological discoveries;
- disasters;
- migrations;
- religious movements;
- economic collapses;
- rebellions;
- treaties;
- betrayals;
- dynastic marriages;
- planetary catastrophes;
- famous battles;
- lost colonies;
- legendary ships;
- historical atrocities;
- political reforms;
- faction schisms;
- extinct factions;
- emerging factions.

The important constraint is causal consistency.

New history should explain something that exists.

> **History is not decoration. History is why the present has the shape it does.**
