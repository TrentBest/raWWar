# raWWar — The Soldier

**Status:** Living design model.

> **A soldier is not a unit. A soldier is a person who has been shaped by training, equipment, experience, relationships, doctrine, and circumstance into someone capable of doing particular work.**

This document is the central dissertation for the raWWar soldier.

It connects:

- identity;
- body and condition;
- exoskeletons;
- equipment;
- training;
- qualifications;
- skills;
- formation;
- crew composition;
- command relationships;
- logistics;
- experience;
- specialization;
- injury and recovery;
- reassignment;
- promotion;
- leadership;
- first-person experience;
- Commander-level consequences.

The exact numerical values in this document are deliberately separated into **Canon**, **Candidate**, and **Illumination Needed**. We should not invent numbers merely because a spreadsheet wants them.

---

## 1. The Fundamental Idea

The soldier is one of the most important scales in raWWar.

The galaxy may contain millions or billions of people, but the player's experience is repeatedly grounded in individual people.

A soldier can:

- have a name;
- have a face;
- have a body;
- belong to a family or community;
- have a history;
- possess qualifications;
- possess skills;
- carry equipment;
- belong to a squad;
- belong to a larger formation;
- have a commanding officer;
- have subordinates;
- have friends;
- have rivals;
- remember places;
- remember victories;
- remember failures;
- become injured;
- lose equipment;
- survive the destruction of a vehicle;
- learn;
- teach;
- be promoted;
- be reassigned;
- become an instructor;
- become an advisor;
- become a commander;
- die.

The important distinction is:

> **The organization has units. The world contains people.**

A Commander may see a squad as a capability.

The Soldier experiences that squad as three other people, a leader, a mission, a place, a procedure, and a set of immediate consequences.

These are not different worlds.

They are different Event Horizons over the same authoritative world.

---

# 2. Soldier State

A soldier's state should not be one giant opaque object.

Conceptually, the soldier is a collection of related state domains:

`Identity + Body + Condition + Skills + Qualifications + Equipment + Assignment + Relationships + Experience + Procedure State + History`

These domains change at different rates.

### Identity

Persistent identity can include:

- identity;
- name;
- origin;
- affiliation;
- age;
- biography;
- language/cultural context;
- current organization;
- service history.

### Body and condition

Physical state can include:

- health;
- injury;
- fatigue;
- nutrition;
- hydration;
- environmental exposure;
- recovery;
- protective equipment;
- physical limitations;
- medical restrictions.

Exact physiological simulation remains **Illumination Needed**.

We do not need a medical simulator merely because the soldier is persistent.

We do need consequences when physical condition affects what the soldier can actually do.

### Skills

Skills represent demonstrated ability.

A soldier can become better through:

`Training → Attempt → Practice → Outcome → Experience Evidence → Skill Progression`

Training accelerates learning.

Experience validates it.

> **Training teaches a soldier how to perform. Experience teaches the soldier what happens when performance meets reality.**

### Qualifications

Qualifications represent recognized/certified capability.

A soldier may have:

- skill without formal qualification;
- qualification without current readiness;
- qualification without current assignment;
- qualification that has become obsolete;
- multiple qualifications;
- instructor/certifier authority.

Qualification and skill therefore must not be collapsed into the same value.

### Equipment

Equipment is physical state.

A soldier can have:

- armor;
- exoskeleton;
- helmet;
- sensors;
- communications;
- weapon;
- ammunition;
- tools;
- medical equipment;
- environmental protection;
- identification;
- personal equipment.

Equipment can be:

- assigned;
- carried;
- stored;
- maintained;
- damaged;
- lost;
- replaced;
- upgraded;
- reconfigured;
- captured;
- transferred.

# 3. The Exoskeleton

The exoskeleton is not simply "armor."

It is a machine worn by a person.

Its purpose may include some combination of:

- strength assistance;
- load carriage;
- endurance assistance;
- stabilization;
- recoil management;
- mobility assistance;
- injury prevention;
- environmental protection;
- sensor integration;
- communications;
- power distribution;
- equipment mounting;
- weapon integration;
- medical monitoring;
- augmented physical capability.

The exact architecture remains **Candidate**.

The important design principle is:

> **An exoskeleton changes what a trained human can physically do; it does not replace the human.**

The soldier still has:

- judgment;
- perception;
- skill;
- fear;
- fatigue;
- training;
- doctrine;
- experience;
- responsibility.

The machine should amplify the soldier rather than turn the soldier into a generic robot.

## 3.1 Exoskeleton configuration

An exoskeleton can conceptually be assembled from:

`Frame + Actuation + Power + Control + Sensors + Protection + Interfaces + Mission Equipment`

Different factions may make different choices.

A faction that values endurance may prioritize:

- efficient actuation;
- power management;
- cooling;
- long-duration operation.

A faction emphasizing heavy assault may prioritize:

- load capacity;
- armor;
- stabilization;
- weapon integration.

A faction emphasizing reconnaissance may prioritize:

- weight;
- sensor integration;
- low signature;
- mobility.

This is where faction provenance becomes meaningful.

The exoskeleton should not exist because a designer wanted a cool silhouette.

It should exist because a society developed a technology for reasons that fit its:

`History → Disposition → Doctrine → Research → Industry → Equipment`

## 3.2 Exoskeleton qualifications

Wearing an exoskeleton should itself be a qualification pathway.

Candidate progression:

`Untrained → Familiarization → Assisted Movement → Operational Qualification → Advanced Configuration → Instructor`

A soldier may need separate qualifications for:

- basic exoskeleton operation;
- heavy-load configuration;
- combat configuration;
- environmental configuration;
- specialized tools;
- weapon integration;
- maintenance;
- emergency recovery;
- field repair.

Exact qualification families remain **Illumination Needed**.

## 3.3 Exoskeleton failure

An exoskeleton is another physical system.

It can experience:

- power depletion;
- actuator damage;
- sensor failure;
- communications failure;
- structural damage;
- environmental degradation;
- overheating;
- control faults;
- maintenance backlog;
- incompatible configuration.

A soldier should therefore be capable of surviving an exoskeleton failure.

The consequences depend on the failure.

A disabled actuator may reduce mobility.

A damaged sensor may reduce awareness.

A power problem may reduce assistance.

A catastrophic failure may leave the soldier physically capable but substantially less effective.

> **The soldier is not the exoskeleton. The exoskeleton is equipment the soldier has learned to use.**

# 4. Training Is the Beginning of Military Capability

Training should not be a menu called "create soldier."

Training is a physical organizational process.

The basic chain is:

`Person → Recruitment → Assessment → Training → Qualification → Assignment → Experience → Specialization`

A military organization therefore needs:

- instructors;
- training facilities;
- training equipment;
- training capacity;
- schedules;
- curriculum;
- supervision;
- assessment;
- certification;
- personnel records.

The existing barracks model provides an important foundation:

> A standard barracks contains four VR training modules and trains one four-person squad at a time.

This makes training capacity physical.

A player cannot simply order unlimited qualified soldiers into existence.

---

# 5. Training Programs

Training should be a family of programs rather than one universal boot camp.

## 5.1 Basic military training

Candidate curriculum:

1. orientation;
2. physical conditioning;
3. discipline and procedure;
4. movement;
5. communications;
6. basic weapons;
7. basic maintenance;
8. first aid;
9. formation behavior;
10. field exercises;
11. assessment.

The exact duration and curriculum remain **Illumination Needed**.

The goal is not to define a real-world military curriculum.

The goal is to establish a reusable simulation structure.

## 5.2 Exoskeleton training

After basic training, appropriate soldiers may enter powered-equipment training.

Candidate sequence:

`Familiarization → Fit/Calibration → Assisted Movement → Load Handling → Mobility → Equipment Integration → Emergency Procedures → Qualification`

The soldier learns not merely to move in the machine, but to understand its limitations.

## 5.3 Weapons training

Weapons training branches from basic capability.

Examples:

- rifle;
- sidearm;
- heavy weapon;
- anti-armor;
- anti-air;
- precision;
- energy;
- explosive;
- crew-served systems.

A qualification may require:

- classroom/simulation work;
- physical handling;
- maintenance;
- safety procedures;
- live practice;
- evaluation.

## 5.4 Vehicle training

Vehicle operation branches from common foundations.

`Basic Soldier → Vehicle Foundation → Vehicle Family → Specific Vehicle → Configuration → Crew Role`

Possible roles:

- driver;
- gunner;
- commander;
- mechanic;
- sensor operator;
- communications operator;
- loading specialist;
- pilot;
- flight systems operator.

## 5.5 Technical training

Some soldiers leave the conventional combat path.

Examples:

- mechanic;
- electronics technician;
- power technician;
- sensor technician;
- communications technician;
- armor technician;
- structural technician;
- propulsion technician;
- robotics technician;
- medical specialist;
- logistics specialist.

This is how a military installation becomes a society of capabilities rather than a warehouse full of generic infantry.

# 6. Training Capacity Is a Real Resource

The player expresses intent:

> Train another squad of vehicle crews.

The organization must resolve that intent through physical capability.

It needs:

- available personnel;
- instructors;
- training modules;
- equipment;
- curriculum;
- schedule;
- facility;
- power;
- maintenance;
- administrative capacity.

If one training facility can train one squad at a time, it cannot train ten squads simultaneously.

Additional capacity can come from:

- more barracks;
- more modules;
- additional instructors;
- additional facilities;
- improved procedures;
- better training equipment;
- faction-specific technology.

The bottleneck should emerge from the organization.

> **Training capacity is infrastructure.**

# 7. Qualification Pathways

The qualification graph already establishes:

`T0 → T1 → T2 → T3 → T4 → T5`

with branching capability families.

The soldier dissertation places the individual inside that graph.

Example:

`Recruit
→ Basic Soldier
→ Basic Weapons
→ Exoskeleton Operator
→ Armored Infantry
→ Anti-Armor Specialist
→ Squad Leader
→ Instructor`

Another soldier might become:

`Recruit
→ Basic Soldier
→ Vehicle Foundation
→ Armored Crew
→ Driver
→ Tank Driver
→ Advanced Tank Driver
→ Instructor`

Another:

`Recruit
→ Basic Soldier
→ Technical Foundation
→ Electronics
→ Sensors
→ Reconnaissance Systems
→ Advanced Sensor Specialist
→ Instructor`

There is no requirement that every soldier follow the same tree.

> **The qualification graph is a landscape, not a class selector.**

# 8. Formation Sizes

Formation size must distinguish **organizational identity** from arbitrary game-design numbers.

### Canon

A squad is currently defined as:

> **Four people.**

This is the strongest existing formation anchor.

### Candidate hierarchy

A useful starting hierarchy is:

| Formation | Candidate size | Purpose |
|---|---:|---|
| Fireteam / element | 2–4 | Immediate tactical task |
| Squad | 4 | Core personnel/training unit |
| Platoon | 3–5 squads | Tactical organization |
| Company | 3–5 platoons | Major maneuver/support organization |
| Battalion | 3–5 companies | Larger operational organization |
| Regiment / equivalent | 3–5 battalions | Large operational formation |
| Brigade / equivalent | 2–5 major subordinate formations | Major combat organization |
| Division / equivalent | Multiple brigades | Strategic/tactical formation |
| Corps / equivalent | Multiple divisions | Operational theater |
| Army / equivalent | Multiple corps | Major theater/strategic organization |

These are **Candidate** ranges, not fixed canon.

The game should permit faction-specific organizational structures.

A faction may use:

- smaller professional formations;
- larger mass formations;
- highly specialized independent units;
- decentralized cells;
- naval-style organizational structures;
- industrial security formations;
- mixed civilian/military organizations.

The important thing is that the formation exists because the organization has a reason for it.

---

# 9. Squad as the Fundamental Training and Social Unit

The four-person squad has an unusually useful role.

It is:

- a training unit;
- a social unit;
- a tactical unit;
- a qualification group;
- a deployment unit;
- a potential vehicle crew grouping;
- a persistent history container.

The squad should therefore develop its own history.

Members may:

- train together;
- deploy together;
- lose members;
- gain replacements;
- develop relationships;
- learn procedures together;
- earn recognition;
- become known for particular accomplishments;
- split apart;
- reunite.

A squad should not be destroyed conceptually merely because the player leaves the area.

---

# 10. Crew Size Is Capability-Dependent

The existing crew model correctly distinguishes:

**Person ≠ Squad ≠ Crew.**

A vehicle may require:

- driver;
- gunner;
- commander;
- mechanic;
- sensor operator.

Another may require only a driver.

A facility may require:

- operators;
- technicians;
- maintenance;
- quality assurance;
- logistics;
- administration.

Therefore:

> **Crew size should emerge from capability requirements, not from a universal unit size.**

A four-person squad may fill a four-person crew.

Two squads may be needed for a larger capability.

A specialist may be attached from another organization.

A reserve crew may replace an unavailable active crew.

---

# 11. Formation Is a Relationship, Not Just a Number

A formation should know:

- who belongs to it;
- who commands it;
- what its mission is;
- where it is;
- what it is trained to do;
- what equipment it possesses;
- what resources it requires;
- its current readiness;
- its history;
- its relationships with other formations.

The same four people can therefore be:

- a training squad;
- a deployed infantry squad;
- a vehicle crew;
- a security detail;
- an instructor group.

The assignment changes their organizational role without erasing their identities.

---

# 12. Command Relationships

A soldier needs more than a rank.

A soldier may have:

- direct superior;
- squad leader;
- formation commander;
- staff relationship;
- technical supervisor;
- instructor;
- trainee;
- subordinate;
- advisor;
- peer relationships.

Command is therefore a graph of relationships.

The commander should not need to know every soldier personally.

But the world should be capable of resolving:

> Who is responsible for this person?

and:

> Who is responsible for this capability?

This becomes especially important when something fails.

If a launch pad is unmanned, the system should be able to determine:

- which crew was assigned;
- who scheduled them;
- whether they are qualified;
- whether they are present;
- whether they are on shift;
- whether they are injured;
- whether another crew can replace them;
- whether the organization has a reserve.

---

# 13. The Soldier's Equipment Load

Equipment should be treated as a capability configuration.

Conceptually:

`Soldier + Exoskeleton + Armor + Weapon + Sensors + Communications + Tools + Consumables`

Different roles produce different configurations.

### Infantry

Likely prioritizes:

- mobility;
- weapon;
- ammunition;
- communications;
- basic medical;
- environmental protection.

### Anti-armor

Adds:

- anti-armor system;
- targeting equipment;
- ammunition;
- specialized qualification.

### Reconnaissance

Prioritizes:

- sensors;
- communications;
- mobility;
- concealment;
- observation equipment.

### Mechanic

Prioritizes:

- tools;
- diagnostics;
- spare parts;
- protective equipment;
- technical interfaces.

### Researcher

May have:

- instruments;
- samples;
- protective equipment;
- analytical tools;
- communications.

The soldier's appearance should therefore be a consequence of their actual equipment.

> **Uniform is identity. Equipment is capability.**

---

# 14. Logistics Reaches the Individual

The soldier is the final consumer in many logistical chains.

For ammunition:

`Production → Stockpile → Depot → Transport → Unit → Soldier → Weapon → Consumption`

For food:

`Production → Storage → Transport → Facility → Soldier → Consumption`

For medical care:

`Medical supplies → Facility → Medical personnel → Soldier → Recovery`

For exoskeleton operation:

`Power/parts → Depot → Maintenance → Exoskeleton → Soldier`

This makes the soldier an excellent test of whether the logistics system is real.

If ammunition does not arrive, the soldier has less ammunition.

If the exoskeleton is damaged, the soldier's physical capability changes.

If food or medical support is disrupted, readiness changes.

> **The supply chain eventually reaches a person.**

---

# 15. Soldiers Do Not Need to Be Micro-Managed

The player should not have to tell:

- Soldier 1847 to reload;
- Soldier 1848 to eat;
- Soldier 1849 to repair equipment;
- Soldier 1850 to sleep.

Those are procedures.

FSMs perform them.

The player expresses higher-level intent.

For example:

> Keep this squad combat-ready.

The organization resolves:

- ammunition;
- equipment;
- maintenance;
- food;
- medical readiness;
- rest;
- replacement personnel;
- transport;
- mission assignment.

Micro-management remains possible when the player chooses to intervene.

That intervention can provide leverage through:

- better information;
- timing;
- prioritization;
- direct leadership;
- crisis response;
- personal coordination.

> **Delegation provides the baseline. Attention provides leverage.**

---

# 16. Experience Changes the Soldier

Two soldiers with identical qualifications should not necessarily be identical.

Experience can influence:

- reaction to familiar situations;
- confidence;
- procedure speed;
- recognition of danger;
- resource conservation;
- improvisation within legal procedures;
- communication;
- leadership;
- teaching;
- judgment.

The soldier should not simply gain arbitrary percentage bonuses.

Instead:

`Experience → learned evidence → changed capability/disposition → different decisions/outcomes`

This is especially important for veterans.

A veteran may recognize:

- a failing vehicle;
- a dangerous route;
- a logistics problem;
- an enemy tactic;
- a procedural mistake;
- a weather hazard.

That recognition is part of the person's history.

---

# 17. Injury, Survival, and Reassignment

The destruction of equipment does not imply destruction of its crew.

If a vehicle is destroyed and a crew member survives:

- the person survives;
- qualifications remain;
- experience remains;
- relationships remain;
- injuries may remain;
- equipment may be lost;
- assignment changes;
- the organization must decide what to do with them.

Possible outcomes:

- rescue;
- treatment;
- recovery;
- reassignment;
- replacement training;
- promotion;
- instructor duty;
- return to combat.

This creates an important asymmetry:

> **A destroyed vehicle is an asset loss. A surviving crew member is an organizational memory that remains.**

---

# 18. Death

Death should be authoritative.

A dead soldier does not respawn.

The consequences may include:

- formation loss;
- qualification loss;
- relationship loss;
- command vacancy;
- replacement requirement;
- morale effects;
- family consequences;
- institutional memory;
- historical record.

Death can therefore affect the organization at multiple Event Horizons.

The exact persistence and presentation of remains, memorialization, family systems, and content-rating boundaries remain **Illumination Needed**.

---

# 19. Promotion and Leadership

Promotion should not merely increase a number.

A promotion changes what the soldier is responsible for.

A soldier who becomes a squad leader may need:

- leadership qualification;
- additional communications;
- command equipment;
- tactical training;
- administrative responsibility;
- authority;
- accountability.

A higher commander may need:

- staff;
- advisors;
- intelligence;
- logistics;
- communications;
- planning capability.

The soldier therefore grows from being someone who performs procedures into someone who directs procedures performed by others.

---

# 20. Soldier → Instructor

T5 is particularly interesting because it can become organizational infrastructure.

A master-level soldier may:

- teach;
- certify;
- supervise;
- develop procedures;
- diagnose failures;
- train other instructors;
- advise commanders.

This creates a feedback loop:

`Experience → Expertise → Instructor → Training Capacity → More Qualified Soldiers → Organizational Capability`

The player's experienced soldiers can therefore become part of the machinery that creates future soldiers.

This is much more interesting than an abstract "+10% training speed" button.

---

# 21. Soldier Career

A possible career is:

`Civilian
→ Recruit
→ Basic Training
→ Qualified Soldier
→ Specialist
→ Experienced Specialist
→ Leader
→ Instructor / Advisor / Commander`

But the career should be non-linear.

A soldier might instead become:

- mechanic;
- pilot;
- researcher;
- intelligence specialist;
- logistics specialist;
- construction specialist;
- instructor;
- officer;
- commander;
- advisor.

A soldier can change direction.

Their history should remain.

# 22. Faction-Specific Soldiers

The soldier is also where faction identity becomes visible.

Two factions may both possess powered infantry.

They may still differ in:

- exoskeleton design;
- materials;
- colors;
- interfaces;
- training;
- formation doctrine;
- weapons;
- maintenance;
- medical practice;
- command culture;
- qualification requirements;
- logistics;
- acceptable risk;
- treatment of civilians;
- relationship between officer and soldier.

This follows the capability provenance model:

`History → Disposition → Doctrine → Research → Industry → Equipment → Training → Soldier → Behavior`

A soldier should therefore make the faction recognizable before the player reads a faction name.

# 23. Local/Sub-Faction Variation

A world should not necessarily produce identical soldiers to the faction capital.

Local differences can arise from:

- available resources;
- local industry;
- climate;
- gravity;
- culture;
- historical conflict;
- captured equipment;
- trade;
- local leadership;
- training infrastructure;
- neighboring threats;
- local research;
- Imperial influence.

Thus:

`Major Faction → Local History → Local Doctrine → Local Training → Local Soldier`

The soldier becomes evidence of where they came from.

# 24. Soldier Simulation and FSMs

The soldier is an ideal demonstration of the Workshop architecture.

A soldier may have multiple concurrent FSM domains:

- posture;
- locomotion;
- interaction;
- equipment;
- weapon;
- communication;
- formation;
- training;
- work;
- health/recovery;
- social activity;
- command;
- navigation;
- environment response.

The exact number remains a performance experiment.

The architecture should not assume one FSM per soldier.

Instead:

> **One soldier is a collection of related behaviors sharing one identity and state context.**

Shared FSM definitions can serve many soldiers.

Only instance state needs to differ.

This is compatible with the simulation-scale model where byte-sized state channels can represent compact hot state while semantic identity/history remains elsewhere.

---

# 25. The Soldier and Gestures

Gestures are the physical manifestation of intent.

The soldier does not need a bespoke animation for every semantic action.

Conceptually:

`Intent → Capability Provider → Legal Procedure → Gesture FSM → Physical Movement`

The same procedure can be performed differently by different people.

Variation may depend on:

- identity seed;
- body proportions;
- skill;
- equipment;
- fatigue;
- urgency;
- environment;
- experience.

Therefore two soldiers can perform the same procedure without looking like clones.

> **The ideal Gesture defines what the soldier is trying to do. The soldier's identity and state determine how that soldier does it.**

---

# 26. Environment

The soldier exists inside a world.

Environmental conditions can affect:

- fatigue;
- movement;
- visibility;
- equipment;
- exoskeleton operation;
- ammunition;
- medical readiness;
- work;
- training;
- formation speed.

Examples:

- heat increases fatigue;
- cold changes protective requirements;
- dust increases maintenance;
- radiation requires shielding/exposure management;
- storms delay outdoor operations;
- flooding blocks routes;
- low pressure changes equipment requirements;
- corrosive atmospheres increase degradation.

The soldier should not have an invisible environmental modifier.

The environment should act through physical systems.

# 28. What the Commander Sees

The Commander sees:

- 4 soldiers;
- one squad;
- one capability;
- one readiness state;
- one mission;
- one logistics requirement.

The Commander can drill down when useful.

At greater depth:

`Formation → Squad → Soldier → Equipment → Qualification → History`

The Commander should not need to do this constantly.

The information exists because the soldier exists.

# 29. What the Soldier Sees

The Soldier sees:

- the people nearby;
- equipment;
- terrain;
- weather;
- buildings;
- vehicles;
- officers;
- orders;
- hazards;
- procedures;
- consequences.

The Soldier does not see:

- a resource bar representing the entire galaxy;
- an abstract production queue;
- a hidden spreadsheet.

Those systems manifest through the world.

A shortage becomes:

> "The ammunition truck hasn't arrived."

A damaged facility becomes:

> "The maintenance crew is still repairing the generator."

A training bottleneck becomes:

> "Your qualification course starts tomorrow."

A command decision becomes:

> "Commander wants this formation moved before dawn."

The simulation is the same.

The manifestation is different.

# 30. Measurement and Implementation

The soldier dissertation should eventually become executable test cases.

Initial implementation targets:

### Soldier identity test

Create a persistent soldier with:

- identity;
- qualifications;
- equipment;
- squad;
- history.

Verify persistence through reassignment.

### Training test

Create:

- four recruits;
- one barracks;
- four training modules;
- one instructor.

Verify:

`Recruit → Training → Assessment → Qualification`

### Exoskeleton test

Create:

- qualified soldier;
- exoskeleton;
- equipment;
- power;
- maintenance state.

Verify that equipment condition changes soldier capability.

### Crew test

Create a vehicle requiring four roles.

Verify that:

- four qualified people are required;
- unavailable personnel reduce readiness;
- replacement personnel can restore capability.

### Logistics test

Consume ammunition.

Verify:

`Consumption → Inventory Reduction → Resupply Demand → Transport → Replenishment`

### Experience test

Run two soldiers through different experiences.

Verify that their histories diverge and their resulting capabilities/decisions can diverge.

### Event Horizon test

Simulate the same soldier:

- remotely;
- nearby;
- directly observed.

Verify that detail changes while authoritative consequences remain consistent.
