# raWWar — Advisors, Staff, and Command Candidates

Status: Living design model.

Design authority:
- **Canon** means explicitly established by the creator.
- **Candidate** means a useful proposal that still needs confirmation.
- **Illumination Needed** means the creator's vision is required before the rule is settled.

> **An advisor is a person in the military, not a bonus icon on a command screen.**

## 1. The core idea

### Canon

Advisors are soldiers in their exoskeletons. They have progressed through the chain of command and qualified for military-advisor responsibilities or other specialist advisory roles.

The advisor system should let the player encounter and consider alternative candidates. Choosing who advises the player is part of command gameplay, not merely a one-time character customization screen.

Advisor specializations extend beyond general military command. They can include science and other areas of expertise.

### Candidate

The command staff can be presented as a set of actual people the commander can meet, question, observe, compare, appoint, retain, dismiss, or replace. Their expertise is earned through careers, training, assignments, and demonstrated performance—not assigned solely because the game needs a particular menu option.

## 2. Advisors are qualified people

An advisor's authority should follow from a credible service history.

Potential qualifications and career evidence include:

- command training and successful leadership assignments;
- operational experience in the specialty they advise on;
- specialist qualifications and recertifications;
- training, evaluation, and readiness records;
- missions served and their outcomes;
- commendations, citations, and disciplinary history;
- equipment, vehicle, or system expertise;
- experience working with the current faction's doctrine and forces.

These are candidate record fields, not a finalized personnel schema. The important principle is that the player can inspect why a candidate is considered qualified.

## 3. Candidate specializations

The following examples illustrate the intended range; they do not establish a closed list of advisor roles.

| Advisor path | Example candidate | What the commander might seek |
|---|---|---|
| Military / operations | A senior officer qualified up the command chain | Operational planning, readiness, doctrine, and coordination |
| Aviation | An ace pilot | Flight operations, pilot training, air tactics, and aviation risk |
| Armor | The faction's best tank operator | Armored tactics, vehicle employment, and crew proficiency |
| Systems / cyber | A top systems hacker | Adversary systems, electronic intrusion, defensive hardening, and technical exploitation |
| Intelligence | A spy master | Intelligence collection, source handling, counterintelligence, and uncertainty assessment |
| Science | A highly accomplished researcher or scientific officer | Research priorities, experimental evidence, technical feasibility, and scientific risk |

An expert is not automatically the best candidate for every situation. The ace pilot may be extraordinary in the cockpit but less capable of teaching, communicating uncertainty, or working with a particular command team. The strongest candidate depends on what the commander needs and what the candidate can actually deliver.

## 4. Choosing an advisor is gameplay

A candidate-selection experience can let the commander:

1. Discover which candidates are available.
2. Inspect their service records and qualifications.
3. Compare demonstrated abilities and relevant experience.
4. Interview or meet candidates in-world.
5. Consider their recommendations and how they reason about a problem.
6. Appoint a candidate, retain an existing advisor, or leave the position vacant.
7. Observe the consequences of that choice over time.

The interface should reveal people and evidence, not collapse every candidate into a single unexplained rating.

A candidate's record may be incomplete, classified, contested, outdated, or shaped by faction politics. Those possibilities are candidates for the design and should not be treated as already-finalized rules.

## 5. Advisors should affect the living world

### Candidate behaviors

An appointed advisor might:

- prepare recommendations when relevant events occur;
- request access to people, facilities, reports, or experiments;
- disagree with another advisor and explain the basis of the disagreement;
- identify a risk that the commander has overlooked;
- revise a recommendation when new evidence arrives;
- take responsibility for a delegated review or investigation;
- develop a professional relationship with the commander and other staff;
- become unavailable through deployment, injury, reassignment, dismissal, or other world events.

These behaviors should arise from the advisor's role, knowledge, circumstances, and independent behavior—not from a scripted sequence that always produces the same answer.

An advisor can inform a decision; the advisor does not silently take command away from the player. Any delegated authority must be explicit and traceable.

## 6. Advice is not omniscience

An advisor knows what their experience, access, observations, and reports allow them to know.

Recommendations should be able to distinguish:

- observed fact;
- a report from another person or organization;
- professional inference;
- prediction;
- uncertainty or missing evidence.

A brilliant specialist can still be wrong outside their expertise, work from stale information, misunderstand an adversary, or disagree with another qualified person. This creates meaningful command decisions rather than a menu that reveals the objectively correct answer.

## 7. The staff is an organization

Advisors should be considered alongside the broader command staff, subordinate commanders, specialists, and support personnel.

Their relationships and working practices may matter. Two exceptional people might collaborate well, compete for resources, disagree about doctrine, or interpret the same evidence differently. Whether such dynamics become explicit mechanics is a candidate design decision.

The commander's challenge is not to collect the highest-scoring people. It is to build a staff whose expertise, trust, communication, and judgment fit the current mission and organization.

## 8. Connection to qualifications and career history

The advisor path should be a visible expression of the same qualification and career system available to soldiers elsewhere in raWWar.

A person can develop from a capable operator into an instructor, specialist, unit leader, and eventually an eligible advisor when their record and qualifications support that progression. Not every soldier must follow this path, and seniority alone should not imply mastery of every specialty.

This connects command gameplay to the lives of individual soldiers: the commander is choosing among people whose careers have unfolded in the same living war.

## 9. Questions still needing illumination

These decisions should remain open until the creator establishes the intended rules:

- Which advisor positions are mandatory for a command and which are optional?
- Who nominates or qualifies candidates, and who has final appointment authority?
- Can the player recruit advisors from outside their current organization or faction?
- How are classified records, hidden loyalties, and incomplete service histories represented?
- Can advisors refuse an appointment, resign, be reassigned, or be lost through events?
- How much autonomy can a commander delegate, and how is delegated authority revoked?
- What measurable effects should advice have on planning, research, training, readiness, intelligence, and operations?
- How should the game distinguish genuine expertise from reputation, political influence, or propaganda?
- How should advisor availability and candidate quality vary between factions?
- How should the player learn about candidates without turning every appointment into a spreadsheet exercise?

## 10. Design principle

> **The commander chooses people, not perks.**

Advisor gameplay should make expertise legible while preserving uncertainty, personality, accountability, and the commander's responsibility for the decision. The advisor is part of the living military world first; any interface that presents them is only a way to meet and understand that person.
