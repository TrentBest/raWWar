# raWWar — Faction Capability Matrix

**Status:** Living design model.  
**Canon:** Major factions have distinct capabilities and tendencies.  
**Candidate:** Exact inventories, technologies, production chains, and faction-specific variants remain open.

> **A faction should not be a skin applied to the same army. Its industrial history should make its capabilities make sense.**

## 1. What this matrix actually determines

This is not a table saying "House X gets tank Y."

It is the first layer of a capability generator.

The matrix answers:

1. What kinds of capability does a major faction naturally emphasize?
2. What kinds of buildings and vehicles are likely to be developed locally?
3. What capabilities are likely to be common, scarce, imported, experimental, or captured?
4. What does a sub-faction inherit?
5. What can local circumstances change?
6. What personnel skills and qualifications are consequently valuable?

The chain is:

```
Major faction doctrine
        ↓
technology + industrial tendency
        ↓
sub-faction specialization
        ↓
local buildings + production
        ↓
local vehicles/equipment
        ↓
local personnel
        ↓
skills + qualifications
        ↓
actual readiness
```

## 2. Capability status vocabulary

| Code | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **N** | Native — defining or unusually characteristic capability |
| **S** | Strong — major investment and likely local excellence |
| **C** | Common — normally available |
| **A** | Available — plausible but situational, regional, or dependent on acquisition |
| **R** | Rare — unusual enough to be strategically notable |
| **X** | External — normally obtained through trade, alliance, capture, or special circumstance |

These are **probabilities/tendencies**, not hard restrictions.

A House marked C for aerospace does not mean every world owns an aerospace factory.

A House marked X for a capability can still possess one if history explains how it got there.

Kharos should be terrifyingly competent at organized warfare.

Voss and Draven should not simply be "poor factions." Their asymmetric capabilities can make them dangerous despite weaker conventional industry.

## 6. Why this becomes interesting at the sub-faction level

Suppose House Oris controls three worlds:

### Oris Prime
A mature industrial core.

Likely:

- enormous materials factories;
- precision manufacturing;
- vehicle factories;
- aerospace production;
- shipyards;
- research facilities;
- trained industrial crews;
- large logistics networks.

### Oris Mining Moon
Resource-rich but industrially immature.

Likely:

- exceptional extraction;
- materials processing;
- heavy utility vehicles;
- mining spacecraft;
- large storage;
- limited precision manufacturing;
- imported advanced vehicles;
- unusually experienced industrial crews.

### Oris Frontier Colony
Recently acquired territory.

Likely:

- mixed inherited equipment;
- shortages;
- old vehicles;
- local improvisation;
- captured enemy technology;
- inadequate training capacity;
- high demand for imported specialists.

All three are Oris.

They should not play the same.

## 7. Capability provenance

Every significant capability should eventually be explainable through provenance:

```
Why does it exist here?
    ↓
Inherited doctrine?
    ↓
Local research?
    ↓
Local manufacture?
    ↓
Imported?
    ↓
Captured?
    ↓
Gifted / contracted?
    ↓
Recovered from ruins?
    ↓
Improvised?
```

The provenance affects more than flavor.

It can affect:

- maintenance;
- spare parts;
- ammunition compatibility;
- training;
- reliability;
- upgrade paths;
- political relationships;
- replacement availability;
- resale value;
- reverse engineering;
- crew familiarity.

A captured enemy starfighter should not magically become identical to a locally manufactured starfighter.

## 8. Faction capability is not faction ownership

The Experience must distinguish:

**Can the faction build it?**

from:

**Does this sub-faction own one?**

from:

**Is one currently operational?**

from:

**Does anyone here know how to use it?**

from:

**Does anyone here have authority to use it?**

This produces the useful state chain:

```
Technology exists
      ↓
Production is possible
      ↓
Production capacity exists
      ↓
Physical asset exists
      ↓
Qualified crew exists
      ↓
Crew is assigned
      ↓
Resources/support exist
      ↓
Maintenance/readiness exists
      ↓
Asset is operational
```

## 9. The matrix should generate scarcity

The most interesting faction differences should often be **bottlenecks**, not missing buttons.

Examples:

- A faction can build fighters but lacks trained pilots.
- A world has excellent tanks but no replacement engines.
- A colony has advanced research but insufficient manufacturing.
- A shipyard can build capital ships but cannot crew them.
- A mining world has enormous resources but poor logistics.
- A revolutionary faction captures sophisticated equipment but cannot reliably maintain it.
- A wealthy merchant world can buy almost anything but cannot manufacture it locally.
- A frontier faction has exceptional combat veterans but obsolete equipment.

This is where the vehicle/building matrix connects directly to the soldier skill system.

## 10. Faction identity should emerge from the whole graph

Do not make faction identity a list of exclusive units.

Instead:

```
Faction
  ↓
Doctrine
  ↓
Research priorities
  ↓
Industrial investments
  ↓
Building distribution
  ↓
Vehicle families
  ↓
Training requirements
  ↓
Skill distribution
  ↓
Crew quality
  ↓
Operational behavior
  ↓
Observed faction identity
```

The player should eventually recognize a faction because of **how it operates**, not because a tooltip says "Kharos unit."

## 11. Sub-faction modifiers

A sub-faction should be able to modify its parent's tendencies.

Candidate modifiers:

- resource abundance;
- resource scarcity;
- industrial maturity;
- population;
- specialist population;
- infrastructure age;
- research investment;
- military investment;
- trade access;
- Imperial patronage;
- isolation;
- local threat;
- historical victories;
- historical defeats;
- captured technology;
- migration;
- cultural specialization;
- leadership;
- accumulated experience.

The resulting capability score should therefore be conceptually:

```
Local Capability
=
Faction Tendency
+ Local Investment
+ Local Resources
+ Local Knowledge
+ Acquired Technology
+ Historical Experience
- Local Constraints
```

The exact formula is intentionally open.

## 12. Consequence

The faction matrix gives us a way to generate a galaxy that is internally explainable without manually authoring every army.

The galaxy can contain:

- common equipment;
- faction signatures;
- regional variants;
- local specialties;
- obsolete equipment;
- experimental prototypes;
- captured equipment;
- improvised equipment;
- rare expert crews.

That is far more interesting than thirteen identical technology trees painted thirteen different colors.

> **The faction defines what is likely. History determines what actually happened.**


## 13. The Dune problem — and why raWWar should not have it

A useful test for this system is the classic science-fiction faction problem: one faction has an ornithopter, another has a trike, another has light infantry, another has a sonic tank, and the differences exist because the game designer needed different pieces for different sides.

That can make a game fun, but it leaves the deeper question unanswered:

> **Why does this society have this thing, while that society does not?**

raWWar should be able to answer that question.

An asymmetric capability should emerge from the same graph that generates the faction itself:

```
History
  ↓
Collective memory
  ↓
Disposition / values
  ↓
Doctrine
  ↓
Economic priorities
  ↓
Research priorities
  ↓
Industrial investment
  ↓
Buildings / infrastructure
  ↓
Technology / production
  ↓
Training / qualifications
  ↓
Vehicles / equipment
  ↓
Operational doctrine
  ↓
Observed behavior
```

Therefore, if two factions possess different vehicles, the difference should ideally be explainable without saying merely **"because faction A gets vehicle A."**

One faction might develop an atmospheric aircraft family because its worlds invested heavily in aerospace engineering, its terrain rewards atmospheric mobility, its doctrine values rapid deployment, its economy can sustain the maintenance burden, and its training institutions produce the required pilots and ground crews.

Another faction might possess the technology but deliberately reject it because its doctrine considers it too maintenance-intensive, its industrial base is optimized elsewhere, its officers distrust the tactical role, its terrain makes the capability less useful, or its culture considers the associated operational doctrine unacceptable.

That distinction is critical:

**"Cannot build it" and "will not build it" are different facts.**

Likewise:

- **Can build** ≠ **has built**
- **Has built** ≠ **deploys widely**
- **Deploys** ≠ **uses well**
- **Uses well** ≠ **believes it should use**
- **Does not use** ≠ **cannot use**

This lets faction asymmetry become a consequence of civilization rather than a designer-assigned roster.

### Capability rejection is itself meaningful behavior

A faction's refusal to adopt a technology should be modeled as meaningful historical and institutional behavior.

A faction may reject a capability because of:

- doctrine;
- cost;
- maintenance burden;
- resource requirements;
- training burden;
- political symbolism;
- cultural values;
- ethical constraints;
- strategic geography;
- vulnerability to enemy countermeasures;
- distrust of the organization that invented it;
- dependence on foreign supply chains;
- institutional inertia;
- previous catastrophic experience;
- deliberate preservation of another way of fighting.

That means a faction can have a **negative preference** for a capability even when the technology is available to it.

The resulting asymmetry can be richer than a conventional technology tree:

```
Technology available
       ↓
Faction evaluates it
       ↓
Disposition + doctrine + economy + history
       ↓
Adopt / adapt / restrict / reject
       ↓
Local production and training
       ↓
Actual deployment
```

The goal is not to eliminate authored creativity. The goal is to make authored creativity **causally believable**.

> **We can still invent the weird shit. We just want the galaxy to be able to explain why it exists.**

### Faction identity extends beyond military hardware

The same causal chain should influence everything the player observes:

| Observable | Should emerge from |
|---|---|
| **Colors / materials** | resources, industry, culture, environment, tradition |
| **Flags / heraldry** | history, legitimacy, identity, political memory |
| **Uniforms** | doctrine, climate, rank structure, manufacturing, tradition |
| **Architecture** | resources, security needs, technology, culture, geography |
| **Rules / laws** | values, history, political structure, threats |
| **Economy** | resources, trade relationships, institutions, priorities |
| **Spycraft** | threat model, paranoia, intelligence institutions, doctrine |
| **Warfare** | geography, doctrine, training, equipment, experience |
| **Research** | problems the faction wants solved and knowledge it values |
| **Logistics** | geography, infrastructure, commerce, military doctrine |
| **Medicine** | value of life, resources, scientific tradition, military needs |
| **Construction** | available materials, engineering tradition, threats, labor |
| **Social behavior** | collective memory, values, incentives, institutions |

The player should therefore be able to walk into a faction's territory and gradually recognize it before seeing a faction name.

**The faction should look, sound, behave, fight, build, trade, research, spy, heal, and organize like the society that produced it.**

That is the standard the capability matrix is intended to support.
